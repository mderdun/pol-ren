\section*{How to use this guide}

\initial{T}{he editions} print each text in its own spelling and leave the
sounds to this guide. An edition’s notes mention only what is peculiar to its
text. Sounds are described by comparison with English, Polish or Italian
words. Syllables are divided with hyphens where the division matters for
underlay, and the stressed syllable is in capitals: po-PROŚ-my.

Each section gives the series default first: what the editions assume and what
their underlay is built on. Historical options follow, with the evidence for
each. Take an option only if the whole choir can keep it, and say so in the
programme. A mixed pronunciation is worse than either.

\section*{Modern Polish}

Polish spelling is regular. A letter or a pair of letters has one sound, with
a few rules of context given below. Every vowel is a full vowel, never
reduced to the neutral vowel of English \textit{about}. Stress falls on the
next-to-last syllable.

\begin{soundtable}
\tablegroup{vowels}
a & as in \textit{father}, short & \pl{matka}\\
e & as in \textit{bed} & \pl{dzień}\\
i & as in \textit{machine}, short & \pl{miły}\\
o & as in British \textit{hot} & \pl{noc}\\
ó, u & as in \textit{food}, but short; the two letters sound the same & \pl{Bóg, używać}\\
y & near the \textit{i} of \textit{bit}, with the tongue drawn back & \pl{był, chytrość}\\
ą & nasal o, as in French \textit{bon} (see below) & \pl{są}\\
ę & nasal e, as in French \textit{vin} (see below) & \pl{męka}\\[0.4em]
\tablegroup{consonants}
c & \textit{ts} as in \textit{cats} & \pl{noc}\\
ch, h & as in Scottish \textit{loch} & \pl{chytrość}\\
cz & \textit{ch} as in \textit{church}, tongue drawn back & \pl{czart}\\
ć; ci before a vowel & a soft \textit{ch}, tongue against the hard palate & \pl{ciemność}\\
dz & \textit{ds} as in \textit{adds} & \pl{dzwon}\\

g & always hard, as in \textit{go} & \pl{Boga}\\
j & \textit{y} as in \textit{yes} & \pl{jesteś}\\
ł & \textit{w} as in \textit{wet} & \pl{był}\\
ń; ni before a vowel & \textit{ny} as in \textit{canyon} & \pl{Panie}\\

\subsection{The silent i} Between a consonant and a vowel, \textit{i} only
softens the consonant. It is not a vowel and never takes a note: \pl{się} is
one syllable, \pl{Panie} two (PA-nie), \pl{miłosierny} four
(mi-ło-SIER-ny). Elsewhere \textit{i} is a vowel. Latin and borrowed words
are different (see the Latin section).

\subsection{Nasal vowels} \pl{Ą} and \pl{ę} are true nasal vowels before
\textit{s, z, sz, ż, ś, ź, f, w, ch} and at the end of a word: the vowel,
then a nasal glide towards \textit{w}. Before \textit{p, b, t, d, k, g} and
the like they become the vowel plus \textit{m}, \textit{n} or \textit{ng}:
\pl{męka} sounds as \textit{menka}, \pl{święty} as \textit{świenty},
\pl{ząb} as \textit{zomp}. Before \textit{l} and \textit{ł} they lose their
nasality. At the end of a word \pl{ę} is usually a plain \textit{e} in speech.
On a long note, hold the plain vowel and let the nasal come only at the end
of the note, as it does in speech.

\subsection{Voicing} A voiced consonant at the end of a word is voiceless:
\pl{Bóg} sounds \textit{buk}. In a cluster the consonants agree with the last
one: \pl{wszytko} sounds \textit{fszytko}, \pl{swej} \textit{sfej},
\pl{przez} \textit{pszes}. A one-letter preposition joins the next word:
\pl{w ciemności} is sung as one word, beginning with \textit{f}.

\subsection{Stress} On the next-to-last syllable: po-PROŚ-my,
u-ży-WA-ją. Short unstressed words (\pl{się, mi, cię, go, nas}) lean on the
word before.

\section*{Polish of the sixteenth century}

\subsection{Default} Modern pronunciation of the old forms as printed. The
editions keep forms such as \pl{zmierzka, naszem, swoję, wszytki, jenż}
because they change rhyme, syllable count or sense. Their sounds changed
gradually and unevenly over two centuries, and singing them in modern
pronunciation is what Polish choirs do. Sing \pl{naszem} with \textit{-em},
\pl{swoję} with a nasal \textit{e}, exactly as written.

The following historical options are attested for the Polish of about 1550.
They are listed from the best attested and easiest to the least certain.

\subsection{Ł as a dark l} In the sixteenth century \textit{ł} was a true
\textit{l}, the dark \textit{l} of English \textit{full}, and not yet the
\textit{w} of modern Polish. The change began around 1600 and became standard
only in the twentieth century; stage speech kept the dark \textit{l} until
then. This is the safest historical option.

\subsection{Rz as a trilled fricative} Written \textit{rz} began as a soft
\textit{r}. By the fourteenth century it had become an \textit{r} trilled
with friction, the sound of Czech \textit{ř} in \textit{Dvořák}. It merged
with \textit{ż} gradually between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries,
earlier in some places than in others. For Kraków around 1550 it is
defensible.

\subsection{Stress over the stress group} Stress already fell on the
next-to-last syllable, but it was counted over the whole group, including a
short word leaning on the one before. \pl{Stało się} was stressed sta-ŁO się,
where modern Polish has STA-ło się. This matters at a cadence where
\pl{się, mi} or \pl{cię} follows a two-syllable word.

\subsection{Nasal vowels} In the sixteenth century \pl{ą} was still closer to
a nasal \textit{a} than a nasal \textit{o}; it reached its modern sound in the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. \pl{Ę} was more open than now, towards
the \textit{a} of English \textit{cat}.

\subsection{Narrowed vowels} The long vowels of Old Polish had become
narrowed vowels (\pl{samogłoski pochylone}) by about 1500: a narrowed
\textit{a} between \textit{a} and \textit{o}; a narrowed \textit{e} between
\textit{e} and \textit{i} or \textit{y}; a narrowed \textit{o} between
\textit{o} and \textit{u}, which became the modern \pl{ó}. Which words had
them is a fact about each word, and the prints are no reliable guide.
Printers usually marked the narrowed \pl{é} and \pl{ó} with an accent, but for
\textit{a} they usually did the opposite, accenting the clear vowel and
leaving the narrowed one plain (Statorius, 1568: \pl{bábá, spáć} against
\pl{pan, sad}). Practice was inconsistent. A choir that wants narrowed vowels
needs a list for the text in hand; the editions do not mark them.

\subsection{Reading the old prints} The diplomatic texts in the critical
editions keep the printers’ spelling. These are the differences that matter:

\begin{soundtable}
y before a vowel, final y & \textit{j} & \pl{Yeſu} = \pl{Jesu}, \pl{pokoy} = \pl{pokój}\\
y, i after p, b, m, w & softness, as modern \textit{i} & \pl{zmyerzka} = \pl{zmierzka}, \pl{ſwyętego} = \pl{świętego}\\
v at the start of a word & \textit{u} & \pl{Vżywáyą} = \pl{używają}\\
ſ & \textit{s} & \pl{ſwey} = \pl{swej}\\
ſſ & \textit{sz} & \pl{náſſym} = \pl{naszym}\\
á & usually the clear \textit{a} & \pl{Bogá} = \pl{Boga}\\
ē, ā & a vowel followed by \textit{m} or \textit{n} & \pl{zduchē} = \pl{z Duchem}\\
words run together & a preposition joined to its noun & \pl{wćyemnośći} = \pl{w ciemności}\\
\end{soundtable}

\section*{Old Polish}

\pl{Bogurodzica} is sung in Woronczak’s normalised text in modern
pronunciation. That is the usual practice, and the edition’s underlay is built
on it. The Polish of about 1400 differed a good deal. Stress fell on the first
syllable and moved to the next-to-last only around 1500. Vowels were long or
short. There was a single nasal vowel, written \pl{ǫ} in the diplomatic text,
which later split into \pl{ą} and \pl{ę}. \textit{Ł} was a dark \textit{l}
and \textit{rz} the Czech-like \textit{ř}. A sung reconstruction could be
built from the historical grammars, but no performing tradition has tested
one, and the editions do not print one.

\pl{Kyrie eleison} is Greek: \textit{y} is \textit{i}, and the refrain has
seven syllables, KY-ri-e e-LE-i-son.

\section*{Latin as sung in Poland}

\subsection{Default} The Polish tradition. Polish choirs sang Latin with
Polish sounds for some letters, and Polish churches and schools kept many of
them. It differs from the Roman pronunciation that most choirs know in about
a dozen spellings. Everything not listed is as in Roman Latin.

\begin{latintable}
c before e, i, y, ae, oe & \textit{ts} & \textit{ch} as in \textit{church} & \la{caelum, principio}\\
sc before e, i & \textit{sts} & \textit{sh} & \la{scio, descendit}\\
ch & \textit{ch} as in \textit{loch} & \textit{k} & \la{Rachel}\\
h & \textit{ch} as in \textit{loch} & silent & \la{Herodis, hodie}\\
g & always hard & soft before e, i & \la{angelum}\\
gn & \textit{g} and \textit{n} both sounded & \textit{ny} & \la{agnus}\\
qu & \textit{kv} & \textit{kw} & \la{qui}\\
ngu before a vowel & \textit{ngv} & \textit{ngw} & \la{sanguis}\\
s between vowels & \textit{z} & \textit{s} or \textit{z} & \la{misit, Iesu}\\
ti before a vowel & \textit{tsi} & \textit{tsi} & \la{gratia}\\
z & \textit{z} & \textit{dz} & \la{zelus}\\
ae, oe & \textit{e} as in \textit{bed} & the same & \la{saecula}\\
y & \textit{i} & the same & \la{Kyrie}\\
\end{latintable}

\subsection{Vowels and syllables} Five pure vowels, none reduced. Unlike
Polish, \textit{i} before a vowel is a syllable of its own: GRA-ti-a,
Ma-RI-a, FI-li-os. The sixteenth-century Polish forms of these words show it:
\pl{gracyja, Maryja}.

\subsection{Ci, si, ti, di, ri before a vowel} These stay hard, as in Italian,
and are not softened as Polish would soften them. The Polish loans of the
period have hard consonants (\pl{lekcyja, pasyja, historyja}). \textit{N}
before \textit{i} is another matter. Polish speakers soften it, and the loans
have a soft \textit{n} (\pl{unija, Antoni}), so it may stay soft.

\subsection{Ti} \textit{tsi} with a full \textit{i}: ex-spe-cta-ti-O-ne. Not
after \textit{s, t, x}: HO-sti-a. Polish schools now often teach a plain
\textit{ti}; the church tradition and the period have \textit{tsi}.

\subsection{S between vowels} \textit{z}: MI-zit, JE-zu. The Polish loans
voice it (\pl{muzyka, filozof, Jezus}), and the series follows them.

\subsection{G before e and i} Hard. Medieval Polish readers said \textit{j}
here, which is why \la{angelus} became \pl{anioł} and \la{evangelium}
\pl{ewanjelija}; the hard \textit{g} is the later learned norm. When it took
over in sung Latin is not known. For fifteenth-century repertory, \textit{j}
is a historical option.

\subsection{Qu} \textit{kv}, which Polish voicing rules turn into \textit{kf}
in quick speech. Either is right.

\subsection{Stress} By the rules of Latin, not of Polish: the next-to-last
syllable if it is long, otherwise the one before. Jerzy Liban’s treatise on
church accentuation (Kraków, c.\,1539) teaches these rules, and Polish kept
Latin stress even in borrowed words (MU-zy-ka). Do not move the stress to the
next-to-last syllable: DO-mi-nus, AN-ge-lum, e-RI-pu-it, FI-li-os, NO-lu-it.
