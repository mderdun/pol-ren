# Mitchell (2025) and Fitch (2020): notes against the underlay rules

Read 4 October 2026 for the Polish Early Music editions. Page numbers for Mitchell are the printed page numbers, which match the PDF page numbers. Fitch page numbers come from the print-page markers embedded in the EPUB. Quotations are kept under 20 words. "Our rules" means `docs/editorial-principles.md` §10 and §1.4.

## 1. Mitchell

**Citation.** Robert J. Mitchell, *Texting in Earlier Fifteenth Century Sacred Polyphony, with particular reference to the repertory of the Trent Codices and related sources* (2025), 183 pp., PDF, © the author. The file does not give a publisher or place. Its PDF metadata dates it to December 2024. Margaret Bent and David Fallows were the draft-readers (p. 1).

**What the work is.** It is not a thesis. It is a practical manual by an experienced editor, written for "those who are relatively new to editing fifteenth century music" (p. 1). It draws on his 1989 Exeter PhD (p. 11 n. 20) and on his online DIAMM editions of Trent 89 and 91 (D89, D91; p. 2 n. 5). He calls his method empirical and makes "no claims to rectitude or authority" (p. 1). He sets theory aside on purpose, because he values the empirical over the speculative (pp. 177–8).

**Period and corpus.** Sacred polyphony c. 1400–1460, especially Trent 87–93, together with Q15, Ao, Old Hall, ModB, Turin J.II.9, Zwettl, Strahov, SP B80 and others (sigla pp. 178–82). It is not a sampled corpus. There are 87 numbered music examples and 21 plates, chosen from his own editions. He does not count pieces systematically.

**Method.** He sets out rules from the theorists: two Italian treatises of c. 1420–50 published by Harrán, Zarlino 1558, and Stocker c. 1570 via Lowinsky (pp. 5–9). He then gives 14 "general principles" (§2c, pp. 10–42), a typology of textures (§3) and four worked examples, with step-by-step decisions numbered (§4, pp. 51–77). Later sections cover the order of copying (§5), texture types (§§6–11), performance (§12), a literature review (§13) and conclusions (§14). He tests his decisions by singing and listening. For example, he recorded himself on all three parts on a multitrack machine (p. 125).

**Quantitative findings.** There are no statistics on texting practice. Everything countable in the book is below:
- Turin J.II.9 holds 41 motets of c. 1410–20 (p. 43).
- Table 1 shows voice-function exchanges in the Agnus of the 89 equal-voice Mass, bar by bar (p. 51).
- He made "17 or more" underlay decisions in that Agnus and calls two of them less than definite (p. 57).
- His texting differs from the source in a few places. In *Beata viscera* the differences are 5 in the Tenor, 4 in the Superius and 5 in the Contra (pp. 58–60). In the Strahov *Surge propera* the differences are listed at 7 places, plus an added "Alleluia" (p. 94).
- In *Missa Quand ce viendra*, the Gloria's first section runs "over sixty measures long". It has 5 upper-Contra text cues and 1 lower-Contra cue, 1 upper-Contra repeated pitch and 4 in the lower Contra. The Credo's mm. 31–139 have 5 upper-Contra cues (p. 111).
- He lists eleven two-voice instances of pseudo-imitation. "The greater part" come from works of the German-speaking world (p. 138).
- He gives page sizes for six performance-sized manuscripts (p. 160).
- **King 1999** (reported pp. 172–3): 15 ModB thematic-index incipits place text differently from the main copy. In 7 of them the index reproduces the main copy's texting and adds syllables. In 5 there are further departures. In the 12 together the scribe copies from his understanding of the exemplar.
- **Munn 1991** (reported pp. 170–1): for each voice of the *Caput* Kyrie, Munn marked which notes could take a syllable under the theorists' rules and computed percentages. Mitchell reports only that "quite a lot of notes remain eligible". No figures are given.
- **Igoe 1971** (p. 168): Igoe "statistically calculates the permutations" of text or no text in three-voice pieces. No figures are given.

**Conclusions on underlay practice.**
- There was "little or no standardisation" (p. 1). No universal standards existed, and features found in one body of work fail when applied to slightly older or newer music (p. 177).
- Syllables generally prefer the strong beat, especially in supporting voices. Four exceptions apply: chant cantus firmus, chant paraphrase, vocalised preludes and postludes, and anacrusic two-note entries at the same pitch (p. 10). Strong stresses are best found empirically, through performance (p. 11).
- Same-pitch repeated notes sung to one syllable are genuine. This applies at the starts of phrases and in cadential clichés, and editors should tolerate them rather than airbrush them out (pp. 14–22, especially p. 21).
- Many lower-voice ligatures are scribal and do not carry syllables (p. 39). He would break ligatures to keep imitation (p. 39). Simultaneous ligatures in different voices are probably functional (p. 39).
- Lower voices were often vocalised, or given incipits only (§3, pp. 42–50; §12).
- Editors should not multiply text repeats over melismas. He pleads to keep the "superbly melismatic style" (p. 29).
- After c. 1450, copying music before text removes visible word–music links (p. 85). Neat sources are not always authoritative (p. 78).

**Recommendations to editors.**
- Split notes sparingly and keep the splits simple, not "too artful" (p. 35).
- Underlay imitation identically where possible (pp. 39, 57, 66–7). Occasionally leave imitation untexted (p. 140b), or text it off the beat to make it audible (p. 140c).
- Treat identical clichés consistently (pp. 17, 178).
- Expect lower voices to catch up with the text and to sing disjunct text (pp. 116, 125, 129).
- Text cantus-firmus tenors last (p. 160).
- Use another source if the chosen one is poor (p. 42).
- Expect to revise: he made three or four attempts on some pieces (p. 41).
- He prefers approximate guidelines to "some primary-source textual detail" (p. 125).

## 2. Fitch

**Citation.** Fabrice Fitch, *Renaissance Polyphony*, Cambridge Introductions to Music (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2020).

The book has no dedicated treatment of underlay. The relevant passages are listed below.

- **Ch. 7, "Genre, texts, forms" → "The genre problem" (pp. 96–102).**
  - A composer's first decision was to respond to the form of the text (p. 98).
  - Latin contrafacta of French chansons were "especially common in German and central-European manuscript sources" (p. 98). Renaissance sources show no bias against contrafacta (p. 98).
  - Tone shaped style. Syllabic chordal declamation went with light texts, and courtly topics called for melismatic writing (p. 100).
- **Ch. 7 → "Words and music" (pp. 102–107).**
  - Around the time of the *Missa Caput*, copyists switched from words-first to music-first copying. As a result, alignment can look haphazard, which misleadingly suggests that coordination was secondary (p. 102).
  - Stress on strong beats and long notes, word-painting and intelligibility are assumptions "from the Baroque onwards". He calls this cluster "text sensitivity" and finds it problematic (pp. 102–3).
  - Such habits "had begun to take root" by the end of the period (p. 103).
  - Mid-16th-century religious and secular thinkers held that music should make the text intelligible (p. 104).
  - Gombert's "approach to scansion can be described as inconsistent", yet he was praised as modern (p. 104).
  - Recent research questions how far humanist ideals reached (pp. 104–5).
  - Composers felt free to omit Credo phrases (p. 105).
  - Text setting was "subject to competing priorities", and he rejects a teleological account that runs from abstract to sensitive setting (p. 106).
  - Notes 7, 8 and 11 cite Edwards, "Understanding Fifteenth-Century Chansonniers"; *The Motet around 1500*; and Edwards, "Text Treatment in Motets around 1500".
- **Ch. 6, Ex. 6.2 (p. 89).** In *Confort d'amours* the "opening melisma carries no text".
- **Ch. 9, *Si dedero* (pp. 120–1).** The sources give only the chant words, and matching them to the music is "far from straightforward". It has been said that Agricola set "not the words … but the chant" (n. 4, Edwards, "Intuitive Syllable Deployment"). Even so, the words remain "integral to the musical design".
- **Ch. 9, de Silva *Intonuit* (p. 129).** The points of imitation pair voices "thanks to which the text is always audible".
- **Ch. 10 (pp. 132–3).** *Je requier* reserves melismas for phrase endings; in *Ma bouche rit* phrase–text mapping "is unclear".
- **Ch. 11, "Varietas" and "Pervasive imitation" (pp. 151–2, 159; 163).**
  - Points of imitation mark off the beginnings of text lines (p. 152).
  - In Gombert, a new subject typically corresponds to a new text phrase (p. 159).
  - Gallus's *Moralia* (Prague, 1596) has "speech-like declamation throughout", like the French *vers mesurés* (p. 163).
  - In Ex. 11.7 a single voice is displaced in chordal writing, which keeps "the sense of forward motion" (p. 160).
- **Ch. 14, "Performance practice" (pp. 223–35).**
  - Editions differ by source and by editorial choice. On ficta, a single "correct" solution misreads practice. The distinction between emic (insider) and etic (outsider) views applies (pp. 223–4).
  - Texting "varies greatly between sources". Often one voice carries the full text and the others carry incipits, and texting is fuller in sacred sources (pp. 227–8).
  - Discantus underlay is "usually straightforward", while fitting the contratenor and sometimes the tenor is hard. Vocalisation on a neutral vowel avoids the problem (p. 230).
  - Latin was once pronounced following the local language ("period pronunciation", p. 234).

## 3. Against our rules (§10.1–10.15)

- **10.1 (new syllable only on a minim or longer).**
  - **Supported:** Stocker's modern rule 1, under which minims and larger notes all take a syllable (Mitchell p. 9), and Zarlino rules 3–6 (p. 6).
  - **Qualified:**
    - Stocker's older optional rule 2: an isolated semiminim "often receives a syllable", and then so does the following note (p. 8).
    - Zarlino 4 and Stocker's modern rule 3: the note *immediately after* semiminims takes no new syllable (pp. 6, 9). Our rule does not state this.
    - The c. 1440 Italian treatise puts syllables only on tactus pulses (rule 6, p. 5). This is earlier than our period.
- **10.2 (syllable on the first note after a rest).** Supported by Zarlino 7 and Stocker 5 (pp. 6–7). Mitchell says he will describe exceptions to Zarlino 7 (p. 6). I found none labelled as such.
- **10.3 (last syllable on the cadence note).**
  - **Supported:** Zarlino 10 (p. 6) and King's scribal habit 2, which says "usually" (p. 173).
  - **Qualified:** final syllables can sit on ligated penultimates, and before melismatic postludes (§2c vi, p. 17). In a [Dufay?] Gradual the final syllables come "well before" the final notes (p. 147). All of this is 15th-century practice.
- **10.4 (no rest divides a word).**
  - **Supported:** King's habit 3 says rests of a semibreve or longer generally do not interrupt a word (p. 173).
  - **Qualified:**
    - Bent: "words can be interrupted by rests" (Mitchell p. 175).
    - Part-word lower-voice entries occur in English works (p. 129 n. 205; p. 57, '-lis').
- **10.5 (elision).** Not directly addressed.
  - Mitchell records varying syllable counts in 15th-century Latin: *huic* as one syllable, *e-tiam*, a Gallic *re-mis-sio-nem*, and *e-ley-son* against *e-le-y-son* (p. 42).
  - King finds possible elisions in the ModB and Q15 copies of Dufay's hymns, "as a Francophone or Italian speaker might" (p. 173).
  - This qualifies "none in Latin" as a description of practice, though not as an editorial policy for our repertory.
- **10.6 (words and music together).**
  - **Supported:**
    - Find strong stresses through performance (Mitchell p. 11).
    - Voices need not move together: catch-up and disjunct texting (pp. 116, 125, 129).
    - "One phrase will usually cater for each word" (p. 57).
  - **Qualified:**
    - Fitch treats stress-on-strong-beat as an assumption from the Baroque onwards (pp. 102–3), though one "beginning to take root" by the end of the period (p. 103). Intelligibility was the mid-century ideal (p. 104). For 1550–1611 this supports us more than it warns.
    - Mitchell warns that "modern aesthetics" creep into aural testing (p. 125).
    - Meconi doubts that humanist declamation is "somehow better or more musical" (pp. 171–2).
    - Mitchell finds that ModB, Q15 and Verona 759 texting is generally reliable (pp. 51, 116).
- **10.7 (Wacław's practice).** The melisma on the stressed penultimate is supported by Zarlino 9 (a penultimate melisma only on a long syllable, p. 6) and Stocker's optional rule 1 (an accented penultimate or antepenultimate may take semiminims, p. 8). In English motets the penultimate syllable is "an occasion for extended melismatic writing" (p. 129).
- **10.8 (penultimate accent rule).**
  - **Qualified:** "imposing modern Latin accentuation criteria" on a whole work's texting is likely anachronistic (p. 42 n. 97). Bent finds that received Latin stresses "do not always apply" (p. 175).
  - Munn gives a history of Latin accent by period (p. 170).
  - Fitch notes that Latin pronunciation followed the local language (p. 234).
  - All of this concerns the 15th century. Our Kraków source (Sebastian z Felsztyna) is local and contemporary, which is the right kind of evidence.
- **10.9 (repeated notes take a new syllable).**
  - **Supported:** Stocker's obligatory rule 4 and its exception for semiminims, which match ours (pp. 7–8).
  - **Qualified:** Mitchell defends one syllable over same-pitch repeated notes in clichés and at phrase starts (pp. 14–22). Repeated altus notes at cadences "should usually not be taken" as calling for repeated text (p. 22).
  - On the clause "long free melismas may be replaced by repeating the clause":
    - Stocker favours repetition near the end "to prevent the singing of a long melisma on an accented syllable" (p. 9), and also says frequent repetition breeds aversion.
    - Mitchell resists editorial repeats in 15th-century music (p. 29).
    - Villard finds "little justification" for word repetition (p. 166).
- **10.10 (imitation carries its head-motif's words).**
  - **Supported:**
    - Mitchell preserves imitation and disregards ligatures (p. 39), and matches underlay at imitative entries (pp. 57, 66–7).
    - In Fitch, points of imitation mark text lines (p. 152), and in Gombert a new subject means a new text phrase (p. 159).
  - **Qualified:**
    - Some imitation is best left untexted (p. 140b).
    - Not all imitative passages can take identical texting (p. 136).
    - Bent: "not all imitative or pseudo-imitative answers" need syllabic exactitude (p. 175).
- **10.11 (block-texted sources: use the spacing).**
  - **Qualified:**
    - Text blocks in Chantilly take "little account" of melodic sequences (Upton, p. 78).
    - The reliability of spacing depends on whether text or music was copied first (pp. 77–85; Fitch p. 102).
    - The ModB scribe placed text by his understanding of the exemplar (King, p. 173).
  - Our rule's second half (prefer the voice whose text fits note for note) matches Mitchell's practice of choosing the clearest voice or source (pp. 42, 51).
- **10.12 (keep source departures from the theorists).**
  - **Contrasted:** Mitchell's own stance is to let guidelines override source detail (p. 125), and he often moves syllables (pp. 51–6). His sources are 15th-century copies made music-first. Ours (1553 print, 1611 print) are a different case, so §1.4 still stands.
  - **Supported:** "music theory has followed practice rather than establishing it" (p. 178).
- **10.13 (lower voices may skip words; divide notes only where text requires).**
  - **Supported:**
    - Omitting phrases in long-note tenors (pp. 33 n. 76, 124).
    - Split notes kept simple (p. 35).
    - Credo phrases omitted in practice (Fitch p. 105).
  - **Further option, not in our rule:** vocalising lower voices, or texting them with incipits only (Mitchell pp. 42–50; Fitch p. 230).
- **10.14 (strophic songs).** Marginal. ModB underlays several hymn verses in the Superius (pp. 49–50). Lower-voice singers were unlikely to know every verse (p. 49).
- **10.15 (theorists cited by paraphrase).** No bearing.

## 4. The editor's tendencies (a)–(d)

**(a) Light words off the beat inside moving figures.**
- Stocker, in Mitchell's reading, discourages melismas on *et* or *in*, and on combinations such as *et in* and *qui pro* (p. 8). This fits light words not being sustained, but it says nothing about off-beat placement.
- Stocker's commentary on dotted rhythms says they reflect correct stress, as in *Dominus* with a short second syllable (p. 7).
- **Partly against:**
  - The c. 1440 treatise allows syllables only on tactus pulses (p. 5).
  - Mitchell prefers syllables on strong beats (p. 10), and treats weak-beat syllables as "unavoidable" exceptions (pp. 117, 124).
  - Zarlino 4 and Stocker's modern rule 3 forbid a new syllable on the note *immediately after* semiminims (pp. 6, 9). A light word sung on semiminims therefore cannot hand on to a new syllable on the next note under those rules. On minims it can.
- **Support from Mitchell's exceptions:** anacrusic entries on repeated pitches (pp. 10, 14–15), and off-beat texting needed to make imitation audible (p. 140c).
- **Not found:** no statement that light words lead into a stressed or open syllable on the beat, nor about long notes allowing them on the beat.

**(b) Melismas favour open or bright vowels.** **Not found** in either book. The nearest material:
- Villard notes repeated vowels written out for melismas in P 568 and ModA (*P a a a a tris*; Mitchell p. 166).
- Fitch mentions vocalisation "on a neutral vowel" (p. 230), Gothic Voices' "identically formed vowels" (p. 233) and period pronunciation (p. 234).
- Neither book relates vowel quality to melisma placement.

**(c) Off-beat syllables propel the line into open sounds on the beat.** **Not found** as a claim.
- Tangential: Fitch's single displaced voice that keeps "forward motion" (p. 160) concerns rhythm, not syllables.
- Mitchell's anacrusic same-pitch entries (pp. 14–16) are the closest practical analogue. He presents them as an English and triple-time feature that cuts against modern taste (p. 15).

**(d) Long melismas need not sit on the stressed syllable.**
- **Support (15th century):**
  - Long melismas can fall on the last syllable of *Kyrie* or the first of *eleyson* (pp. 15–16).
  - In the [Dufay?] Gradual a final '-la' is "very extended" (p. 147).
  - Leonel's melisma on *mihi* is "odd but" it happens (p. 117).
  - Melismas fall where the syllables run out: singers used "one per measure" and then a melisma before the cadence (p. 56).
  - Mitchell argues against editorial repeats that break up melismas (p. 29).
  - Fitch: *Confort d'amours* opens with a melisma carrying no text (p. 89). Agricola set "the chant" rather than the words (p. 121). Courtly texts invited melisma (p. 100).
- **Against:**
  - Zarlino 9: a penultimate melisma on a short syllable produces "a barbarism" (p. 6).
  - Stocker prefers repeating text to a long melisma on an accented syllable (p. 9).
  - Fitch's mid-century ideals of intelligibility and minimal melisma (pp. 104, 163).

**Testing (a) and (b) on Wacław's 1553 *Lamentationes*.** Mitchell has no data. Two methods he reports can be adapted:
1. **Munn's eligibility method** (pp. 170–1). For every note in each voice, record whether the theorists' rules would allow a syllable on it. Then compute the share of eligible positions where the print actually places a syllable. This separates choice from constraint.
2. **King's comparison of index and main copy** (p. 173). Compare the underlay of identical motifs in different voices, or in different copies, to estimate how much of the placement is the printer's.

**Coding.** Code each syllable for:
- word class (function word: *et, in, de, ad, a/ab, ex, cum, per, non, qui*; content word);
- stress (penultimate rule, §10.8);
- the vowel nucleus (*a e o* against *i u*; *ae/oe* separately);
- note value;
- metrical position (on or off the tactus);
- number of notes on the syllable;
- distance to the next cadence.

**Test for (a).** Compare the proportion of function words that sit off the beat with the proportion of other syllables off the beat, split by note value: semiminim figures, minims, longer notes. Also record the class of the next syllable.

**Test for (b).** Compare the vowel distribution of melismatic syllables (say three or more notes) with the vowel distribution of all syllables in the text, using a chi-square test. Control for stress and for the pre-cadential melisma of §10.7, which would otherwise confound the result, since penultimates of cadence words dominate melismas.

**Caveat (Mitchell pp. 78, 85, 173).** A source's underlay records the scribe's or printer's reading. Treat the results as evidence about the 1553 print, as §1.3 already requires.

## 5. Relevance to Central European and Polish practice, 1550–1611

- **Mitchell's period is c. 1400–60.** His theorists' section brings in Zarlino and Stocker (pp. 6–9) but sets aside Lanfranco and Vicentino (p. 6 n. 16), the two authorities behind our 10.1.
- **Polish and Central European sources in Mitchell:**
  - **Glogau partbooks**, now Kraków BJ mus. 40098: pieces concordant with Trent 91 "tend to be fully texted", as is much of Glogau's sacred repertory (p. 146 n. 257; sigla p. 180).
  - **Kras**, Warsaw BN III.8054: one of the sources whose guide-lines clarify texting (Bent, reported p. 174; sigla p. 180).
  - **Neumarkt Cantionale**, Wrocław (pp. 94, 105).
  - **Strahov**, Central European and often badly copied (p. 93).
  - German-speaking origins for most of the pseudo-imitation instances (p. 138) and for German-variant Magnificat tones (p. 155).
- **Fitch:**
  - Central European sources are rich in Latin contrafacta (p. 98).
  - The Speciálník omits the Agnus (p. 115).
  - Gallus's *Moralia* (Prague, 1596) uses speech-like declamation (p. 163). This is the nearest Central European parallel to Zieleński's time.
  - Latin was pronounced locally (p. 234). This supports the series default of Polish Latin.
- **Untexted and block-texted sources:** Mitchell §3 (pp. 42–50), §5 (pp. 77–93) and §12 give a framework for deciding whether lower voices need text and how far to trust spacing. This is relevant to *Zmierzka* (§10.11) and to editorially texted lower voices (§10.13). Mitchell's own default is "the maximum amount of editorial text that is practically possible" (p. 129).

## 6. Leads

All of these are cited by Mitchell or Fitch.

- **Harrán, *Word-Tone Relations in Musical Thought* (MSD 40, 1986)**, appendix pp. 360–489 (Mitchell p. 5 n. 14). The original texts and translations of nearly all the underlay theory. This is the source for checking 10.1 and 10.15.
- **Harrán, "New light … prior to Zarlino" (ActaM 45, 1973) and "Vicentino and his rules of text underlay" (MQ 59, 1973)** (p. 6 n. 16). These are the bases for 10.1 and 10.5.
- **Harrán, "How to 'Lay' the 'Lay'" (MD 51, 1997)** (p. 165 n. 295). Covers the 16th century, which Mitchell omits for that reason, so it should be relevant to us.
- **Lowinsky 1961 on Stocker** (p. 6 n. 15). Stocker's optional rules bear on (a) and (d). Note Mitchell's correction of Lowinsky's p. 237 (p. 9 n. 18).
- **Lewis, "Zarlino's Theories of Text Underlay as Illustrated in His Motet Book of 1549", *Notes* (1949)** (p. 6 n. 16). Tests theory against a printed motet book of our period. This is a methodological model for the 1553 print.
- **Munn, PhD, Oklahoma 1991**, pp. 316–442 (p. 170). The only counting method in either book.
- **King, "Texting Practices…" (JRMA 124, 1999); DPhil, Oxford 1996** (p. 172). Scribal habits counted from the ModB index.
- **Schmidt-Beste, *Textdeklamation in der Motette des 15. Jahrhunderts* (Brepols, 2003)** (Mitchell p. 176 n. 311). Likely the most systematic study of declamation.
- **Edwards, "Text Treatment in Motets around 1500", in *The Motet around 1500*, ed. Schmidt-Beste (2012)**, and **"Alexander Agricola and Intuitive Syllable Deployment" (EM 2006)** (Fitch ch. 7 n. 11; ch. 9 n. 4).
- **Bent, "Text Setting in Sacred Music of the Early 15th Century"** (repr. 2002) (pp. 38 n. 88, 174).
- Also: Meconi 1992 (p. 171); Ferrari 2023 (p. 176); Fallows, "A word about ligatures" (2013) (p. 41 n. 94).

Neither book cites any phonetic or vowel-based study of underlay. Tendency (b) has no lead here.
