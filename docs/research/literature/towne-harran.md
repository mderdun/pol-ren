# Towne and Harrán on text underlay: reading notes

Five articles, read in full. Page numbers are the journals' own. "Th." marks a period theorist's statement, "Auth." the modern author's conclusion, and "Mus." evidence from music. Abbreviations: Lf = Lanfranco, *Scintille di musica* (Brescia 1533); V = Vicentino, *L'antica musica* (Rome 1555); Z = Zarlino, *Istitutioni harmoniche* (Venice 1558), IV.33; S = Stoquerus (Stocker), *De musica verbali*, MS c.1570. "Older" and "modern" are Stocker's two generations: Josquin's, and Willaert's from *Musica nova* (1540s) on.

---

## 1. Per article

### A. Harrán 1973a
Don Harrán, "New Light on the Question of Text Underlay Prior to Zarlino," *Acta Musicologica* 45 (1973): 24–56.

**Argument.** Zarlino's ten rules (1558) are largely a paraphrase, without acknowledgement, of Lanfranco's (1533). Lanfranco wrote for the choirboys of Brescia Cathedral, about Masses and motets only, and drew his rules from Willaert's music (26–30, 51–53). Zarlino addresses composers. He drops Lanfranco's exceptions and adds the quantity ("barbarism") rule (47–50). Exceptions disappear between Lanfranco and Zarlino because style grew more uniform. The rules therefore probably hold, broadly, for the earlier sixteenth century too (53–56).

**Rules (Th.).**
- Lf I: cadences should follow the meaning and division of the words, though in polyphony words are grouped "according to counterpoint and the necessity of rests" (32–33). Z, end of IV.32: do not separate a clause with rests, and use no rest longer than a minim before the sense is complete (32–33).
- Lf II = Z I: every separate note, except "almost always" the semiminim, carries its own syllable (35–36). Z adds that a ligated note is not separate (36).
- Lf III = Z II: one syllable per ligature, at its start (38–39); Agricola (Wittenberg 1532) agrees (39). Lf IV = Z III: none on the dot (39).
- Lf V: "on rare occasions" a syllable goes on the semiminim after a dotted minim, and then on the following white note too (40). Z IV: rarely on any semiminim or smaller note, or on the note that follows it. Z V: no syllable on notes after a dot that are smaller than the dot (a semiminim after a dotted semibreve; a fusa after a dotted minim) (40–41). Z VI: if a semiminim takes a syllable, the next note may take one too (41). Auth.: so Z permits a syllable on a note equal to the dot (a semiminim after a dotted minim), and such cases were more frequent in practice than "rare" (41).
- Lf VI: the first semiminim of a series carries its syllable. By good custom the middle and last ones do not, nor does the white note after the series. The exception is pieces imitating French chansons (41–42). Stocker's modern optional rules 2–3 repeat this, and his older option 4 allows two-note groups within a series (42).
- Lf VII = Z VIII: repeat words only when there are enough notes. Z adds: repeat only complete sense units, and sparingly (43–44).
- Lf VIII: extra notes go to the penultimate syllable and the last syllable goes to the cadence note or "the last singable note" (44–46). Z IX: only if the penultimate is long (accented); a melisma on a short syllable is a barbarism (44–45). Auth.: Strunk's translation of this passage misleads (45). Z X: last syllable on last note (45). Lanfranco's "last singable note" anticipates Stocker: after a run of semiminims, the final syllable goes on the first note of the run (46).
- Z VII: a new syllable on the first note and the first note after a rest (48).
- Z I (beginning): suitable note values for long and short syllables, so that "no barbarism" is heard (48–49). Earlier statements: Gaffurius (*Practica musicae* 1496, II.1), Vanneo (1533). Coclico (1552) reproaches Netherlandish musicians for ignoring quantity (49–50).
- Z, implied before the rules: underlay suits each part; one voice's elision need not be copied in another. Auth.: imitation need not be texted identically (50–51).
- Lf: these rules are for Masses and motets, "for I speak neither of French chansons nor of madrigals" (44, 51).

**Mus.** None counted; Willaert named as the model (30, 53).

### B. Harrán 1973b
Don Harrán, "Vicentino and His Rules of Text Underlay," *Musical Quarterly* 59 (1973): 620–632.

**Argument.** Vicentino's chapters IV.29–30 contain twelve rules. Four match Lanfranco, one matches Zarlino and seven are his own (621). Harrán tests them against Vicentino's first book of five-voice madrigals (1546, examples in halved values) and finds frequent breaches (622–630). He argues the rules reflect Willaert's teaching of the 1530s and 1540s (631–632).

**Rules (Th., V 1555).**
1. After semiminims or fusae, the new syllable goes not on the first white note but on the second; breaking this is a "barbarism" (622).
2. Near rests or at a phrase end, two minims after semiminims may each take a syllable (622–623). Mus.: absent from Vicentino's own madrigals (623).
3. The semiminim after a dotted minim may take a syllable: "non sarà gran fallo" (it will be no great fault) (623).
4. Do not repeat words unless written. Meaningless repeats are faults (623).
5. Long and short syllables need suitable notes, in every language: French accents, Latin usage, Tuscan pronunciation (624).
6–7. In the vernacular, two vowels that meet are elided, except across a line end. Where two lines run on without a rest, both vowels are kept (624–625). Mus.: often broken in his madrigals (626).
8. No elision in Latin (626).
9. No elision across a rest (626).
10. An octave leap should not carry two syllables of the same word. Begin the word on the upper note (627). Auth.: Lowinsky and Kaufmann misread *dittione* as "text" (627 n.15). Mus.: mostly confirmed, sometimes broken (627–628).
11. **Vowels for melismas by register: low voices a, o, u; middle a, e, o; high a, e, i.** The i is hard low and the u hard high (628). Auth.: the reasoning is acoustic, about ease and purity of the vowel (628).
12. No syllable on a dissonant note, except a fourth, "especially when it is syncopated" (628–629). Mus.: seldom confirmed in his madrigals (629).

Also (Th.): in chant, many notes on one vowel ("a-a-a-a") "moves listeners more to laughter than to piety" (V IV.18, fol. 80v). Auth.: Vicentino uses melismas sparingly and puts clarity of text first (624). Th.: Aron (*Toscanello*, I.37) says a syncopated semibreve or minim after a breve rest is reproved "for the difficulty of pronunciation" (630). Auth.: Stocker says "modern composers give syllables to all minims". Mus.: Vicentino's 1572 book gives syllables to all semiminims (630).

### C. Towne 1990 (Part I)
Gary Towne, "A Systematic Formulation of Sixteenth-Century Text Underlay Rules (Part I of II)," *Musica Disciplina* 44 (1990): 255–287.

**Argument.** Towne collates six theorists (Leno c.1440, Lanfranco, Vicentino, Zarlino, Stocker, Luchini c.1590), with Calvisius, Burmeister and Cerone via Harrán's *Word-Tone Relations*. He groups the rules in the order a singer or editor meets them (Tables I–V, Rules 1–35). The scope is limited to Latin sacred music (262). Luchini is derivative and twice misreads Vicentino (271, 275–277).

**Rules and conclusions** (Th. unless marked Auth.):
- R4: repeat only if the notes allow. Older music may repeat single words; modern music only coherent phrases. A full repeat is better than an extended melisma (S) (265–267).
- R9: first note, and first note after a rest, takes a syllable (Z 7) (267). R10: last note takes the last syllable. After a ligature or a semiminim run, the first note of either takes it instead (Lf, S) (268).
- R11: a syllable on each minim or longer note, not on semiminims. A lone semiminim generally takes one, and then so does the next note. **Semiminims may carry syllables in C** (S) (268). Auth.: under C, the minim rules apply one level down; under proportio tripla, one level up (270).
- R13: repeated notes take separate syllables. If the first is a semiminim it may continue the previous syllable (S ch. 15) (269).
- R15: the melisma goes on the penultimate (Lf); only if accented (Z); on the accented penultimate or antepenultimate (S). Calvisius, *Melopoeia* (Erfurt 1592): "accented syllables are appropriate for melismas, unaccented ones are not" (271). Auth.: Lanfranco's plain penultimate is permissible in older works, but Stocker's version is better for both (273–274).
- R16: a semiminim run takes one syllable, starting on its first note or on the longer note before it. The first semiminim may begin a syllable only if it is accented relative to the next (S ch. 24). In modern works the note after the run keeps the same syllable. But it "may require its own syllable" (V), especially as penultimate (Calvisius) (271–272).
- R17: one syllable on two minims or semiminims if the first is accented, mostly in older works (272). Auth.: Stocker's accent-based two-note grouping is "one of his most important contributions" (274).
- R18: individual semiminims may take syllables if all voices move in semiminims, or if the run contains leaps (S ch. 27, Calvisius) (272).
- R23–24: Leno: syllables on adjacent or more distant beats. Stocker: a new syllable on the first of two equal notes if it is "a semiminim on the beat or a minim at the beginning of a tactus" (278). Auth.: "new syllables should occur on relatively-accented notes" (280).
- R25: older composers put no syllable on the syncopated note of a cadential clausula; moderns treat the notes equally (S ch. 23) (278). Calvisius's exception is "suspect" as late style (281 n.38).
- R26: underlay suits each part (Z). Auth.: editors may allow imperfect imitation and occasional asynchrony, but "must be well justified" (281).
- R27: no syllable on a dissonance (V). Auth.: impractical, and broken in his own works (279, 281–282).
- R28: long and short notes for long and short syllables. Burmeister, *Musica poetica* (1606): **smaller notes may take long or short syllables, "since their speed obscures the accent"** (282).
- R29: **accented syllables on accented beats, short syllables on lesser beats** (V ch. 29). Stocker: "lesser beats should receive no syllable" (S ch. 23) (282). Auth.: rigid observance "would conflict violently" with the rules for dotted figures and melismas, so this is "more an ideal principle" (285). Syllables "may not begin on a relatively unaccented rhythmic division, like a semibreve off the beat" (285 n.44).
- R30: individual word accents may be overlooked, but not the rhetorical accent of the sentence (Z, *Sopplimenti* 1588, VII.13; S ch. 20) (282–283).
- R31–33: elide in Italian, but not between lines or across rests; Zarlino lets musical reasons dissolve an elision. No elision in Latin. Cerone (1613): ae, oe, au, eu, ei and consonantal i/u count as single syllables (283). Auth.: the Latin ban does not cover Greek (Kyrie) (286).
- R34: **Vicentino's register-vowel rule.** Finck and Bovicelli "have disparate opinions" (via Harrán, WTR 253–254). Singers sometimes substitute vowels (V); Zarlino (III.45) calls substitution incorrect; repeating vowels is ugly (V IV.18) (283–284).
- Auth.: accent prescriptions come after Lanfranco, so "poor accentuation may be an acceptable result of counterpoint in an OLDER piece" (285). Only Vicentino and Luchini treat vowel colour (286).

**Mus.** No statistics in Part I. Note 29 (269): text is enunciated meaningfully at minim and semiminim speed; "longs and breves move too slowly" (Auth.).

### D. Towne 1991 (Part II)
Gary Towne, "A Systematic Formulation of Sixteenth-Century Text Underlay Rules: Part II," *Musica Disciplina* 45 (1991): 143–168.

**Argument.** Towne adds modern scholars' principles (Table VI, 36–55) and condenses everything into a 21-rule editor's digest for Latin sacred music (Table VII, 152–156). He tests the digest on seven passages from two Masses by Gaspar de Albertis: the autograph *Missa de Sancto Roccho* (1524) and the *Missa Queramus cum pastoribus* (print, 1549) (157–166). Many passages have several defensible solutions (151, 166).

**Principles (Auth. or modern unless marked).**
- 40, 42: no syllable on the dissonant second semibreve of a cadence. A syllable on the note after a semiminim run is better than one on the syncopated cadential note (Lowinsky) (145).
- 43–46, from the sources: by the 1520s a word is copied roughly where it begins, and the rest is left to the singer. A repeated last syllable marks where a word ends. Idem signs leave the extent of a repeat unspecified. Single words spaced under successive phrases mean repetition. A word divided across a rest may mean repetition. Diphthongs and elision follow the space in the source (146, 149–150).
- 48–49: a dot at a staff's end may be written as a note on the next staff. Dividing notes, excising text or supplying text are last resorts that presume scribal error (147, 150).
- 50: text repetition on long melismas after imitative points, "cautiously". Stocker's preference may be "late sixteenth-century taste" imposed on an earlier aesthetic. Josquin's generation "may have preferred to hear these as the long melismatic sections they appear to be" (147, 150–151).
- 51: slight infractions of accent were condoned for "a clear and comfortable delivery" (Bellermann) (148, 151).
- 52–53: similar figures suggest similar underlay. Homophony suggests simultaneous text; counterpoint allows divergence. Both are subject to Zarlino's "parts are unique", especially the Altus (148, 151).
- 54: **voices modelled on chant or other pre-existent melodies follow the model's underlay** (148, 151).
- Table VII 19e: a dotted figure plus a fourth minim may take one syllable "in order to place the ensuing syllable on a strong beat" (156).
- Table VII 21: if there is "leisure", adjust melismas by meaning and vowel (Vicentino's scheme) (156).

**Mus. (Albertis).** Kyrie, S. Rocco: the three-note melisma falls on Kyrie's secondary accent. The rhetorical accent of *eleyson* lands on the syncopated clausula at a high point (159). The autograph divides *e-y*, so the two-syllable *ei* is preferred where space allows (159). Christe, Queramus: elision and a one-syllable *ei* ("Chris-t'e-ley-son") give the clearest imitation. Auth.: this "violates conventions of Renaissance spoken Latin pronunciation" (161 n.33). Gloria and Agnus, Queramus: "chattering semiminims" each take a syllable, parodying the French model (164). Auth. conclusion: the accent–quantity relation "operates most forcefully at the level of semibreve/minim/semiminim". At breve and long, or in very fast values, accent is "too slow to be noticeable" or too fast to attend to (164). Albertis sets text at similar speeds in C and cut-C (166 n.37). There are no counts or statistics. Towne calls quantifying composers' differences "an important process" (166).

### E. Harrán 1997
Don Harrán, "How to 'Lay' the 'Lay': New Thoughts on Text Underlay," *Musica Disciplina* 51 (1997): 231–262.

**Argument.** Lanfranco describes "text underlay": the music comes first and the syllables are fitted to it. Zarlino describes "music overlay": the notes are shaped to the words, chiefly their accents (233–242). Two further types exist: "text overlay" (later stanzas of strophic songs, contrafacta) and "music underlay" (recitative, psalmody, falsobordone; Aegidius de Murino c.1400) (245–250). Lanfranco's rules rest on separating and joining notes, not on accent, so they apply to earlier music too (243–245, 260). Strict word accent became an expectation only towards the mid-sixteenth century, and even then it was not scrupulously observed (243, 257–260).

**Findings.**
- Auth.: Lanfranco "set little store by accentuation". His penultimate-melisma rule is the nearest he comes, and it works because the penultimate is "usually accented in Latin and Italian" (243–244).
- Auth.: Lanfranco's phrase model is syllabic at the start and melismatic at the end, with the melisma on the last accented syllable, for the sake of comprehensibility (239).
- Mus.: Tromboncino (c.1520) paces Petrarch by iambic metre; Rore (1542) by word accent. The comparison is shown in the top voice (258).
- Th.: del Lago (1540) warns against barbarisms. In Italian lines the penultimate is always sustained; in eight-syllable lines the third and penultimate; in eleven-syllable lines the sixth and penultimate, sometimes the fourth (259). Zarlino, *Sopplimenti* (1588, VII.13, p. 325): follow the rhetorical accent, "not the grammatical one" (259). Stocker (fol. 30): consider "the whole sentence" more than single words (259–260).
- Auth.: infractions were willed, not ignorant (260).
- Th.: Rossetti, *Libellus* (Verona 1529): in Graduals, Introits and Responsories, with ligatures and melismas, "grammar is the handmaiden of music". *Instituta patrum* (12th century) says the same for psalm cadences. Cerone (1613) agrees with Rossetti (261–262). Auth.: "melismatic writing, by nature, encourages greater freedom" of accent (262).
- Auth.: in strophic songs, later stanzas often impose their own syntax and accents and produce "barbarisms". Did composers intend all the stanzas to be heard? (246–247)
- Auth.: "no firm conclusions" about practice can be drawn "from their prescriptions alone" (251).

## 2. Against our rules (section 10; 1.3, 1.4)

- **10.1 (minim or longer; semiminim after a dotted minim).** Supported in outline (Lf II, Z I: 73a 35; Towne R11 1990 268). Three points need correcting or adding:
  - **"Zarlino (1558) is stricter" is contradicted by Harrán's reading.** Z V bans only notes *smaller* than the dot. Z VI allows the note after a texted semiminim to take a syllable (73a 40–41). Lanfranco says "rare" (73a 40) and Vicentino "no great fault" (73b 623). Neither is a free permission.
  - **Missing condition:** when the semiminim takes a syllable, the next note takes one too (Lf V, Z VI; Stocker "must": Towne 1990 277; never second without third: 1991 155).
  - **"Never on a semiminim" otherwise is too absolute.** A semiminim beginning a run takes its syllable (Lf VI: 73a 41). A lone semiminim generally does (S: 1990 268). Semiminim series may be texted if all voices move in semiminims or the line leaps (S, Calvisius: 1990 272). **Under C, the semiminim is the normal syllable carrier** (S: 1990 268, 270; 1991 144, 154; 73a 40). If a Zieleński piece is under C, 10.1 as worded contradicts every source here. Check the mensuration.
- **10.2.** Supported (Z VII: 73a 48; 1990 267).
- **10.3.** Supported (Lf VIII, Z X: 73a 44–45). Qualified: after a semiminim run or ligature, the last syllable goes on the first note of the run (Lf, S: 73a 46; 1990 268; 1991 154).
- **10.4.** Supported, and Zarlino goes further: no rest longer than a minim mid-sense (73a 32–33). Note: in sources, a word divided across a rest may signal repetition (1991 146, 152).
- **10.5.** Latin: supported (V 8: 73b 626; 1990 283). Towne exempts Greek, i.e. Kyrie (1990 286; 1991 145, 161). Vernacular: Vicentino elides whenever vowels meet within a line, not "only where the count needs it". He keeps both vowels across line ends and rests (73b 624–626). His examples are Italian. Zarlino lets an elision be dissolved for musical reasons, so parts may differ (73a 50–51). Our Polish rule is our own extension, not his.
- **10.6.**
  - "Stressed syllable on long note or high point": supported (Z I; V rule 5; 1990 282).
  - "Long run on stressed syllable": supported by Z IX, S and Calvisius (73a 44–45; 1990 271). Qualified for older music (1990 273–274, 285; 97 243–244) and for melismatic style (97 261–262).
  - **Vowels: qualified, partly contradicted.** Vicentino's only rule is by register: low a, o, u; middle a, e, o; high a, e, i (73b 628; 1990 283; 1991 156). So i suits top voices and u suits basses. Our blanket "rather than i, u" ignores this. Only a suits every register. Finck and Bovicelli disagree with each other (1990 284).
  - "Light words off the beat": see section 3.
  - "Voices need not change syllable together": supported (Z: 73a 50–51; 1990 281; 1991 151).
  - "Tendencies to weigh by ear": supported (1991 151, 166–167; 97 251, 260).
- **10.7.** Consistent with Lanfranco's phrase model and Lf VIII / Z IX (73a 44–46; 97 239). Nothing here bears on Wacław directly.
- **10.8.** Not addressed. The articles only state that the penultimate is "usually" accented in Latin and that Stocker allows penultimate or antepenultimate (97 244; 1990 271).
- **10.9.** Supported (S N4: 1990 269). Qualified: a repeated semiminim may continue the previous syllable (S ch. 15). Ornamental repeats in a cadential suspension take a new syllable optionally (1991 154). On replacing melismas with text repetition: Stocker supports it (1990 265–267). Vicentino forbids unwritten repeats (73b 623). Zarlino allows only meaningful whole clauses (73a 43–44). Towne urges caution for earlier music (1991 147, 150–151).
- **10.10.** Supported by "similar figures, similar underlay" (1991 148). Qualified by Zarlino's "parts are unique": imitation need not be exact, but departures "must be well justified" (1990 281; 1991 151–152).
- **10.11.** Supported and extended: words are copied near where they begin; spacing and divided words show repeats; a repeated last syllable marks word ends; staff-end dots may be split (1991 146–147, 149–150, 152).
- **10.12.** Supported (1991 151, 166; 97 251, 260).
- **10.13.** "Divide notes only where text requires": supported. Division is a last resort that presumes scribal error (1990 266–267; 1991 147, 150). Skipping words in lower voices: not found. Excising excess text is listed as a last resort (1991 147).
- **10.14.** Consistent: later stanzas create barbarisms (97 246–247). There is no editorial rule here.
- **1.3, 1.4.** Supported (97 251: theory needs testing against sources; 1991 166 on quantification).

## 3. The editor's tendencies

**(a) Light words off the beat in moving figures; may stand on the beat on long notes.**
- Support (Th.): Vicentino ch. 29, "accented syllables to accented beats and short syllables to lesser beats" (1990 282). Burmeister (1606): in small notes, speed obscures accent, so either kind of syllable will do (1990 282).
- Support (Auth.): accent matters most at semibreve, minim and semiminim speed and is "too slow to be noticeable" at breve and long (1991 164; 1990 269 n.29). This matches the second half of (a).
- Counter (Th.): Stocker, "lesser beats should receive no syllable" (1990 282). A new syllable on the first of two equal notes when on the beat or at the start of a tactus (1990 278). Two-note groups take their syllable on the accented note (1991 154). Towne: syllables "may not begin on a relatively unaccented rhythmic division" (1990 285 n.44).
- Counter: inside semiminim figures, no new syllable on middle notes or on the first white note after the run (Lf VI, V 1, S: 73a 41–42; 73b 622).
- Counter: Aron reproves syncopated entries after long rests "for the difficulty of pronunciation" (73b 630). That bears on off-beat entries.
- Towne calls Vicentino's rule "an ideal" that rigid application would break (1990 285).
- On monosyllables or conjunctions as such (*et*, *in*, *de*): **not found.** The only word-class point is Vicentino's octave-leap rule. A new syllable on the upper note should begin a new word (verb, noun, participle, pronoun), not continue one (73b 627; Towne 1991 155). His example, *Fin che m'amasti* (73b 627), puts separate words on the leap.

**(b) Melismas favour open, bright vowels.** Qualified. The only period rule is Vicentino's register scheme (73b 628; 1990 283; 1991 156): a is good everywhere; o is good low and middle; e middle and high; i high only; u low only. Vicentino's reason is ease and vowel purity (73b 628). Finck and Bovicelli disagree (1990 284). Zarlino forbids vowel substitution; Vicentino notes singers do it (1990 284). Nothing measures how often composers chose vowels for melismas.

**(c) Off-beat syllables propel into open sounds on the beat ("momentum").** As a concept: **not found.** Related material:
- Towne 1991 Table VII 19e: delay a syllable "to place the ensuing syllable on a strong beat" (156).
- Vicentino's enjambment rule: lines run together without a rest connect (73b 625).
- Towne's closing image of word accents and metre in "dynamic tension, an audible shimmer" (1991 168).
- Stocker's accent-on-the-beat preference (above) points the opposite way for off-beat syllables.

**(d) Long melismas may let the music shine, not always on the stressed syllable.**
- Counter (Th.): Z IX, a melisma on a short penultimate is a barbarism (73a 44–45). S: accented penultimate or antepenultimate. Calvisius: unaccented syllables are not appropriate (1990 271).
- Support (Th.): Lanfranco ignores accent (97 243–244). Rossetti (1529): in melismatic genres "grammar is the handmaiden of music", seconded by Cerone (97 261–262). Zarlino (1588) and Stocker: the sentence accent counts more than word accents (97 259–260; 1990 282–283).
- Support (Auth.): melismatic writing "encourages greater freedom" (97 262). Poor accent is acceptable in older pieces (1990 285). Josquin-era melismatic sections may be intended (1991 150–151). Melismas tolerate new texts better (97 248).
- Against (Th.): Vicentino distrusts many notes on one vowel, and Stocker prefers repeating text to a long melisma (73b 624; 1990 265).

## 4. Relevance to our repertory

- **Central European / German.** No Polish, Kraków or Central European source is mentioned. German theorists cited: Agricola 1532 (ligatures for text: 73a 39); Coclico 1552 (Netherlanders ignore quantity: 73a 49 n.74; praise of Josquin's text placement: 73a 56 n.103); Finck 1556 (vowels: 1990 284); Calvisius 1592 and Burmeister 1606 (1990 271–282). Towne distrusts Calvisius's late licences (1990 281 n.38). Stocker is a German writing in Spain c.1570 (1990 260). Humanist odes (Cochlaeus 1511, Tritonius, Senfl) are the exception where Latin quantity was set strictly (73a 50–51).
- **Chronology.** Older/modern divide at Willaert's *Musica nova* (1540s–1559) (1990 266; 1991 153). Wacław (1550s) falls at the turn and Zieleński (1611) in the modern period. The 1590s German licences (Calvisius) and Burmeister's speed point (1606) are the nearest in date to 1611.
- **Latin vs vernacular.** Elision differs (73b 624–626). Del Lago's sustained-syllable positions in Italian lines (97 259) are a model for verse-based placement, not directly for Polish.
- **Motets vs songs.** Lanfranco excludes chansons and madrigals (73a 51; 97 243). Chanson style licenses semiminim syllables (73a 41–42; 1991 164). Later stanzas of strophic songs are "text overlay" (97 246–247).
- **Untexted sources (organ tablature): not found.** Nearest: Towne's rules for parody and contrafacta (1991 147, 152, 163). The rule that voices modelled on chant follow the model's underlay bears on chant-based pieces (1991 148, 151). Harrán: editor as "surrogate" singer, reading shapes, cadences and ligatures first (97 256). Aegidius de Murino: music first, then words "as best you can", syllabic until notes must be extended (97 249–250).
- **Block-texted prints:** see 10.11 above (1991 146–152). Zarlino blames confusion on composers' negligence (97 241). In fifteenth-century manuscripts the text was often written last (97 236).

## 5. Leads

1. **Harrán, *Word-Tone Relations in Musical Thought*** (1986). Appendix of 381 theorist citations (1990 255). Vowels (Finck, Bovicelli) at 253–254; accent citations at 376–384 (1990 285 n.42); Rossetti exceptions on elision at 412. **Highest priority** for (b).
2. **Lowinsky, "The Problem of Text Underlay," *Medici Codex*** I (1968), 90–107. Rule priorities (1991 145 n.8). Says accentuation is far from consistent "even in the best works" (1990 274 n.32).
3. **Rotola, ed., Stoquerus, *De musica verbali*** (1988). Already uploaded. Accent groups at 48–50, 75–77, 216–227 (1990 274 n.33).
4. **Tirro, diss. (Chicago 1974)** I, 322–352, tabulating Spataro's underlay habits; and "La stesura del testo," *RIM* 15 (1980). The nearest thing here to measuring a composer's practice (1990 261 n.18).
5. **Copeman, *Singing in Latin*** (1990) and **McGee, *Performer's Guide*** (1985), on pronunciation (1991 167 n.39). McGee and Meconi on *ei* (1991 159 n.30).
6. **Calvisius, *Melopoeia*** (1592), with Benndorf (1894); **Burmeister, *Musica poetica*** (1606). German rules closest to 1611 (1990 259 n.11, 271, 282).
7. **Finck, *Practica musica*** (1556), via Schlecht 1879 (1990 259 n.11). Vowels.
9. **King, "Texting in Early Fifteenth-Century Sacred Polyphony"** (Oxford diss. 1996); bibliography at 273–287 (97 231 n.3).
11. **Harrán, "Toward a Rhetorical Code of Early Music Performance," *JM* 15 (1997)**; Perkins on Dufay (1976). Both on rhetoric and declamation (97 252 n.38, 256 n.50).
12. **Fellerer, *Deklamationsrhythmik*** (1928), and Lowinsky's 1935 reply. Declamation rhythm against tactus (1990 260).

None of the five articles measures underlay statistically, and none counts vowel choice in melismas.
