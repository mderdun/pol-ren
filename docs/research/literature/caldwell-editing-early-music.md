## 1. Reference, scope, authority, limits

John Caldwell, *Editing Early Music*, Early Music Series 5 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1985). xii + 124 pp. ISBN 0-19-816143-3 (hardback), 0-19-816142-5 (paperback). Copyright page: "© John Caldwell 1985"; preface signed "Oxford, 1984". **This copy is the first edition (1985).** Nothing in it mentions a later edition. I cannot confirm a 1995 second edition from this copy. If we cite it, cite 1985. If a 1995 edition exists, Appendix IV and the accidentals chapter are the places to compare first.

**What it is.** A short practical handbook, written at OUP's request to replace the 1963 pamphlet by Dart, Emery and Morris (preface, v). It grew from Caldwell's Oxford seminar on transcription and from his general editorship of *Musica da Camera* (v–vi). Chapter 1 sets general principles. Chapters 2–4 cover music to c. 1450, 1450–1600, and Baroque/Classical. Chapter 5 covers preparing copy for a publisher. Appendices give signs (I), part-names (II), sample score layouts (III) and a summary of accidental treatment (IV). It does not teach notation or palaeography (preface, v).

**Authority.** Caldwell (b. 1938) edited the *Corpus of Early Keyboard Music* and *Musica da Camera*. He writes from the Anglo-American tradition of *Musica Britannica*, *Early English Church Music*, Apel, Schrade, Bukofzer and Dart, and names it as his model (44). His stated bias (12): notation closer to the original, fuller rather than thinner reporting of sources and variants, a plain text, and a low opinion of the claim that performers cannot read old notation. Scholars and performers need the same edition (preface v; 1). That is our principle 2.1.

**Limits.** (a) 1985. Before computer engraving: the chapter on copy assumes hand-drawn copy for a human engraver (95–102). (b) English-centred: English repertory, MB/EECM conventions, Fellowes. No Polish, Bohemian or German vocal repertory is discussed. Central Europe appears only through German keyboard tablature (65–67, Table VI). (c) Underlay gets about two pages (43–44, 63–64) and almost no rules. Our sources on underlay are Towne/Harrán and Stoquerus. (d) Nothing on chiavette, period pitch theory or high/low clefs. (e) Musica ficta: how to *mark* accidentals, at length; which accidentals to *add*, almost nothing (31–32). (f) Chant: he declines to treat neume editing (34) and covers only staff-notation monophony and square vs diamond puncta. (g) No engraving dimensions: no staff spaces, sizes or thicknesses anywhere. The numbers he gives are reduction ratios, bar lengths, tempo equivalences and metronome figures.

## 2. Chapter notes

### Preface and note (v–vi)
- Replaces the Dart–Emery–Morris pamphlet (1963). Aim: advice on editorial procedure, not palaeography or notation (v).
- "editions of the utmost integrity" is the present need. Performers' and scholars' needs are, or should be, identical (v).
- English terms for note-values (semibreve, quaver); "bar-line" vs "bar" (vi).

### Ch. 1. Principles of transcribing and editing (1–12)
**Aims.**
- Two fundamental requirements: "clarity and consistency". No difference in aim between scholarly and practical editions. A collected edition must be performable as it stands. A practical edition must keep the composer's work apart from the editor's (1).
- Editing is more than transcription. Close "diplomatic" transcripts are rarely needed now that photography exists (1; n.1 on the term).
- The draft transcription should note everything that might affect a reading: clefs, signatures, mensuration signs, the placing of accidentals, line-ends, ligatures, coloration. It should be able to take variants and allow a change of base source later (2).
- "the intentions of the composer" is often unreachable. A good substitute is a version demonstrably current at one time and place, usually best given by a single representative source (2).

**Sources and their relationship.**
- First task: assemble all the evidence. Microfilm is usually safe but can hide evidence (3).
- Secondary material that throws no light may be set aside. Report its existence and say why it is not used (4).
- Stemma where possible. When the evidence is thin or contaminated, choose a single source and emend it rather than reconstruct the archetype. Avoid "haphazard conflation" (4–5; n.3 on Reynolds & Wilson, Maas).
- Presentation should take a single source as reference point even when a stemma is used (5).
- Emend rationally, but always be able to explain how the corruption arose. Support from another source neither validates nor invalidates an emendation. With an equal choice, do not emend. *Difficilior lectio potior*: a scribe may have "edited out" an awkwardness (5).

**Introduction and sources section.**
- An introductory note is a *sine qua non*. A discussion of sources is the irreducible minimum. For older composers give liturgical or social context rather than biography (5–6).
- Printed sources: at least the BUCEM-level catalogue entry. Title-pages may keep upper/lower case and roman/italic, with line-ends as vertical strokes. Publisher, place and date always. Format (preferably in mm), pages and plate number are useful. Name the copy used and its shelfmark (6; n.4 defines edition, issue, state).
- Manuscripts: place, library, shelfmark in the library's own form. Beware obsolete shelfmarks (6–7).
- Sigla need a key, even RISM ones. Own sigla are often better. Letters, "if necessary printed in bold" (7).
- MB and EECM list sources between the music and the commentary, "often the neatest way" (7).

**Performance notes.**
- The score should be kept "as clear as possible". Performance advice goes in a separate section. A subsidiary staff above the score is acceptable where ornament or rhythm cannot be explained in words (7).
- Do not be dogmatic about forces or instruments (7–8).

**Method statement.** Every edition needs one. Older music needs more. Established conventions "still need to be stated in the edition", accidentals above all (8).

**Critical commentary: form.**
- A generous tabulated layout is the ideal. Publishers usually insist on compression (8). Footnotes are possible for variants of special interest to performers, or a special sign in the score (8 n.5).
- Entry order: bar; staff; source; reading. His example "23 III C 1st note a′". Alternatively source by source or part by part. Entries may be separated by a diagonal stroke. Staff by roman numeral or abbreviated part-name (9).
- No numeric system for position in a bar: write "5th note" (9).
- No verb, or the present tense. The past tense only for lost or destroyed sources (9).
- Helmholtz pitch names (9; Appendix I).
- Refer to the edition's note-values, not the source's, unless obsolete technicalities must be named ("brevis coloured in MS"). Be consistent either way (9 and n.8).
- Do not hesitate to describe in words what the jargon cannot (9).

**Critical commentary: content.**
- Decide which classes of variant to report and be consistent. Whole classes (ligatures, coloration, underlay) may be ignored in late or untrustworthy sources (9).
- "obvious errors have been corrected" is "not a very happy" formula. Note every corrected error except trivial typography (an upside-down crotchet) (10).
- Emendations that establish the text go in the commentary and need no typographical distinction. Additions made for performance (signs, accidentals, ornaments, conjectural restorations) are "editorial" and are distinguished in the score (10 n.9).

**Preliminary staves (incipits).**
- Valuable, and recommended for many kinds of edition. They show original clefs, which bear on scoring and pitch, and the original signature, which bears on accidentals. Later signature changes go to the commentary (10).
- Show voice ranges, at least for medieval and Renaissance music. If the preliminary staff also gives the first note or phrase, the range is better shown at the start of the score proper, in modern clefs (10). That is our layout.

**Tempo.**
- Editorial tempo marks and metronome figures go in square brackets (11).
- Tempo relationships need no brackets; use round brackets if doubtful. Write them as new value = old value at the head of the new section, or with the equals sign over the double bar (11; n.10 recommends arrows).
- Original mensuration or proportion signs go above the staff at the new section, over the modern time signature, with the scale of reduction if it changes (11). Mensuration signs are in bold in the book, modern signatures in ordinary type (11 n.11).

**Barring and numbering.** In metrical music use regular barring for performance and reference. Number bars at the left of each line except the first, not counting an initial incomplete bar (11).

**Signs.**
- Editorial tie or slur: Dart's crossed tie (a tie with a short vertical stroke through it) (11). Ligature: a horizontal bracket with downward hooks. Coloration: open corner brackets ⌜ ⌝ (12; checked on the image).
- Text: distinguish hyphens from the "continuous, ground-level line" of the last syllable. The line runs to the last note of the phrase sung to that syllable. In medieval and Renaissance music extenders "might well be omitted altogether" because they look like staff lines (12). With Mensurstrich they usually run to where the sound stops (12 n.12). Dotted rows at word-ends look like omissions and are inelegant (n.12).
- Editorial slurs marking syllable changes "are unnecessary and confusing" (12).
- Separate syllables need separate, unbeamed notes ("a matter on which views may differ") (12).

### Ch. 2. Medieval and early Renaissance music (to c. 1450) (13–44)
**Note values (13–14; Tables I–IV, 15–21).**
- Original values are "wholly misplaced" before 1300. Perotinus best at 1/16 (Apel, Waite, Thurston) (13). Late 13th and early 14th century 1/8. Early Ars Nova 1/8. Most 14th-century and early 15th-century music 1/4. Halved values "during the course of the fifteenth century". "from the sixteenth century onwards I would argue for original values in most cases" (14).
- Italian trecento: a breve may hold 4, 6, 8, 9 or 12 minims, so one scale cannot serve all values (14; Table III, 18–19: octonaria and duodenaria minims 4:3 against the others, signatures marked 2*/4 and 3*/4).
- Proportions: simultaneous ones are unambiguous; successive ones are the difficulty. Up to c. 1450 a strictly numerical reading is usually valid (16–17).
- O followed by ¢: the semibreve of ¢ is faster, but probably not twice as fast. Dufay, *Missa L'Homme armé*, Credo bars 84 ff., suggests **4:3** (17). Table IV note 5 gives sample rates for Binchois: minim = 60 in O and C, crotchet = 90 in Φ, crotchet = 80 in Ↄ, quaver = 100 in the next sign (21 n.5).
- Pre-1400 signs are rare. Where present they go on the preliminary staff; later changes go above the staff. "It is not good practice to give them in place of modern signatures" (23).
- Show the scale of reduction as a visual equivalence (minim = semibreve). Avoid ×1/4 or ÷4, which become ambiguous with proportions. In proportional passages state the equivalence "in terms of the visual relationship, not … *integer valor*" (23 and n.7).
- Original values for later 15th-century music (Wolf's Obrecht, Smijers' Josquin) "carries conviction". The older method allows "closer involvement with the composers themselves" (25).

**Beaming (25–27).** Reduction makes beaming the editor's responsibility. Guard against over-interpretation. Keep to standard groupings that subdivide the bar simply. Beam for syncopation only in the clearest cases (hemiola) (25–27). Mensurstrich "does naturally inhibit the use of the tie", but avoid "factitious complexity" (27).

**Barring (27–28).** Little music before 1500 has bar lines. The editor has a free hand but must follow the underlying pulse of the whole texture, not one part's phrasing (27–28). For 13th-century motets the tenor governs (27). Upper parts of 14th-century motets may be barred more often than slow tenors (28). Barring should not normally conflict with mensuration or tactus, though following them strictly gives bars that are too short (27–28). For music to 1450 a straight line through the whole score is the historical form (28). Layout may show structure (MB Dunstable) (28).

**Clefs (28–29).** For music to 1450 only treble, bass and octave treble (28). An all-purpose clef of medium range is needed. Do not exclude the octave treble (29). Voices may be numbered or named by period conventions. Order by pitch. Tenor and contratenor in one range: tenor below (29 n.12).

**Transposition (29).** "little point" for medieval music. Pitch is relative except for organ music. Transposition compromises adaptability. If needed, keep to the fourth and fifth. A tone has medieval precedent (Robertsbridge "Tribum", up a tone) (29 and n.13).

**Accidentals (29–33, 36).**
- What the sources do. No clear line between accidental and signature. A medieval accidental governs only the pitch level where it stands. Signatures may differ between parts. An accidental may stand well before its note, or above or below it. A sharp may cancel a flat and vice versa. The early sharp looks like a natural (29–30).
- Rule of thumb: an accidental at the start of a staff, with or without clef, is a signature. So is one with a mid-line clef change. Consistent "signatures" become modern signatures. Intermittent ones are accidentals of "prolonged validity" (30).
- Modern signatures are printed in modern form and carry modern octave meaning. Partial signatures are acceptable. Cancellations that follow from this go in small print (30).
- Theorists' ficta discussions are "only partly relevant". They exclude b♭, and they rarely say what a performer should add to a given written source. Many inconsistencies come from changes of line in copying. "It is a healthy modern tendency to restrict editorial additions to the barest necessities" (31). Bent, Hughes and Bray are liberal; Harrison and Doe sparing (32 n.15).
- **The system (32–33).** Four kinds of accidental:
  1. *Large*, before the note: in the source, or from another source of authority (or treat those as editorial).
  2. *Small*, before the note: implied but not stated, because of signature conventions or the editor's view of how far a source accidental reaches (extended past the editorial bar or cancelled within it). Context shows which.
  3. *Purely editorial*: "small above or below the staff".
  4. *Cautionary*: in round brackets.
  All carry modern bar validity (33). Some editors mark signature-consequent accidentals as full size with a dot above (33 n.17).
- Also needed: (a) the source's line-ends, in the commentary or with a small "NL" above the staff, so readers can judge how far an accidental reaches; (b) source accidentals that are redundant in modern notation, which Caldwell would print in the score (33).
- Eton Choirbook (Harrison, MB x–xii) moves every signature change to the start or end of the printed line and notes the exact point in the commentary (32).
- **Unbarred music**: every accidental belongs only to the note it precedes. Source accidentals full size, unless the editor rejects them (then to the commentary). Supplements small before the note or above the staff, depending on how close they are to the source. Cautionaries in round brackets (36).

**Chant and monophony (34–36).**
- Neume editing is beyond the book. There is "little agreement about aims and methods" (34).
- Free-rhythm chant: tail-less black noteheads are best. Quavers beamed by syllable or ligature are an alternative but not preferred. The last option for liturgical music is "traditional plainchant notation on the four-line staff" (34–35).
- Tailed and untailed notes and ligature shapes carry no meaning in non-mensural notation, but ligatures "must certainly be shown". Curved slurs get confused with syllable slurs, so drop syllable slurs. Distinguish true ligatures (solid bracket) from climacus/conjunctura groups (dashed bracket or slur) (35; Appendix I, 104).
- Plica: one sign for single and double forms. The editor decides the pitch of the subsidiary note; simple prolongation is occasionally right (35–36). In mensural music use a small note of a value the editor chooses. Not an acciaccatura. Not a stroke through the stem (39).
- Rhythmic interpretations of monophony go either above the staff as pitchless symbols (commits the performer less) or in the transcription itself. If metrical, say so (36).
- Notre Dame organum: sustained tenor notes as breves or semibreves, barred where the upper part changes, not chopped into tied notes. Silbenstrich becomes a rest, a comma, or better a short vertical stroke (37).

**Other medieval details (38–40).**
- Where parts have different bar lengths, number only the shorter bars or the controlling ones (38).
- Source barring in keyboard music: keep it. Mark a superfluous bar line with a short stroke above, a missing one with a dotted bar line (38).
- **Ligatures** should be shown in all medieval music. They stop performers redistributing syllables and help articulation (38). After c. 1320 one sign only. When the first or last note of a ligature is tied, the bracket need not cover the tied note. Draw ligatures "above the staff, so as not to impede any text, and at an angle to it" to avoid confusion. Keep the sign even over beamed groups (38–39).
- **Coloration**: corner brackets ⌜ ⌝. Variant brackets for white-void and red-void coloration, explained in the notes (39; signs checked on image). Coloration "ought certainly to be indicated": it shows tempo, accent and the composer's thought. The editor can also make clear that a black minim is not the same as a crotchet in white notation (39 and n.22).
- Repeats: modern signs, with the commentary saying what in the source they stand for. Put ouvert/clos where the source has them, or say what was moved (39–40). Estampie repeats written out in full, in angle brackets (40).
- **Lacunae**: square brackets are traditional; some publishers prefer small notes. Distinguish scribal omission from physical damage, using one method for each, explained. Angle brackets ⟨ ⟩ for written-out abbreviations (40).
- **Alternatim chant** must be included, edited from sources close in time and liturgical milieu to the polyphony. Modern Roman chant is better than nothing. Leaving it out is like cutting the spoken dialogue from a Singspiel (40).
- A full edition prints in an appendix the chant of every cantus-firmus piece and other borrowed material. The commentary identifies it and its liturgical function (40 and n.23).

**Verbal text (41–44).**
- Composers set corrupt texts. The editor purges scribal corruption but "not to correct the composer" (41). For songs, the musical source is primary for the musical-textual unit even when literary sources are older (41).
- Resist respelling. Prefer explanations of sound values and discreet diacritics (41–42). Expand abbreviations silently or with italics for restored letters. u/v to modern values. Long i eliminated except where it means j. Capitals for the start of pieces, sentences and proper names, *not* for verse lines. Other capitals silently lower-cased (42). Minimal modern punctuation (42).
- **Syllable division**: medieval principles are phonological but sources show them only fitfully. Latin: follow classical rules and explain in the preface. A syllable begins with any consonant group that can begin a Latin (or Latinised Greek) word: *San-ctus*, *o-mnis*. Compounds split into their parts (*red-emptor*; *Liber Usualis* prints *pot-ens*) (42).
- Opposed to beaming across syllable changes. A triplet sign over separate notes should be drawn so as not to look like a ligature (42–43; sign checked on image).
- Later stanzas underlaid only with care; many stanzas "can lead to illegibility". Composers did not always consider later stanzas (43).
- Ideally all underlaid text (except the Ordinary) appears separately as prose or verse with an English translation, and gets its own commentary. For strophic songs print the whole poem after one underlaid stanza. A plain prose translation is "an essential aid" and better than a glossary. Singing translations are mostly pointless (43).
- **Underlay**: the manuscript is "the ultimate authority" but may be unreliable. A scribe who wrote the text before the notes produces a near-"constant" displacement, syllables too early in melismatic passages and the opposite in syllabic ones (43). The editor decides whether a part is sung and places the syllables "as correctly as possible". A facsimile does the job of reproducing the source better (43–44). He might move each phrase's last syllable to its last note, but is "far from saying" that is justified (44).
- Editorial text in italics or square brackets (43 n.26). Partial texting in performance is possible. **Word repetition is "virtually unknown" before 1450; supply none editorially before then, and only "very sparingly" before 1500 (c. 1540 in England)** (43 n.26).
- Variant underlay in a commentary is rarely read by performers and gives scholars no clear picture. EECM has a method. Flag hard cases by footnote or sign (44).

### Ch. 3. Renaissance music (c. 1450–1600) (45–68)
**Note values (45–48; Table V, 26).**
- Practice varies widely. Caldwell accepts halved values for Continental music 1450–1520, and they "do no harm" in ordinary polyphony up to 1580 at least. They are a nuisance in *note nere* madrigals and ornamented idioms. Decide for consistency across a composer or a manuscript. "Where the arguments are evenly balanced, I would always opt for original values" (45–46).
- English music: quartered values for early Tudor, halved for late Henrician to Marian. After c. 1560 mostly original values (46).
- From c. 1450 normal duple time is ¢, which puts the tactus on the breve with little or no speed change. C in a ¢ context then implies augmentation. C as the sign of a whole piece (*note nere*, late English music) is just another time signature (46).
- Triple time after 1450 is mostly sesquialtera or tripla proportion against ¢, which makes triple time faster than duple. Editors often halve triple-time values again, or quarter them when duple values are unreduced (47–48). O2 signals an imperfect-breve tactus and normally takes a further halving (47 and n.2).
- Table V (26), 1500–1600: under ¢ semibreve = minim when halving, original values (×1) otherwise. Unreduced values for late 16th-century English music in C, and for Italian *note nere* madrigals (26 nn.1–2).

**Barring (48–50).**
- Most Renaissance music is unbarred in the sources. Barring "should neither conflict with nor be dictated by tactus theory" (48).
- Under ¢ with a breve tactus the bar is normally the tactus. **Late 16th-century music in unreduced values, slower in tempo, "will often require barring by the semibreve", whether ¢ or C** (48). Triple-time bar length follows the editor's sense of pulse (48).
- Barring of vocal polyphony is understood to be editorial and needs no broken lines. **His own preference is unbroken bar lines through the whole score.** Ties are only arithmetic. Barring only through individual staves weakens co-ordination, though staff groups may be barred separately for polychoral choirs (48).
- **Mensurstrich** (49): metrical divisions marked between the staves only. For: rhythmic independence of the parts, few editorial ties. Against: metre less visible, and "singers tend to get lost when counting the longer values". A recent convention allows a tie at line-ends, better than the older small bracketed remainder at the start of the next line. Mensurstrich editions usually run the word-end line to where the sound stops, which adds to confusion with staff lines and ligatures. **"Legibility would be considerably improved if all notes longer than the (modern) breve were written as tied notes."** Mensurstrich *can* show different bar lengths in different voices by attaching strokes only to the staves concerned (49).
- n.6 (49): Shipley's Busnois *O crux lignum* (Ex. 2) shows Mensurstrich "at its most sophisticated" (23 n.5). A newer compromise bars each staff only and puts a small stroke above and below a note that would otherwise be tied; any note longer than a breve is tied. "In many ways this is the most satisfactory method of all" (Milsom's Ockeghem *Salve Regina*) (49 n.6).
- Isochronous layout without bar lines (Van Crevel) is an eccentricity (48–49 and n.4).
- Time signatures: follow sensible modern convention. Double signatures such as 3/2 6/4 only for a genuinely variable pulse. Avoid odd forms (49–50).

**Signs and scale of reduction (50).**
- Original mensuration and proportion signs do not go on the staff. At the start they go on the preliminary staff; later changes go above the staff in the score, **in square brackets if missing in the source but understood by the editor**. **Exception: keeping ¢ and C on the staff when values are unreduced is "unobjectionable"** (50).
- Give the scale of reduction, though not for every piece if a publication is uniform (50). The preliminary staff is no substitute for it. Best form: minim = semibreve (modern note = old note). Use the largest value not subject to mensural change. Equivalence by written appearance, not *integer valor*. "×½" begs the question (50).
- Show tempo relationships between sections in different mensurations, and suggest tempos where useful (50).

**Layout, sections, voices (51–53).**
- Mensurstrich needs layout forethought. Supply a printable layout (51; n.5 at 49).
- Each main part of a Mass gets fresh preliminary staves. Separate *partes* of a motet may be titled. A thin double bar marks sections within a movement. **Bar numbers run continuously through a movement, "whether or not interrupted by plainsong insertions"** (51).
- Dropping voices: make clear which remain. Normally only for a whole section. Mid-line changes need a double bar through the score. Larger changes start a new line with abbreviated voice names (51).
- **Voice names**: follow the principal source where possible. 1450–1500 sources often name only Tenor and Contratenor. For an unnamed top part, "Cantus raises the minimum of preconceptions as to performing pitch". **Editorial voice names go in square brackets**, or a new nomenclature is used and the original names listed in the commentary. Partbook names do not always apply to every piece in the book (51–52). Roman numerals are ambiguous. V, VI = Quintus, Sextus (52). Note any change of nomenclature in the commentary (52).
- Order: Contratenor above Tenor when range and clef are the same, guided by the final note (52). Texted part placed by compass, not by name (52 n.8). Choirs grouped (52–53).
- **Clefs**: show originals on preliminary staves, or else in the commentary, together with each part's range, because arguments about pitch and vocal colour rest on them (53). Modern edition: treble, octave treble and bass only for Renaissance vocal polyphony, for consistency with medieval practice (53). Alto clef is acceptable for instrumental consort inner parts (53–54).

**Transposition (54–55).**
- "a minefield". Absolute-pitch arguments are only partly relevant. Singers rarely have absolute pitch. Conductors have their own needs. Pitch is not standard even now. **"If performers are going to have to transpose anyway, it may as well be from the original written pitch"** (54).
- Against that: keyboard accompanists (54). Remote keys are not objectively wrong (Schlick 1511 avoided D and E major; Bermudo 1555 allowed signatures to three sharps or two flats; Morley 1597 on organists' "shiftes … for ease of the singers") (54 n.10). It is confusing if transposition changes the signature pattern (54).
- The real objection to remote keys is psychological: a romantic style and a false air of exact pitch (55).
- Where written pitch is unrealistic some transposition is "inevitable". Keep to a fourth or fifth; a tone is consistent with period practice. Direction matters: C minor goes more readily to D minor than to B♭ minor (55). Mendel 1978 recommended on pitch (55 n.11).

**Keyboard parts** (55–56): decide whether an organ part is a performing part or a choirmaster's *aide-mémoire*. The latter may be omitted. Fill out in small notes. An editorial keyboard part for rehearsal only if the publisher asks (56).

**Accidentals (56–60; Ex. 3, 58).**
- Early in the period, use the medieval system (56).
- **Do not modernise signatures** (add or remove sharps or flats). In ensemble music do not edit out partial signatures, though they carry modern octave meaning. Keep small (consequential) accidentals as well as editorial ones above the staff (56).
- For much of the period an accidental can be taken to last no further than the editorial bar. Line-ends "can now be ignored" (56–57).
- The late Renaissance convention: every note needing an accidental gets one (except immediate repeats); cancellations are not marked (57).
- **Ex. 3** (58, looked at): a cadential phrase in C-clef partbook notation, halved, transcribed six ways, each (a) with and (b) without a sharp at the doubtful note A. Methods:
  1. Omit redundant accidentals and mark unmarked cancellations full size (the Bach-edition method). Works only with perfectly consistent scribes, never true of Renaissance sources except tablatures. Hides the evidence (58).
  2. Omit redundant accidentals, treat cancellations as consequential (small). Ambiguous in (a): prolonged validity or a source accidental left out? (58)
  3. As 2, with cancellation treated as editorial. Same ambiguity (58–59).
  4. Hilda Andrews' method. Unambiguous, but inconsistent with modern notation and over-supplied with editorial signs (59).
  5. **Reproduce the source exactly apart from bar lines: each accidental applies only to its own note.** The sharp at A is then editorial; cancellation is shown by silence or by a cautionary natural in brackets. **"The great disadvantage of this method is simply that it is too imperfectly understood to be workable." It would work only "with the very liberal addition of cautionary accidentals"** (59).
  6. **Recommended.** Accidentals have modern bar validity, but source accidentals that are now redundant are kept. A cancellation the editor adopts is shown as consequential (small); a retention can take a cautionary sharp in round brackets. "Overkill", but unambiguous. "recommended for scholarly editions of specialist repertories" (59–60).
- Alternative: method 2 plus the commentary. If the source marks nearly everything, list in the commentary only the places where the source's own convention would have marked an accidental and it is missing (60). "Accidental (or 'no accidental') before 5th note" is usually enough (60).
- Source accidentals the editor rejects as late additions: signal the omission by footnote or a special sign (Doe's small circle, EECM xxiv) (60).

**Ligatures and coloration (60–61).** Show them in the usual way. Minor color (two black notes) usually means dotted value plus short value, not a triplet (60). **Leaving out ligatures removes "important negative evidence" for underlay and articulation. Even when underlay cannot be achieved without breaking a ligature, print the primary source's ligatures** (60–61). Editorial ties are rarely needed in this period; use the crossed sign (61).

**Chant in Renaissance contexts (61).**
- Include alternatim chant from a suitable source (61).
- English *strene* note in a cantus firmus usually a simple prolongation (61).
- **Late medieval and Renaissance syllabic chant: distinguish square from diamond puncta, which may carry rhythm, e.g. by diamond noteheads.** Other shape differences (virga vs punctum) have no rhythmic meaning (61).
- Cantus firmus in chant-like notation in a polyphonic voice: usual equivalences long-ish shapes = breve/semibreve etc. (61; the signs are only partly legible in the scan); bring them into line with the other voices and explain in the commentary (61).
- In collected editions quote cantus firmi complete in an appendix and identify them (61).
- Lacunae as for medieval music: scribal omission, damage and expanded abbreviation kept distinct (61–62).

**Text (62–64).**
- Vernacular spelling: practice varies (French keep old spelling, English modernise). The musical edition "itself … provides the best context for preserving the old spelling", since it can keep differences between parts (62–63).
- Caldwell's view: old spelling is no difficulty if typographic tricks are changed: u/v and i/j to modern values, *y* for thorn to *th*. Expand abbreviations silently. Modernise punctuation, capitals and apostrophes as needed (63).
- **Repeats marked in the source by signs such as *ij* are written out in full and may be marked by angle brackets. Italic or square brackets are kept "for purely editorial supplementation"** (63).
- **Latin: keep the source's orthography (medieval or Renaissance), except u/v. Consonantal j may be kept.** A recommended pronunciation (Roman or national) may be given (63).
- The musical source's version of a text may be preferred to a literary one if that is what the composer set (63).
- Later stanzas sometimes ignored by the composer "may not be worth underlaying". If later stanzas are printed after the music, print the first there too, to save duplication in the commentary. All underlaid text should also be given separately, with a translation (63).
- Underlay: fewer difficulties than in medieval music. Word division by modern grammars unless the source clearly shows another system (63–64).

**Keyboard and lute (64–68).** English keyboard to c. 1560: medieval accidental methods; partial signatures edited out; source barring ignored (64). Editorial accidentals in keyboard: square brackets acceptable (64). Dot-marked accidental systems and German tablatures are consistent enough for method 1, with editorial accidentals in square brackets (64–65). Tablatures may need an editorial signature; missing accidentals covered by it go in the commentary (65 and n.18). Re-barring shown by short strokes above the staff (65). **Keyboard values not normally reduced after c. 1550; halved or quartered before. German rhythmic signs do not map exactly onto mensural values (Table VI, 67: Buxheim c. 1470, Virdung 1511, Schlick 1512, Kotter 1513–32, Kleber 1520–24; "all other German tablatures up to 1551" follow Kleber). Such transcriptions "demand special discussion" in the commentary** (66–67). Lute: sixth course as G (or A) (66). Polyphonic prolongation as a tied note in square brackets (68 and n.19). Rests are inherently editorial and need no special sign (68).

### Ch. 4. Baroque and Classical music (69–94)
Mostly outside our repertory. Points that carry over:
- Do not reduce values. Some early Baroque triple time may be halved or quartered, with the scale and tempo relation stated (69).
- Ligatures and coloration in early Baroque: use the conventional signs, not the old shapes (69).
- A line-end in the source may or may not be a bar line. Mark editorial bar lines dotted, or (Dart) original bar lines through the score and editorial ones through each staff. A removed bar line is a short stroke above (70 and n.3).
- Keep original time signatures when values and barring are kept (70–71). Editorial tempo marks in square brackets (71).
- Beaming: either the source's beaming or none of it. Separate symbols for separate syllables (71–72). No editorial syllable slurs (72). Bar-crossing dots and notes are modernised silently (72).
- Archaic key signatures (Dorian, Mixolydian, Phrygian) must not be modernised (73–74).
- Accidentals: the "Bach" method vs the small-print method, both rejected for inconsistent sources in favour of method 6. Consequential small, editorial in square brackets in this period, cautionary in round brackets (74–80; Ex. 4, 77–79).
- Dynamics and verbal indications exactly as in the source, additions in square brackets (86–87). Placing matters more than form (87).
- Lacunae: three kinds (damage, scribal omission, conventional omission). Conventional omissions restored silently or in angle brackets. Small notes for continuo and "reasonably extensive editorial reconstructions". Short lacunae in square brackets, with "om.", "illeg." or "cut off" in the commentary. Editorial slurs and ties crossed through (93).
- Readings from subsidiary sources should either be admitted to the text or treated as editorial, not marked with special typography. Typography may distinguish later alterations to a source (94).
- Vocal text: regularise u/v and i/j. Consonantal j may stay in Latin. Prose translation for foreign texts (92). Plain texts "innocent of transposition, re-scoring" are better than editions made for one performance (92).

### Ch. 5. The preparation of copy (95–102)
- Black ink, white paper. Keep copies of everything, including proofs (95). Small material in red ink or ringed in red (95; also 39).
- Follow the publisher's house style but insist on "follow copy" for beaming, bar lines and signature conventions where it matters (95–96).
- **Bar numbers** continuous through each movement, at the left of each system ("some editors prefer to number in fives or tens"). Incomplete opening bars ignored. **First- and second-time bars are treated as alternatives: 29a, 29b.** Written-out da capo sections numbered continuously (96 n.2).
- Text: clear and consistent capitals, punctuation, word division (Hart's Rules) (96). Syllables separated by hyphens; word-ends by a ground-level line where a final syllable covers several notes, "unless this feature is being designedly omitted" (96–97). Text below the staff; in vocal music dynamics above (97).
- **Title page of the music**: the first page of music should identify the work unambiguously. Opus or catalogue number with the title. **Composer at the right, "full, standardized, authoritative form" with dates** (New Grove forms), editor at the left (97–98). Capitals or bold lower case for titles; italics only for foreign words (98). Movement headings in italic, centred; numbered movements help the commentary (98).
- Separately titled movements get renewed bar numbering and end with a final bar line. Sections within get a thin double bar (99).
- Verbal instructions in italic unless roman is asked for (99).
- Letterpress double-spaced, footnotes minimal. List all abbreviations, including sigla and pitch systems (99). Pitch names (Helmholtz) underlined for italic (99 n.7).
- Proofs: read part by part, then check feature by feature (clefs, signatures, bar lines, accidentals, articulation, figures), then once more, preferably by a second reader. At least two proof stages (100).
- Business and copyright (UK, 1985): 25-year typographical copyright, and editor's copyright on the editorial contribution for 50 years after death (101–102).

### Appendix I. Special signs and conventions (103–104)
**In the score.**
- [ ] purely editorial: missing music (except substantial lacunae), missing text not in italic, missing accidentals not set small above or below, missing ornaments, dynamics and verbal indications.
- ⟨ ⟩ shown by special signs in the source but not written out: musical and verbal repetition, part duplication.
- ( ) not in the source but implied by its conventions: editorial cautionary accidentals (consequential ones are small instead), part-distribution labels.
- Small music type: grace notes small in the source; editorial accidentals above or below the note; consequential accidentals before the note; editorial rests for part-writing; substantial restored lacunae; editorial interpretations above the staff; continuo realisation.
- Italic: missing verbal text (if not in [ ]); text visually distinct in the source (red text), which then forces [ ] for missing text.
- Editorial tie or slur: crossed tie, "in preference to the older dotted form". Ligature: hooked bracket (also compound neume, archaic beam). Coloration: ⌜ ⌝. Compound neume of separate symbols: dashed hooked bracket (103–104).

**In the commentary** (104).
- Helmholtz names **in italics**: *C, c, c′, c″*. Pitch classes in roman capitals.
- Clefs by letter and line counted from the bottom (F4, C3, G2). For early music give the octave of the clef, the number of staff lines and the exact position of accidentals where needed (his example "clef d^4/6", signature "B flat, b flat").
- Note values: spell out, or for variant underlay use small superscript letters l b s m c q … A dot for dotted, round brackets for rests, hyphens for ties. Pitch names under rhythm symbols and text below that if necessary.
- **Sigla: bold capitals strongly recommended where several sources are mixed in one commentary.**
- Abbreviations: k-s, t-s, M (mensuration sign), P (proportion sign), NL (new line), LH (later hand).

### Appendix II. Part names (105–106)
- 13th–14th c.: Tenor, Duplum/Motetus, Triplum, Quadruplum (derived from theory, not sources). "Motetus" only for texted voices.
- 14th–15th c.: Tenor, Contratenor; supply Cantus/Discantus/Superius (C, D, S), Cantus Primus/Secundus (C1, C2). English: Triplex (Treble), Medius (Mean), Tenor; Quatriplex, Contratenor, Bassus.
- **Later Renaissance: Cantus/Discantus/Superius C, D, S; Cantus Primus/Secundus C1, C2; Altus A; Tenor T; Bassus B; Quintus, Sextus V, VI (roman, to avoid clashing with arabic numerals for "first", "second"); Bassus Generalis BG** (105 and n.1).
- Keep original nomenclature, abbreviate intelligibly, be consistent between score and commentary (106).

### Appendix III. Sample score layouts (107–111), looked at
1. 13th-c. motet: preliminary staves, ligature brackets, through-score bar lines, "NL" over the staff.
2. Vitry: [O] above the staff as an editorial sign, triplet bracket.
3. 15th-c. chanson, three barrings: (a) through the score, (b) Mensurstrich with long notes uncut, (c) bar lines through each staff only.
4. **Guerrero motet (1555): (a) halved values (semibreve → minim), bars through the score; (b) unreduced values under ¢, Mensurstrich, breve bars.** Version (b) is our layout. Its preliminary staves show clef, signature, sign and first note.
5. Byrd consort, C clefs kept for instruments. 6. Dowland lute song. 7. Purcell trio sonata. 8. G. Gabrieli polychoral, Mensurstrich within each choir. 9. Arne overture.

### Appendix IV. Editorial treatment of accidentals (112–113)
1. The edition is in modern notation: signatures in modern form and octave meaning (partial signatures may stay); accidentals valid for the editorial bar; cancellations restore the signature.
2. Score and commentary together must let a reader reconstruct the accidentals of the primary source (and of any other source whose readings are used). Do not conflate sources in the score: accidentals only in subsidiary sources are editorial. Always record later additions.
   - (a) Original signature on the preliminary staff or in the commentary; all later changes in the commentary.
   - (b) Source accidentals full size. Redundant ones: (i) in the score (most convenient, not always acceptable for wide circulation), (ii) in the commentary, or (iii) "negatively noted" where the source's own system would have marked one.
   - (c) Implied accidentals small, for three reasons: signature difference; prolonged validity (then the source's lineation must be shown); reduced validity (cancellation within the bar).
   - (d) Purely editorial accidentals small above or below the staff (medieval and Renaissance), or in square brackets (later music). **Note 1: editorial accidentals may further be marked as optional "within round brackets, or with ? attached" or as essential** (113 n.1).
   - (e) Editorial cautionaries before the note in round brackets.
3. Tablatures: cancellations full size, editorial accidentals as 2(d).
4. "the editor must in every case fully describe his method" (113).

### Principles §1–3 (stance, two editions, sources)
- **1.1 first-degree source; 3.2 base text.** He takes a single source as point of reference, emends it, avoids conflation (4–5). *Agree.* NOTE.
- **1.2, 3.8 distrust of editions; emend with reasons.** Explain how each corruption arose; with an equal choice, do not emend; *difficilior lectio* (5). *Agree.* **ADOPT** as a test in our critical notes: every "Emended." note says how the error arose (a misread clef, a line-end, a skipped note). Ours mostly do (Vox 17–18 A).
- **1.5 / 13.4 say what is editorial; method states only what is particular.** He says conventions "still need to be stated in the edition" (8) and that the editor "must in every case fully describe his method" (113). *Partly differ.* We state them once in the series guide and cite by number. This is defensible only while the guide travels with the editions. **NOTE**: keep the guide's URL or citation in every method section.
- **2.1 one score for performers and scholars.** "The requirements of performers and scholars are—or should be—identical" (v; also 1, 12). *Strong agreement.* NOTE. Useful to cite.
- **2.2 transposed performance edition.** He accepts transposition where written pitch is unrealistic, by a fourth or fifth, or a tone (55). He warns that singers transpose anyway, so "it may as well be from the original written pitch" (54), and that remote keys give a false air of exact pitch (55). *Agree for Vox (a fifth down). Silent-to-cautious for Zmierzka (a minor third up)*: not among his preferred intervals, though not ruled out ("Nor do I entirely discount", 55). **NOTE.** Our reason (a modern SATB choir, the clefs follow the new ranges) is the kind of practical reason he allows (54: keyboard accompanists).
- **2.2 transposed edition drops incipits.** He wants original clefs shown wherever clefs change (preliminary staves, 10, 53, 73). *Differ slightly.* **CONSIDER**: the transposed performance edition could keep the incipit with a one-line masthead note ("incipits at written pitch"), or point to the critical edition. Ours is simpler, and singers do not need the original clef.
- **2.3 editorial suggestions as small notes in round brackets (performance only).** His brackets: ( ) = implied by the source's conventions; [ ] = editorial; small notes = restorations and interpretations above the staff (103). *Differ in sign semantics.* NOTE only: in our two-edition system the performance edition explains the sign in one sentence.
- **2.4 no working material; 2.5 name only primary-source gaps.** He wants secondary material that is not used mentioned, with the reason (4). *Mild difference*: his "secondary" means later sources, not literature. NOTE.
- **3.1 sigla in a ring/frame.** He recommends bold capitals so sigla stand out from other abbreviations (7, 104). Same aim, different means. *Agree in purpose.* NOTE.
- **3.3 own sigla, key given.** He prefers editor's own sigla and always a key, even for RISM (7). *Agree.*
- **3.6 recordings collated.** *Silent* (1985).
- **3.7 lost voices from modern editions.** He prints "reasonably extensive editorial reconstructions" in small notes (93) and says readings from subsidiary sources are either admitted or treated as editorial (94). *Differ*: our Vox Altus from modern editions is full size. **CONSIDER** small notes for whole reconstructed voices in the critical edition only. A judgement call: a whole voice in small type is hard to read, and the title page and method already say which voices are reconstructed (our 3.7).

### Principles §4 Pitch
- **4.1 written pitch in the critical edition.** Caldwell: "little point" in transposing medieval music (29); for Renaissance, written pitch is the safe base (54). *Agree.* NOTE.
- **4.2 performance notes give ranges and the usual transposition; notation unchanged.** He would let the conductor transpose from written pitch (54). *Agree* with this half of our practice.
- **4.3 unclefed sources, 4.4 chiavette, 4.5 octave tests.** *Silent.* Caldwell never discusses chiavette or high/low clef systems. Our period-theory argument (Banchieri, Praetorius) has no support or contradiction here.

### Principles §5 Note values, barring, signs
- **5.1 unreduced values.** He would use "original values in most cases" from the 16th century (14). Halved values are acceptable to 1580, but "Where the arguments are evenly balanced, I would always opt for original values" (45–46). For late 16th-century music unreduced is the norm (48; Table V, 26). *Agree.* NOTE.
- **5.1 doubling a tablature's halved values (Nunc scio).** He says German tablature values do not map simply onto mensural values (Table VI, 67) and "demand special discussion" in the commentary (66). Keyboard values after c. 1550 "should not normally" be reduced (66), which is consistent with our not leaving them halved. *Agree.* **ADOPT**: per his rule on showing the scale of reduction (23, 50), the Nunc scio critical edition should print a visual equivalence above the first system, e.g. "semibreve = minim of T", since the values are *not* the source's. The method text already argues it (Nunc scio crit., Pitch and note values); the score does not show it.
- **5.2 bar = breve under ¢.** Under ¢ the bar is normally the tactus, a breve (48). But late 16th-century unreduced music "will often require barring by the semibreve" (48). *Agree for Wacław (1550s). CONSIDER for Zieleński (1611)* only if the Vox pulse reads in semibreves. Our Mensurstriche are light enough that the cost of a long bar is small, and bar numbers then match older editions. Probably keep.
- **5.2 bar numbers count both endings of a repeat.** He treats first- and second-time bars as alternatives with one number: 29a, 29b (96 n.2). *Differ.* **CONSIDER.** His way keeps the bar count equal to the music's length. Ours keeps every number unique without letters. Both are defensible; the series must pick one and state it. If we change, Plaude euge's critical notes renumber.
- **Bar numbers continue through chant insertions** (51). Our principles are silent. **ADOPT** as stated practice (check Nunc scio: does numbering run through the psalm verse? It appears to, Gloria Patri at 63–71).
- **5.3 Mensurstriche, dashed, between staves; no ties.** His own preference is through-score bar lines (48). He recognises Mensurstrich and lists its costs (singers lose count in long values; extender lines confuse) (49). He calls Milsom's variant (bars through each staff, small strokes above and below a sustained note) "the most satisfactory method of all" (49 n.6). Appendix III no. 4(b) shows our layout exactly: unreduced, ¢, breve bars, Mensurstrich (108). *Agree in kind, differ from his preference.* NOTE. Our dashed line is a further lightening he does not discuss. He says no broken lines are needed to show that barring is editorial (48). Our dashes mean "secondary", not "editorial", so the point does not bite.
- **No ties; "Legibility would be considerably improved if all notes longer than the (modern) breve were written as tied notes"** (49). *Differ*, but his advice was for halved-value editions where a long spans two bars. In unreduced values with breve bars, a long fills two bars and our rule 5.7 prints it whole. **NOTE.** One relevant point: the line-end tie convention (49). LilyPond will not break a system inside a sounding note, so the problem does not arise. Our break lists avoid it automatically.
- **5.4 mensuration sign as in the source; editorial sign stated in the method.** He keeps ¢ and C on the staff when values are unreduced ("unobjectionable", 50). He puts an editorial sign above the staff "in square brackets" (50; Ex. 1, 22, shows [Φ] in a box above the staff). *Agree on the first; differ on marking.* **ADOPT**: where a sign is editorial (Nunc scio's ¢), print it in square brackets ([¢]), so the score says what the method says. It costs one bracket.
- **5.5 incipits; 5.6 ambitus after the clef.** His preliminary staves give clef, signature, sign, first note or phrase, and he places the range at the start of the score proper when the preliminary staff has notes (10, 23, 50, 53). His examples include initial rests in the preliminary staff (Ex. 1, 22). *Agree exactly.* NOTE. He adds: fresh preliminary staves for each main part of a Mass, and for *partes* (51). Relevant if we edit a two-*pars* motet.
- **5.7 longs at finals, no fermatas.** *Silent* on fermata vs long. His Ex. 2 ends on a long without editorial fermata (24). Consistent with us.
- **5.8 rests cut at bar lines in the performance edition.** *Silent* for vocal music. He says rests in lute transcription are inherently editorial and need no special sign (68), and editorial clarifying rests may be small (90, 103). NOTE.
- **Scale of reduction where values are unreduced.** Not needed per piece if the series is uniform (50). Our principles 5.1 covers it. *Agree.*
- **Tempo.** He would add editorial tempo or metronome marks in square brackets above the score (11; Ex. 2, 24 "[minim = 60]"), and tempo relations between mensurations (50). We put pulse in the rehearsal notes only (house 9: no notes on the music page). *Differ.* **CONSIDER** for pieces with a mensuration change (none so far). For single-mensuration pieces, NOTE only.

### Principles §6 Clefs and voice names
- **6.1 the source's voice names.** "follow the naming of the principal source" (51). **Editorial names in square brackets** (52). *Partly differ.* Nunc scio's names are editorial (tablature) and Vox's lost voices are named from editions. **ADOPT**: bracket editorial voice names at their first appearance ([Cantus]) in the critical edition, as in his Ex. 2 ([Superius], Contra [II], 24). The performance edition need not.
- Abbreviations: his Appendix II gives C, A, T, B, V/VI for Quintus/Sextus in roman (105). *Agree* (house 9 uses C. A. T. B.).
- **6.2 treble, octave treble, bass.** Exactly his recommendation for Renaissance vocal polyphony (53; also 28–29 for medieval). *Agree.* NOTE. He wants the octave treble drawn unambiguously (92).

### Principles §7 Accidentals: the main area of disagreement
- **7.1 source accidentals before the note.** *Agree* (full size, 32, 112).
- **7.2 editorial accidentals small, above the note.** *Agree* for medieval and Renaissance music (33, 36, 113).
- **7.2 optional accidentals bracketed above.** *Agree*: Appendix IV n.1 allows marking optional editorial accidentals "within round brackets, or with ? attached" (113). Caution: in his main system, round brackets *before* the note mean "cautionary" (33, 103, 113). Ours are above the note, so no clash in the score; the method sentence should say what the brackets mean (it does). NOTE.
- **7.3 accidental style "forget": a sign governs only its own note; no cautionaries.** This is **his method 5** (59): reproduce the source exactly apart from bar lines, each accidental valid only for its note. His verdict: "too imperfectly understood to be workable", acceptable "Only with the very liberal addition of cautionary accidentals". His recommendation for specialist editions is method 6 (59–60; Appendix IV): modern bar validity, plus all source accidentals kept even when redundant, plus small consequential accidentals for inferred cancellations, plus cautionaries in round brackets. *Differ.* **CONSIDER, high priority.** The risk is concrete: in a bar with f♯′ (source sharp) followed by f′ (no sign), a modern singer carries the sharp. With "forget", the edition shows nothing to stop them. Options:
  - (a) Keep "forget" and add a cautionary natural, in round brackets before the note, wherever the same pitch returns unaltered within one Mensurstrich bar after an altered note. Caldwell's condition for method 5 to work. Cheap to automate as a check (section 4).
  - (b) Switch to method 6. More change, and it puts small consequential naturals before notes.
  For our Mensurstrich scores and singers used to modern convention, (a) keeps our rule and meets his objection. 7.3's "no cautionaries" would have to go.
- **7.2 "applies to that note only" for editorial accidentals.** In his system editorial accidentals follow the modern bar rule (33). Ours do not. Same problem as above; (a) solves it.
- **Redundant source accidentals.** He keeps them in the score (59, 112 2(b)(i)). Under "forget" we print every source accidental, so we already keep them. *Agree.*
- **Partial signatures, no modernised signatures.** "should not … modernize them … nor … should 'partial' signatures be edited out" (56). Our 4.1 keeps the source's signature. *Agree.*
- **Original signature on the preliminary staff; later changes in the commentary** (10, 32, 112). *Agree* (incipits). NOTE: say so for any piece with a signature change.
- **7.4–7.5 grounds (mi contra fa, sixth to octave, una nota super la, both-or-neither).** *Silent*: he does not discuss which accidentals to add, beyond "restrict editorial additions to the barest necessities" (31). NOTE: his stance supports our 7.6 (justify every editorial accidental).
- **7.6 an accidental only in a modern edition is editorial.** "accidentals found only in subsidiary sources are best regarded as 'editorial' if adopted at all" (112). *Agree.*
- **Source accidentals judged later additions.** Always record (112); signal an omitted source accidental by footnote or a small circle (60). Our principles are silent. **NOTE**; relevant if a later hand is found in a partbook.
- **Line-ends of the source.** For late Renaissance music "line-ends can now be ignored" (56–57). For medieval or quasi-medieval sources, mark them ("NL") (33). *Silent in ours.* NOTE: for Bogurodzica and Plaude, if a source accidental's reach is in question, cite the line-end in the critical note.

### Principles §8 Signs
- **Ligature: horizontal bracket.** *Agree* (12, 38–39, 103). His details: above the staff, never covering tied extensions, kept even over beamed groups (38–39). **ADOPT** the check that the bracket is always above the staff (never below with the text).
- **Coloration: corner brackets ⌜ ⌝.** *Agree exactly* (12, 39, 104). His note on minor color (two black notes = dotted value + short, not a triplet, 60) is a transcription rule worth stating if it arises. NOTE.
- **Note supplied: small notehead; illegible/missing: square brackets.** He allows either sign, provided scribal omission and damage are kept apart and the method explains which is which (40, 61–62, 93). *Agree in principle.* NOTE: our split is "editor's addition" vs "unreadable in the source", a slightly different axis from his "omission" vs "damage". Fine if the method states it.
- **Dashed tie = source note divided for text.** His editorial tie is the crossed tie, "in preference to the older dotted form" (11, 103). *Differ in glyph.* A reader trained on Dart reads our dashed tie as an editorial tie, which is in effect what it is (one source note, two edition notes). **NOTE**; no change needed.
- **Italics for editorial text, including ij.** Editorial text in italics or square brackets: *agree* (43 n.26, 103). But text the source gives by an *ij* or idem sign is, for him, *not* editorial: written out, "may be marked by (angle) brackets", and italics are kept for "purely editorial supplementation" (63; ⟨ ⟩ in Appendix I, 103). *Differ.* **CONSIDER**: ⟨ ⟩ for text expanded from *ij*, italics only for text the source does not indicate at all. That keeps the evidential distinction he cares about (Towne also treats idem signs as evidence of repetition). Cost: a second text sign under the notes.
- **[c.f.] above the staff.** *Silent* on the sign. He wants the cantus firmus identified in the commentary and, in a full edition, quoted complete in an appendix (40, 61). **CONSIDER** for Nunc scio: print the introit chant (from a Kraków source of c. 1550–90) in an appendix of the critical edition. It would also justify the [c.f.] entries.
- **Emendation silent in the score, noted in the commentary.** *Agree*: text-establishing emendations need no typographical distinction (10 n.9).
- **No editorial syllable slurs.** *Agree*: they are "unnecessary and confusing" (12; 72).

### Principles §9 Repeats and structure
- **9.1–9.2 each repeated section on its own system; write variant bars into both endings.** *Silent* on the first. He writes out repeats with variants in full (40). *Agree.*
- **9.3 liturgical order by rubrics.** He requires alternatim chant to be included from a close source (40, 61). *Agree.* **NOTE**: the Nunc scio psalm verse should come from a period Polish source where possible, not the modern Graduale (40: modern Roman chant is a last resort, "better … than to omit").
- Double bars for sections within a movement (51, 99). *Agree* (our solid section lines).

### Principles §10 Underlay
Caldwell offers little on underlay rules. His points:
- The source is the ultimate authority but may be displaced systematically; the editor must place syllables "as correctly as possible" (43–44). Supports 10.11–10.12 and 1.4.
- Word repetition: none editorially before 1450, sparingly before 1500 (43 n.26). Our repertory is later, so it does not bite. Consistent with 10.9's caution.
- Ligatures: print those of the primary source even where the underlay must break them (60–61). Our principles are silent on breaking a ligature. **ADOPT** as a rule beside 10.13: the ligature bracket stays even when the edition puts a syllable inside it, and the critical note says so.
- Separate unbeamed notes for separate syllables (12, 42, 71–72). *Agree* (`\autoBeamOff`).
- Extender lines: in Renaissance music they "might well be omitted altogether" (12). With Mensurstrich they run to where the sound stops (12 n.12, 49). We print extenders to the last note of the syllable (Vox). *Differ mildly.* NOTE; ours is the modern norm and helps singers.
- Later stanzas: underlay with care, the whole poem printed after one underlaid stanza (43, 63). 10.14 already handles this more carefully. *Agree.*
- **Syllable division**: classical rules, consonant groups that can start a Latin word begin the syllable (*San-ctus*, *o-mnis*), compounds split, explained in the preface (42). Our scores use *San-cto*, *ex-spe-*, *re-spi-*, *de-spi-* (classical) but also *om-ni-* (non-classical by his rule). **ADOPT**: state the division rule once in the series guide (Text section) and make the scores consistent; decide *o-mnes* vs *om-nes*. Polish follows the modern dictionary (64: modern grammars unless the source shows another system).

### Principles §11 Text
- **11.1 Latin: classical spelling (exspectatione), u/v distinguished.** He would keep the source's Latin orthography, medieval or Renaissance, changing only u/v (and allowing consonantal j) (63, 42, 92). *Differ.* Our 11.6 already keeps source spellings like *confidencium*. So our rules pull both ways. **CONSIDER**: make 11.1 and 11.6 one rule. Caldwell's version: keep the source's spelling, normalise only u/v (and i/j), expand abbreviations silently.
- **11.2 Old Polish: modern spelling, old forms kept.** Close to his approach for vernaculars: modernise typographic tricks, keep forms and sounds (63). *Agree.*
- **11.3 diplomatic text in the critical edition.** He says a facsimile reproduces a source better than literal copying (44), and title-page transcription can keep case and line-ends (6). *Agree.*
- **11.4 literal translation.** "a plain prose translation is an essential aid … very much more useful … than a glossary" (43). *Agree.*
- **11.5 one pronunciation guide.** He allows the editor to recommend a pronunciation (Roman or national) (63). *Agree.*
- Capitals: not at the start of verse lines (medieval texts, 42). Our stanzas capitalise line openings? Check. NOTE.

### Principles §12 Chant
- **12.1 oldest notated witness; neume grouping followed.** *Silent* on base choice; he insists ligatures and compound neumes be shown (35). *Agree.*
- **12.2 square notation unmeasured; rhythmic readings reported, not adopted.** He lists four-line chant notation as an acceptable option (35). He prefers tail-less black notes for most purposes (34–35). He allows rhythmic interpretation above the staff (36). *Agree* with ours as one of his options. NOTE.
- **Diamond vs square puncta.** In late medieval and Renaissance syllabic chant they "may have a bearing on the rhythm" and should be kept (61). **CONSIDER for Bogurodzica**: check whether the base witness distinguishes diamond (rhombic) puncta in syllabic passages. If so, Gregorio's inclinatum is not the right sign for an isolated diamond; a note or a distinct glyph would be needed.
- **Plica / liquescent**: a small note, pitch chosen by the editor, or simple prolongation (35–36, 39, 61). If Bogurodzica's witness has plicae, Gregorio's liquescent forms serve; say so in the method. NOTE.
- **12.3 bar lines editorial for verse structure.** He uses short vertical strokes for Silbenstrich (37). *Agree in spirit.*
- **12.4 no B-flat unless the base witness has it.** He adds supplements small before or above (36). Ours are stricter (none). *Compatible.* NOTE.
- **12.5–12.6 faint notes, later hands described.** *Agree*: record later additions (112), distinguish later hands typographically if needed (94).

### Principles §13 Paratext and house style
- **13 critical edition order: … Sources, Method, … Score, Critical notes, Literature.** MB/EECM put the source list between music and commentary (7), "often the neatest way". *Differ in placement*; ours is also standard. NOTE.
- **Source citation.** BUCEM-level entries, case and type distinguished, line-ends as strokes, publisher/place/date, format in mm, copy and shelfmark (6–7). **ADOPT** as the series' checklist for Sources entries of printed sources (the 1611 Vox print, the 1553 Lamentationes, etc.).
- **House 8 / §14 title.** He would have the composer at the right "in a full, standardized, authoritative form" with dates, and the editor at the left, on the first page of music (97–98). Ours: composer right on the score heading, no dates; editor on the title page. **CONSIDER** composer's dates on the score heading (the anonymous rule in 14.1 already puts a date in that slot).
- **House 9 bar numbers at system starts.** *Agree* (11, 96 n.2).
- **House 9 "no notes on the music page".** *Agree* with "the score ought to be kept as clear as possible" (7). He allows a footnote or special sign for variants of special interest (8 n.5, 44). We chose not to. NOTE.
- **House 10 critical notes: bar | voice | note; Helmholtz roman.** His order is bar; staff; source; reading (9). Present tense (9). Past tense only for lost sources (9). Helmholtz in *italics* (104). *Agree on columns and tense. Differ on Helmholtz type.* **NOTE** (roman is fine and consistent with house 10). **ADOPT** the tense rule explicitly: present for extant witnesses, past for lost ones. Zmierzka's lost A and Nunc scio's destroyed T are exactly the case.
- **"obvious errors corrected"** formula forbidden; every corrected error noted (10). *Agree* (our "Emended." notes). NOTE.
- **Classes of variant**: decide and be consistent; whole classes may be omitted for late sources (9). **ADOPT** as a sentence in each method: which variant classes are reported (e.g. "ligature and coloration variants between N and M are not reported").
- **Proof-reading** feature by feature (100). NOTE for our checks list.

## 4. Parameters and checks we could encode

No engraving dimensions are given anywhere in the book. All items below are conventions.

1. **Cautionary natural after an altered note (accidentals, 59).** In `tools/` (lint or a MusicXML pass over the exported `.musicxml`): within one bar of one voice, if a pitch (same step and octave) carries a ♯ or ♭ and a later note at that step has no accidental, flag it. Fix by `\cautionary` (round brackets before the note). LilyPond: a custom `autoAccidentals` rule is possible, but a check plus manual `?` (LilyPond's cautionary syntax `fis?`… `f?`) is simpler and keeps "forget" for everything else. Ref. 59, 113 2(e).
2. **Editorial mensuration sign in brackets (50; Ex. 1, 22).** `pol-ren.ily`: a `\edMensuration` variant printing the sign in square brackets above the staff, or bracketing the time-signature stencil. Used by Nunc scio.
3. **Scale-of-equivalence line (23, 50).** A `\prEquivalence "semibreve" "minim"` markup above the first system, critical edition only, when values are not the source's (Nunc scio). Two note glyphs and an equals sign; the old note in its mensural shape (50: "old-fashioned shape … prevents any confusion").
4. **Editorial voice names in square brackets (52; Ex. 2, 24).** `\prStaff` option `#:editorial-name #t` → "[Cantus]" on the first system, critical edition only.
5. **Ligature bracket always above the staff (38–39),** never covering a note tied in from outside the ligature. Lint: `\startLigature` brackets have `direction = UP` set in the house file.
6. **Angle brackets ⟨ ⟩ for text expanded from *ij* (63, 103)** if Miki chooses the CONSIDER in §3/§8. `\ijText` in `pol-ren.ily` wrapping the syllables in ⟨ ⟩ (check that Junicode has U+27E8/27E9; otherwise take them from the fallback font), italics kept for purely editorial text.
7. **Critical-note tense check (9).** Lint over `.tex`: in a critical note naming a siglum marked *lost* or *destroyed* in Sources, flag present-tense verbs ("prints", "has"). Cheap regex, manual review.
8. **Latin syllable division (42).** A check that every Latin hyphenation in `voices.ily` follows one rule list: consonant groups that begin Latin words (sc, sp, st, ct, mn, gn, pt, ps …) stay with the following syllable; compounds (*ex-spe-*, *con-*, *in-*, *ab-*, *sub-*) split at the prefix. Output only mismatches (*om-ni-*).
9. **Bar numbering of endings (96 n.2)** if changed: LilyPond `alternativeNumberingStyle = #'numbers` gives 29a/29b-style numbering (`'numbers-with-letters`). One switch in `pol-ren.ily`.
10. **Commentary abbreviations (104):** if we ever need them, NL (new line in the source), LH (later hand), M (mensuration sign), k-s (key signature). Cite clefs as letter + line from the bottom (C1, C3, F4), as our Nunc scio method already does.
11. **Sources checklist (6–7):** for a print: title (case and roman/italic kept, line-ends "|"), publisher, place, date, format in mm, pages/foliation, plate or RISM number, copy used and shelfmark. For a MS: city, library, shelfmark in the library's form, with older shelfmarks noted.
12. **Proof checklist (100):** clefs, signatures, bar lines (types of double bar), accidentals, ligature and coloration brackets, underlay hyphens/extenders, bar numbers; then a second reader.
