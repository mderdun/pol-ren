# Gould, *Behind Bars*: reading notes

Elaine Gould, *Behind Bars: The Definitive Guide to Music Notation* (London: Faber Music, 2011). xviii + 676 pp. ISBN 978-0-571-51456-4. Read from the DjVu scan; page numbers below are the book's printed numbers (printed page = DjVu page − 20 throughout the body). Roman numerals are the front matter. Music examples were checked as images wherever a point depends on them (pp. 39, 83, 160, 236–237, 433, 435, 439, 447–449, 483).

## 1. The book: authority and limits

*Behind Bars* is the standard modern reference on how to engrave and lay out music. It was written by a music editor at Faber Music (acknowledgements, xvii–xviii) and is organised in three parts: general conventions (chs. 1–8), idiomatic notation for instruments and voices (chs. 9–15), and layout and presentation of scores and parts (chs. 16–20). Each topic runs "definition, design, placing, use" (xiv). Gould states that she derived her conventions from engraving practice of about the last 150 years, names Henle and Bärenreiter as present-day models, points to Ted Ross for beam detail, and says plainly that many of her views are her own recommendations (xii–xv, 21).

Its authority is that of the house tradition of English and German music publishing c. 1850–2010: it is the best single statement of what a trained performer expects to see, and it gives numbers (in stave-spaces and millimetres) for most things. Its limits for us are equally clear:
- It is about **modern notation for modern performers**. Early music appears in four places only: the extra C clefs (6), diamond heads with central stems (11), editorial accidentals (83) and the vocal incipit (433). There is nothing on Mensurstriche, ligatures, coloration, mensuration signs, musica ficta, longs or unreduced values.
- It is not a book about **critical editing**. "Editorial" means a bracket or a small sign; there is no apparatus, no sources, no method.
- Its repertory is orchestral, chamber, keyboard and contemporary (a large share of the book is extended techniques and aleatoric notation). The vocal chapter (15) and the layout chapters (16–18) are what matter most to us; the choral advice assumes modern SATB choirs reading from published octavos.
- Its word-division rules cover English and Latin only (441–446). Nothing on Polish.

So: read it as the description of the reader we are writing for (a modern singer), not as an authority on how sixteenth-century music should look.

## 2. Chapter notes

### Introduction (xi–xv)
- Bad layout (spacing, page-turns) is the commonest cause of stumbling in rehearsal; performers are "too tolerant of poor presentation" (xi–xii).
- Use established conventions where possible, because the familiar is read fastest; novel notation alienates (xiii).
- Topics run definition, design, placing, use (xiv).

### Ch. 1 Ground rules (3–44)
**Stave and clefs.**
- The stave-space is the unit of all measurements (5).
- Six C clefs; the less common ones "replicate the original clefs of early music vocal scores" (6). A clef is indented about one stave-space into the stave (6).
- Never omit the clef on a stave (7). Changes of clef mid-system are two-thirds size; stay in one clef up to at least three ledger lines (7). Some nineteenth-century editions put tenor voices in the tenor clef (7).

**Noteheads, stems, beams.**
- A semibreve is wider than a black head (2½ semibreves = 3 black heads) and is shaded the other way from the minim (10). The breve has three accepted designs, with single or double side bars (10).
- Diamond heads with a central stem replicate early-music editions (11).
- Stems are thinner than a stave-line; standard length 3½ spaces, never under 2½ (13–14). Notes on the middle line take a down-stem by default. "Some editions of vocal music use up-stems only" so that the text can sit close (14).
- Beams are ½ space thick with ¼ space between (17). Short groups should not slope more than one stave-space (19–20).

**Ledger lines, octave signs.**
- Ledger lines are about twice the thickness of stave-lines and just over two spaces long (26).
- Voices and brass "should never need octave signs" (33).

**Rests.**
- Semibreve and minim rests are ½ space thick and a minim-head long; the breve rest fills one space (34–35). Rests stay centred on the stave whatever the pitch around them (35).
- Many editions keep minim and semibreve rests off the middle line in two-part writing (37).

**Bar lines.**
- A bar line is thicker than a stave-line and conspicuously thicker than a stem (38). The *systemic* bar line joins all staves at the start of a system (38).
- Thin double bar: ordinary thickness, about ¾ space apart, for section divisions only, not for every change of metre (39). Final bar: thick line of beam thickness (½ space), thin line ½ space before it, used only at the end of a movement (39).

**Rhythmic spacing (39–43).** Duration is read from horizontal space, and poor spacing is "often the main problem" of bad music (39). Spacing is not proportional: each longer value gets only a little more room. Gould's units (image checked, 39): semiquaver 2, quaver 2½, dotted quaver 3, crotchet 3½, dotted crotchet 4, minim 5, dotted minim 6, semibreve 7. That is roughly ×1.4 (√2) per doubling of value.
- A longer value must get more space than a shorter neighbour, or at least as much (40). Equal values get equal space across a whole system (40). Like passages should have similar numbers of bars per system (40).
- A bar line sits where the next beat would fall, usually a little earlier (41). Back-to-back stems look too close and need optical adjustment (41).
- A single bar-long note with no other durations in any part sits just left of the centre of the bar; if other parts move, it sits at the start (41).
- Minimum ½ space between any two characters (41). At the start of a system: clef, key and time signature 1–1½ spaces apart; 2–3 spaces before the first note; an accidental never closer than one space to the preceding sign (41–42). Mid-system: one space after a clef and on both sides of a bar line; stems never closer than one space to a bar line; accidentals may close to ½ space (42–43).

### Ch. 2 Chords, dotted notes, ties (45–73)
- Notes of different value need separate stems; upper part stems up (47, 52). Unisons may share a head if the values allow (52–53).
- The duration dot is about twice the staccato dot, about ½ space from the head; a note on a line puts its dot in the space above (54).
- "The function of a tie is to show the absence of rearticulation" (60). A tie is a tapered symmetrical arc, flatter than a slur; square brackets must not be used as ties (60). Depth 1–1½ spaces; the centre of the curve should not sit on a stave-line (61).
- Ties curve away from the stems (64). Across a system break a tie stops short of the last bar line and restarts after the clef etc., keeping its symmetrical shape and direction (65). Same-direction ties at least one space apart (66).

### Ch. 3 Accidentals and key signatures (75–98)
- Exact sizes of sharp, flat, natural (77). Accidentals are reduced only for grace and cue notes (78).
- Conventional rule: an accidental lasts the bar, at that octave, in that clef, on that stave (78–79). When two voices share a stave in choral music, confirm or cancel the accidental in every part (79–80). A tie carries an accidental over a bar line only for the tied note (80). Over a system break the accidental of a tied note may be repeated, optionally bracketed (80). Repeating accidentals after every bar line is not recommended in performers' copies (80–81).
- After a bar line, restate or cancel when the pitch recurs at once (81). In performance material, add accidentals for a pitch that recurs within a few bars (82).
- **Cautionary accidentals** (82–83). They confirm augmented and diminished intervals; in vocal music the singer should be warned of close dissonances (semitones) with other voices (82–83). Traditional form: in (round) brackets; but many brackets hurt legibility, so either write them plainly or, where room is short, put them small above the note (83, image checked).
- **Editorial accidentals**: "Square brackets around accidentals should be used only to indicate editorial additions" in practical or scholarly editions; or small accidentals above the note (83). Note that small-above-the-note is also one of Gould's forms for a *cautionary* sign.
- **Accidental systems** (86–87). The rule "an accidental applies only to the note it precedes" is acceptable when performers have time to learn the piece; it is advisable to add a natural to an altered pitch that is repeated straight away, "for safety" (86). Any non-standard system must be applied consistently and stated in the preface (83, 86–87). In unbarred music an accidental covers its note, immediate repetitions and a beamed group (87).
- Chord accidental stacking (87–91); key signatures on every stave, order of fifths (91); key change after the bar line, double bar only at a new section (92–93).

### Ch. 4 Dynamics and articulation (99–122)
- Dynamics in italic; *f* 2½ spaces high, *p* 2 (101). In vocal music dynamics go above the stave to keep clear of the text (101); in choral scores a dynamic may sit in the outer stave-space to avoid the text above (102).
- Brackets carry meaning here too: interim dynamics are bracketed (107); a bracketed dynamic is a reminder of an unchanged level (108).
- The slur, for a singer, shows "the number of notes sung to one syllable" (109). Slur design and placement (109–111). Across a system break the slur stops short of the bar line, restarts after the clef, and is visibly open-ended (112). A phrase gets one unbroken slur (112).

### Ch. 5 Grace notes, arpeggios, trills, glissandos (123–147)
- Grace notes are slightly smaller than cue notes, which are ¾ size; grace-note accidentals 2 spaces long instead of 3 (125).
- Small notes in brackets recur as a sign for something not separately sounded or not part of the main line: the trilling pitch (139), a glissando's end pitch (144), interim pitches (144).
- Glissando vs portamento: say which in the preface (140).

### Ch. 6 Metre (149–190)
- **Barring for practicality.** Some composers bar only for co-ordination; such scores should say that "barlines are for co-ordination only" and carry no metric accent (151). Repeated at 609: bar lines without metric stress must be explained in a preface.
- Time-signature figures fill the stave height (152); repeated at a new movement; a double bar only at a new section (152).
- Beaming follows the beat; never beam across the middle of a 4/4 bar (153).
- **Rests**: placed at the start of their duration, as if they were notes (159). A **whole-bar rest is centred** in the bar (159). The semibreve rest is the whole-bar rest in any metre, except that for "4/2 or 8/4 and over" the breve rest is the whole-bar rest (160, image checked). Rests are grouped to show the beats; in two- and four-beat metres they expose the middle of the bar (160–161). Older editions avoid dotted rests (161).
- Long notes across beats: expose the beat structure unless the rhythm is very simple (166–168).
- **Dotted bar lines** divide long bars where the beat grouping is not otherwise clear (178).
- Tempo marks bold roman above the top stave (182). Fermata about 1 space high and 2 wide, centred on the note, above the stave (188). With two voices on one choral stave, pauses may go on the outer voices only, to keep clear of the text (189).

### Ch. 7 Tuplets (191–215)
- Numerals italic, 1½ spaces high (193); square brackets, not the curved arcs of older editions, which look like slurs (194). Brackets outside the stave (199). Duplets in compound time may keep the traditional notation (204).

### Ch. 8 Repeat signs (217–240)
- Tremolo and repeat-bar abbreviations: write repeats out in performers' copies unless space is short (219–233).
- Repeat bar lines replace the bar line (233). "Where possible, the beginning of a repeat should coincide with a new system" (234).
- Bracketed reminder accidentals and dynamics help the performer going back to the start of a repeat (235). Any variation between repeats: write it out, or use separate endings (235–236).
- Volta brackets: bold figures with a full stop, "1." "2.", placed like tempo marks (236, image checked). A first-time bracket is open across a system break; at the very end the last bracket may run to the final bar line and close (237).
- Bar numbers: in ensemble music "number consecutively through separate endings" (237); some editions use 19a/19b (237). Sections to be returned to should start a system; a mid-piece *Fine* should end one (240).

### Chs. 9–14 Wind, percussion, keyboard, harp, guitar, strings (241–430)
Read for anything bearing on voices or early music; nothing on either. Points worth having:
- In strict counterpoint on keyboard staves every part has its own rests, including whole-bar rests (313).
- Organ music in landscape; organ accompaniment of a choral work on two staves (342, 364). (Relevant only if an organ part is ever added.)

### Ch. 15 Vocal music (431–476)
**Clefs and incipit (433, image checked).**
- Clefs other than treble and bass are "used only to replicate authentic performing editions of early music" or in an incipit (433). Tenors read the treble clef an octave down, and the clef with an 8 below "is recommended" because it marks the tenor line (433).
- The **incipit** gives the original clef, key signature (if any), time signature and first note in its original value, optionally the rests before the first note, and the voice's original name (433). Gould's example (Marenzio) prints the incipits to the left of the system bracket with the original names in small capitals (CANTUS, ALTUS ...) and the modern names (SOPRANO ...) above the modern staves.

**Marks around the text.**
- Dynamics, expression marks and instructions go above the stave (434). Articulation may go above the stave too, so that the text stays close (434).
- **Beaming.** Until the twentieth century vocal music had a flag for each syllable and beamed notes only within a melisma; Gould prefers instrumental beaming plus syllabic slurs (435, image checked).
- **Syllabic slurs**: a slur joins the notes sung to one syllable (435). Long ones go above the stave (436). Phrase marks are generally not used in vocal music, because they would be confused with syllabic slurs (436).
- **Breaths**: rests, or a tick above the stave (time taken from the note), a bracketed tick (optional), a comma (extra time) (436). An undertie under the text forbids a breath (436).
- Tuplet signs above the stave (437). Spell each vocal line melodically (437).

**The text (437–440).**
- Print the poem before the music in its own layout, with any unset lines in square brackets, and give a translation if the language is unfamiliar (437–438). Credit the text (438).
- Text under the stave in roman; italic for sounds outside the literary text (438).
- Capitals and punctuation as the author wrote them, even against the musical phrasing (438). Repeated words are separated by commas, with the final punctuation only at the very end; a repetition starts lower-case. A voice taking up text begun in another voice starts with a capital only if the source has one (438).
- **Size**: a lower-case letter about one stave-space high; never reduce text to fit (438).
- One baseline for each line of text across the whole system; text is not raised or lowered for low notes (439, image checked).
- **Placement**: one syllable to one note is centred under the note; a very long syllable puts only two or three letters left of the note (439). A syllable over several notes (melisma or tie) is aligned left under the first note (439). Always-left alignment is "not helpful" (439–440).
- Text distorts spacing; give longer values more room where possible, but full instrumental spacing is impractical (440).

**Word division (441–446).** Divide for word recognition and for placing sounds where they are sung, and be consistent (441). English rules (441–444). **Latin** (444–446):
- Divide vowel from vowel (*di-es*, *cre-at*, *tu-us*) except the diphthongs *ae, au, ei, eu, oe* (445).
- Carry a single consonant over (*a-mo-re*, *Do-mi-nus*), including consonantal i (*e-ius*, *in-iu-ri-a*) (445). Prefixes ending in a consonant may stay whole (*ex-au-di-o*) (445).
- Divide double consonants (*an-nus*, *ter-ra*); keep consonant + l/r together (*pa-tris*, *la-cri-ma*); l, m, n, r usually divide from the next consonant (*an-ge-lus*, *sem-per*) (445).
- Other clusters have two valid systems, which "should not be mixed" (445): carry over (*fa-ctus*, *i-pse*, *ma-gnus*, *o-mnis*, *san-ctus*), better in melismatic settings because consonants appear where sung (446); or divide (*fac-tus*, *ip-se*, *mag-nus*, *om-nis*, *sanc-tus*), fine in syllabic settings. Pronunciation (German or Italian Latin) may decide *ig-ne* / *i-gne* (446). Never *sanct-us* or *sa-nctus* (446).
- **Elision**: an undertie under the text joins elided syllables (446).

**Extenders and hyphens (447–449, images checked).**
- Extender: stave-line thickness, at the baseline, running to the last written note (not the end of its value); punctuation before it (447). At a new system it starts just before the first note (447). None for one note per syllable (447). Across short rests it continues; across longer rests the syllable is repeated in round brackets, meaning "not re-articulated" (447).
- Hyphen: centred at half the x-height; never an extender between syllables (448). A word broken at a system end takes a hyphen at the end and, more clearly, another at the start of the next system (448). Hyphens may be omitted only in two-syllable words that cannot be misread as elided (448). In wide gaps, hyphens at regular intervals, never directly under a note (448–449).

**Verses and languages (449–451).**
- All verses may go under one line if the rhythms differ only now and then; otherwise set each verse out separately (449). One alternative rhythm on the stave in cue-size notes; a third needs a cue stave (449).
- A second language goes below in italic; a refrain may be italic (449).
- Different syllable counts: either all rhythms full-size with dotted ties and slurs for what does not apply to every verse, or the principal text's rhythm full-size and extra cue notes (450–451). Slurs for the alternative text only are dotted (451).

**Sounds (451–461).** A pronunciation guide by IPA, or for amateurs by comparison with familiar words (451). After a page-turn, repeat the syllable of a long melisma in round brackets (452). Final consonants are not written separately (453). Speech, Sprechstimme, whispering (456–461).

**Choral writing (462–470).**
- Singers who hold their copies: no larger than A4, no landscape (462). Title says the number of voices and whether accompanied (462).
- Full names in the indented first system; when the layout never changes, labels are "unnecessary beyond the opening system" (462).
- One stave per voice, highest at top, all choir staves joined by a square bracket, even a single stave (462–463).
- **Bar lines**: the systemic bar line joins the whole choir at the left; otherwise "each vocal stave always has separate barlines, to avoid intersecting the text" (463).
- Keep a stave for each voice even when it rests for a system, because singers find their line by its position (464).
- Time signatures on every stave; tempo at the top of the system; dynamics above each stave; each stave its own underlay (465).
- Divided voices on one stave (466–468); two-stave SATB (468–469).

**Choral reduction (471–476).** A rehearsal keyboard reduction "wherever possible", on staves about ¾ size, labelled for rehearsal only, note for note, without octave transposition (471–476).

### Ch. 16 Preparing materials (479–498)
- **Paper** (481): A4 is the usual choral and vocal-score size; printed choral octavos are 254 × 178 mm. Margins at least 15 mm on all sides (481). Landscape is unsuitable for singers (482).
- **Stave sizes** (482–483, table checked as image). Rastral sizes as stave height: 0 = 9.2 mm, 1 = 7.9, 2 = 7.4, 3 = 7.0, 4 = 6.5, 5 = 6.0, 6 = 5.5, 7 = 4.8, 8 = 3.7. Single-stave parts mostly 3–5 (7.0–6.0 mm); piano music and songs 2–5; **choral music 6–7 (5.5–4.8 mm)**; full scores 8. Too small is the real danger; keep one size throughout a piece (482).
- **Pages and bar numbers** (483–484): music starts on a recto; the first page of music is not numbered; page numbers at the top outside corner. The first complete bar is bar 1 and is not labelled; numbers at the start of each system above the top clef, in italic, unboxed; not every five or ten bars (484). Choral music needs bar numbers only, no rehearsal letters (485).
- **Indentation**: the first system of a piece or movement is indented for full names (486).
- **Casting off** (487–490): sections should start a system (487). Similar numbers of staves per page; facing pages aligned (487–488). **Staves at least one stave-height apart; the distance between systems should be greater than the widest stave gap within the adjacent system** (488). **Bar lines on adjacent systems should not line up**, or the eye skips a system (489). Split a bar across systems only exceptionally, with the bar number bracketed (489–490).
- In score, even spacing goes to the busiest part of each beat (490).
- **Performance instructions** (491–495): general instructions go at the front so they can be read before rehearsal; footnotes use asterisks, not numerals (491). Roman for technical instructions, bold roman for tempo, italic for expression (492). "Do not give an existing symbol a new meaning" (494).
- Square brackets may mark a principal voice, if explained (496). **Ossia**: a small note in brackets on the same stem as the main note (497).

### Ch. 17 Score layout (499–553)
- A score records every instruction in the parts; all materials share reference points such as bar numbers (501). Running heads with section names (501).
- Title page and front matter: composer, title, forces, date, author of the text, kind of score, publisher; then publication details, contents, preface, acknowledgements, first performance, duration, forces, texts, notation conventions, performance notes (501–503). Preliminary pages in lower-case roman, music from page 1 (503).
- **Notation conventions** must be explained; "Do not assume that the musicians will be familiar" with any unconventional notation (504).
- **First page of music**: title centred; composer right, flush with the staves; author of the text left, level with the composer; names usually in small capitals; copyright line at the foot (504).
- An 8 on the clef is optional but identifies octave-transposing parts (506).
- Labels: full names on the first page, then abbreviations; a small ensemble that keeps all staves needs no labels after the first page (507). Label type matches the instructions' type (508).
- Brackets: braces only outermost; square brackets beam-thick with curved ends join each section or choir, even a single stave (514–518).
- **Barring**: bar lines join the same staves as the brackets; "Each vocal stave takes separate barlines, so that barlines do not intersect the text" (518–519). In small ensembles, joined bar lines help where there are four or more single staves, because they mark the system breaks (519).
- Tempo above the top stave; only rehearsal marks and volta brackets above tempo (520). System dividers are not used in choral music (521). Small ensembles keep all staves throughout (522). Cue staves ¾ size (523).
- **Playing score** (538–539): stave size as close to a normal part as possible, at least two systems a page, page-turns planned as for a part, each player's own time signatures and instructions.
- Wind band, brass band, orchestral reduction (539–553): not relevant.

### Ch. 18 Part preparation (555–588)
- **Stave size**: performers at a distance, or two sharing a copy, need larger staves (about 7 mm); "An ideal stave size in good lighting conditions is 6.7 mm" (557).
- Generous space between systems, room to pencil in marks (558). Running head with the part's name; a copyright line in every part (558).
- **Page-turns** (559–561): plan them around the longest rests; a four-second turn; *V.S.*; a turn-back for a short repeat only if there are rests at the start or end of the section; no turns in quiet passages; avoid many players turning at once (561).
- Occasional ledger lines rather than frequent clef changes (562); time signatures on the stave (563).
- **Cues**: ¾ size, stems 2–2½ spaces, condensed spacing (569); vocal cue text smaller or underlined (568).
- Repeats and volta bars must match between score and parts (579). Linear, not vertical, spelling in a part (581).
- Combined parts: bracket and bar the staves together to mark the system breaks (583). Playing scores: never change the order of staves, because each player finds a line by position; bar lines and brackets joining all staves make the system breaks clear (588).

### Ch. 19 Electroacoustic music (589–606)
Read; nothing relevant.

### Ch. 20 Freedom and choice (607–650)
- Bar lines are practical; where there is no metrical stress they may be for orientation only, explained in a preface (609).
- Dotted bar lines inside free passages show independent stresses; solid bar lines are kept for co-ordination points (612). Dotted bar lines also divide sections of alternatives (645).
- Proportional notation and approximate pitch (629–642): not relevant. One frame design, one meaning (644).

### Appendix, Further reading (651–655)
International terms for note values (*breve*, *semibreve*, *minima*, *semiminima*, *croma*: 652–653). Further reading includes Ross (1987) and *New Hart's Rules* for text (655).

## 3. Against our practice

ADOPT = clear gain, low risk. CONSIDER = judgement call for Miki. NOTE = context only.

### Principles 1. Stance
- NOTE. Gould is the opposite pole: the conventions of modern publishing, chosen because they are familiar (xiii). Our principles 1.1–1.5 are about evidence; she is about legibility. Where she and we differ below, the question is always whether a modern singer will misread our sign.

### Principles 2. Two editions, one score
- 2.1 (same score in both). Agree. Gould requires all materials to share reference points (501, 579).
- 2.1 (no piano reduction). Differ. Gould recommends a rehearsal reduction for unaccompanied choral music "wherever possible" (471). Our decision is deliberate (MD). NOTE.
- 2.2 (pitch line in the masthead). Agree in spirit: state the pitch of a transposed score (504).
- 2.3 (suggestions as small notes in round brackets). Agree: this is Gould's ossia sign (497). She puts the ossia note on the same stem as the main note; *Vox in Rama*'s `\suggFinal` already puts it in the same column. NOTE.

### Principles 5. Note values, barring, signs
- **5.2 (bar = breve).** Agree. Gould treats 4/2-length bars as normal and gives the breve rest as their whole-bar rest (160).
- **5.3 (Mensurstriche between the staves, dashed).** Gould has no section on Mensurstriche. Four points bear on them:
  - Support for dashing: in Gould's vocabulary a dotted bar line is a subsidiary division that carries no co-ordination or stress (178, 612). Our light dashed lines say the same thing.
  - Support for lines between staves: in small-ensemble and playing scores, bar lines that join the staves make the system breaks clear (519, 583, 588); and an ossia stave's dotted bar line runs only in the space between the staves (497).
  - Explain it: bar lines without metric function should be explained in a preface (151, 609). Our performance notes do ("The light bar lines run between the staves only ...", *Zmierzka*). Agree.
  - **Differ: the text.** Gould's one firm rule for vocal bar lines is that they must not cross the text: "Each vocal stave takes separate barlines, so that barlines do not intersect the text" (519; also 463). Ours run exactly through the lyric line. In the built editions the dashes touch or cut syllables and extender starts (seen in *Nunc scio* critical pp. 7–8, "Nunc¦", "dis,¦", "ve¦re"; *Vox* performance p. 1, "vox¦"). `pr-knockout-holes` clears notes, stems, accidentals, dots and flags, but not lyrics, and by design only the ends of the dash move, never the middle. **CONSIDER**: open a gap in the dash wherever a `LyricText` (and possibly `LyricHyphen`) lies across it, or find a way to make the lyrics avoid the bar position. This is the most visible point on which the editions break a rule Gould states without qualification.
- **5.4 (mensuration sign).** Agree: a time signature on every stave (152, 465). Do not bracket an editorial sign: in Gould a bracketed time signature already means a metre not shared by all parts (172–173). Our practice (method note, no brackets) is right. NOTE.
- **5.5 (incipits).** Agree in substance (433). Gould's incipit also includes the key signature, and optionally the rests before the first note; her example sets the incipit outside the system bracket with the original voice names over it. CONSIDER only the rests (they show the singer how long the voice waits in the source).
- **5.6 (ambitus).** Gould is silent.
- **5.7 (longs, fermata).** Gould is silent on longs. Fermata design: about 1 × 2 spaces, centred, above the stave (188). NOTE.
- **5.8 (rests).**
  - Grouping by bar: agree with Gould's general rule that rests show the metre (160–161). Keeping a primary source's rests as written in the critical edition is a deliberate difference. NOTE.
  - **Whole-bar rests: differ. ADOPT.** Gould centres a whole-bar rest in its bar (159), and for 4/2 bars uses the breve rest (160). Our performance editions print a breve rest (correct) at the *start* of each empty bar (*Vox* performance p. 1, Bassus bars 1–5), because `Completion_rest_engraver` makes ordinary rests. A centred rest is what every singer reads as "this bar is empty", and it is easier to count.
  - Within a bar, Gould wants rests to expose the middle of a two-beat bar (161). Check what `Completion_rest_engraver` with `completionUnit = 1/1` does with a rest that starts on the second minim (it should print minim + semibreve, not semibreve + minim). Older editions use no dotted rests (161); we should not get any either. CONSIDER (a check, not a change).

### Principles 6. Clefs and voice names
- 6.2 (treble, octave treble, bass). Agree exactly: early-music clefs only for authentic editions or incipits; the treble-8 clef recommended for tenors (433).
- 6.1 (Latin names, small capitals, abbreviated after the first system). Gould's example shows the original names at the incipit and modern names on the staves (433); our 6.3 explains why we keep the source's names. NOTE. She also says labels are unnecessary after the first system when the layout never changes (462, 507). Ours are harmless and help after a page-turn. NOTE.

### Principles 7. Accidentals
- 7.1 (source accidental before the note). Agree; never after (51).
- **7.2 (editorial accidental small above the note).** Agree: one of Gould's two editorial forms (83). But in Gould small-above-the-note is also a form for *cautionary* signs (83). Since we print no cautionaries (7.3), there is no clash inside our editions, provided the performance notes say what the sign means (they do: "A small accidental above the staff is the editor's", *Vox*). NOTE.
- **7.2 (optional accidental, bracketed above).** Differ in convention. In Gould, round brackets on an accidental mean *cautionary*: correct but not strictly needed (83); a bracketed dynamic means "unchanged" (108). A Gould-trained singer may read our (♮) as "this is already natural, just reminding you", which is the opposite of "you may alter". CONSIDER: keep (the convention is common in early-music editions), but make sure every performance edition with an optional sign says in one sentence that a bracketed sign is optional (the *Zmierzka* notes already do).
- **7.3 (accidental style "forget").** Gould accepts the system "an accidental applies only to the note it precedes", on two conditions: it is stated in the preface, and a natural is added "for safety" to an altered pitch repeated straight away (86). The first is met. The second is not: a source sharp followed by the same letter in the same bar prints with no sign, and a modern singer will carry the sharp. **CONSIDER** adding a safety natural in that case only. The difficulty is the sign: a plain natural before the note means a *source* accidental in our scheme, and a bracketed one means *optional*. See question 3.
- 7.4–7.6. Gould would also warn singers of semitone clashes between voices with cautionaries (82–83). We deliberately do not. NOTE.

### Principles 8. Signs for editorial work
Gould's general rule: "Do not give an existing symbol a new meaning" (494); one design, one meaning (644). Each of our signs against what a modern singer has learnt from her conventions:
- *Small accidental above the note*: editorial in Gould too (83). Agree.
- *Small notehead (note supplied by the editor)*: differ. In Gould small notes are cues (¾ size, 569), alternative rhythms for another verse (449), ossia (497) or unsounded reference pitches (139, 144): in every case, notes the reader does **not** sing as written. Our supplied notes must be sung. In the critical edition this is standard editorial practice; in the performance edition a singer may skip them. CONSIDER (question 4).
- *Notehead in square brackets*: agree; square brackets are Gould's editorial bracket (83).
- *Dashed tie (divided source note)*: compatible. Gould's dotted tie means "applies to some verses or languages only" (450–451, 469). In a strophic song a division needed for one stanza is exactly that. NOTE.
- *Horizontal bracket for a ligature*: Gould uses square brackets above the stave for tuplets (194), cross-rhythm groupings (171) and principal voices (496). Ours have no numeral and are explained in the notes. NOTE.
- *Corner brackets for coloration*: no clash. NOTE.
- *Italic text (editorial underlay)*: differ. In Gould italic underlay means a second language, a refrain, or sounds outside the literary text (438, 449). The italic convention for editorial text is standard in early-music editions and Gould does not discuss it. CONSIDER keeping, with the performance notes saying it (question 5).
- *Small note in round brackets (suggestion)*: agree (ossia, 497).

### Principles 9. Repeats
- 9.1 (each repeated section starts and ends on its own system). Agree: a repeat should start a system (234; 487; 240).
- 9.2 (changed bars written into both endings). Agree: any variation needs separate endings or full writing-out (235–236).
- Bar numbers through both endings (5.2). Agree (237).
- NEW, CONSIDER: a repeat must not force a page turn back unless there are rests to turn in (561). With A4 sheets this matters for *Plaude* and *Zmierzka* in the performance edition. Check where the page breaks fall relative to the repeat signs.
- Volta numerals: Gould uses bold figures with a full stop the size of time-signature figures (236); our `VoltaBracket.font-size = -2` makes them smaller. NOTE.

### Principles 10. Underlay
Gould says nothing about which syllable goes on which note; she covers the typography.
- Centred syllables, left-aligned melisma syllables (439): agree; the built editions do this.
- Extenders to the last note, hyphen repeated at the start of a new system (447–448): agree; seen in *Zmierzka* ("‑ my" at bar 6).
- 10.4 (no rest divides a word). Consistent with Gould's treatment of rests inside a syllable (447, 449), which we will rarely need.
- 10.5 (Polish elision where the metre needs it). Gould's sign is an undertie under the text between the syllables (446). ADOPT if not already used: LilyPond's lyric `~` gives it.
- 10.14 (stanzas under the notes where underlay differs). Agree: all verses under one line when rhythms differ only occasionally, otherwise separately (449).
- **Latin word division. ADOPT one system.** Gould allows carry-over (*san-ctus*, *o-mnis*) or divide (*sanc-tus*, *om-nis*), but not a mix (445–446). Our lyrics mostly carry over (*San-cto*, *co-gno*, *re-cti*, *ex-spe* in *Nunc scio vere*; *re-spi*, *de-spi*, *in-spi* in *Plaude euge*), but *Nunc scio vere* also prints *om-ni* three times, so the one edition mixes both systems. Carry-over is the one Gould recommends for melismatic writing (446). Pronunciation is her own reason for allowing a choice (446), and in Polish Latin both consonants of *mn* are sounded, so this is Miki's call (question 6); but the series should pick one and write it into the principles.
- Polish word division: not covered by Gould. Her principles (consistency, word recognition, consonants where they are sung, 441–444) apply. NOTE.
- Repeated text: commas between repetitions, final punctuation at the end, lower-case repetition (438). Our editions do this (*Vox in Rama*). Agree.

### Principles 11. Text
- 11.1–11.2 (modern punctuation for Polish; liturgical-book punctuation for Latin). Differ in principle: Gould keeps the author's capitals and punctuation even against the musical phrasing (438). Her case is an authorial modern text; ours are not. NOTE.
- 11.4 (literal translation). Agree: give a translation alongside an unfamiliar language (437–438).
- 11.5 (pronunciation by comparison with familiar words, no IPA). Agree: Gould offers exactly this for amateurs (451).

### Principles 12. Chant
- Gould is silent (only unmeasured chanting on a monotone, 454). NOTE.

### Principles 13. Paratext
- Critical edition order: agrees closely with Gould's title page and front matter (501–503), including "notation conventions" (504). Gould numbers preliminaries in roman and starts the music at page 1 (503); our short, loose-printed documents do not need this. NOTE.
- **Performance edition**: differ twice. Gould puts the poem before the music (437) and puts general performance instructions "at the front ... so that they can be read in advance of rehearsal" (491). We put both after the score (MD, 3 Oct 2026). NOTE; but see question 7 for a one-line pointer.
- No notes on the music page (house style 9). Gould allows footnotes, marked with asterisks, not numerals (491). NOTE.

### Principles 14. Names
- 14.2 (forces line, "for four voices"). Agree: give the number of voices (462).

### House style 2. Page
- A4, portrait, margins 17–23 mm. Agree (462, 481–482).
- **Staff size.** Performance 19 pt = 6.7 mm; Gould's ideal in good light is 6.7 mm (557). Exact agreement. Critical 17 pt = 6.0 mm (Rastral 5), larger than Gould's choral range (4.8–5.5 mm, 483). Agree; too small is the danger (482).

### House style 3. Type
- Lyrics roman (438): agree. Bar numbers italic (484): agree. Bold unused: Gould uses bold only for tempo, rehearsal marks and volta numbers, which we do not have (182, 236, 484). NOTE.
- **Lyric size**: measured in *Nunc scio vere* (critical), Junicode's x-height is 1.09 stave-spaces (cap height 1.7). Gould wants about 1 (438). Agree.

### House style 5a. Finish
- CONSIDER. Gould warns that the white inside of minims and semibreves, and the thin white wedges between beams and stave-lines, "fill in" under poor reproduction or at small sizes (11, 17, 20). The Novello finish adds ink and rounds corners. Check at 100% and on a photocopy that white heads stay open and that ledger lines stay distinct from stave lines.

### House style 7. Headings and breaks
- **Spacing of systems: differ. ADOPT a check.** Gould: staves at least a stave-height apart, and the gap between systems larger than the widest stave gap inside the neighbouring system (488). Measured on the built PDFs (staff line to staff line, in stave-spaces):
  - All critical editions keep stave gaps of 5–11 spaces (the stave-height rule is met everywhere).
  - The system gap is often **not** larger than the widest stave gap. *Vox in Rama* performance: p. 1 system gap 7.6 against 10.4 in the next system; p. 2 9.6 against 10.0 and 12.0; p. 3 9.9 against 10.6. *Już się zmierzka* performance p. 1: 12.6 against 13.3 and 14.3 (two lyric lines under each stave). *Nunc scio vere* performance p. 5: 9.5 against 9.7. *Vox* critical p. 8: 8.6 against 9.6; p. 9: 10.5 against 10.7. *Plaude euge* and *Zmierzka* critical pass clearly.
  The eye then has nothing to tell it where a system ends except the bracket. A higher `system-system-spacing` padding in the performance setting, or a post-build check, would fix it.
- Bar lines on adjacent systems should not line up (489). With breve bars of similar content the dashed lines can stack vertically from system to system. CONSIDER as a check.
- New sections on a new system (487): agree (break at verse lines, house style 1).
- Page-turns (559–561): see 9 above.

### House style 9. Score
- Square bracket for the choir, systemic bar line at the left (463, 516, 521): agree.
- Incipit, clef, ambitus, mensuration sign: see 5.5, 6.2. Gould's spacing at the start of a system (clef, key and time signature 1–1½ spaces apart, 2–3 before the first note, 41–42) is worth checking now that the ambitus sits between them. CONSIDER.
- Bar numbers in small italic at the start of each system only, above the clef (484): agree.
- Ties solid, dashed only for `\divTie`: agree (60–65; dashed tie see 8).
- Rubrics red italic above the system: Gould puts stage directions and rubric-like text at the top of the system (470). Agree.
- Poet left, composer right on the first page of music: agree (504). Gould sets both names in small capitals; ours sets the poet in roman (*Zmierzka* performance). CONSIDER.
- Stanzas after the score: agree (449, verses set out separately when they cannot all go under the notes).
- Beaming: our scores beam fusae within melismas and use no syllabic slurs, i.e. the traditional vocal convention Gould describes (435). She prefers instrumental beaming plus syllabic slurs. Our extenders already show every melisma, and slurs would compete with ligature brackets and dashed ties. NOTE (question 11).

### House style 11. Language
- Gould is silent on prose style. NOTE.

## 5. Open questions for Miki

1. **Mensurstriche and the text.** Gould's one rule for vocal bar lines is that they never cross the text (519). Ours do. Break the dash around syllables, or accept the collision as the price of bar lines between the staves?
2. **Whole-bar rests.** Centre them (Gould 159) in the performance edition only, or in both editions where the voice is regularised?
3. **Safety naturals under "forget".** Gould wants a natural on an altered pitch repeated in the same bar (86). Do we add one? If so, with which sign, given that a plain sign before the note means "source" and a bracketed one means "optional" in our scheme?
4. **Small noteheads for supplied notes.** A modern singer reads small notes as cues or alternatives (449, 497, 569). Keep them in the performance edition with a sentence in the notes, or use another sign there (the critical edition can keep them)?
5. **Italic underlay.** Gould's italic text means a second language or a refrain (449). Keep italic for editorial text (standard in early-music editions), and say so in each performance edition?
6. **Latin syllables.** Carry-over (*o-mni*, *san-cto*) throughout, as most of our lyrics already do, or divide (*om-ni*, *sanc-to*) to match Polish Latin pronunciation? Either way, one rule for the series.
7. **Performance notes before the music.** Gould puts the text and the performance instructions in front (437, 491). We decided against context before the music. Would one line under the masthead pointing to "Reading the score" on the last page be acceptable, or is that already too much?
8. **System spacing.** Raise the space between systems in the performance editions so that it always exceeds the stave gaps (488)? It may cost a system on some pages.
9. **Poet's name in small capitals** on the first page of music, matching the composer (504)?
10. **Page-turns and repeats.** Should the per-score page breaks also avoid a turn-back inside a repeat (561)?
11. **Syllabic slurs.** Gould would add them (435); I would not (they would compete with ligature brackets and dashed ties, and the extenders already show the melismas). Agree to leave them out?
