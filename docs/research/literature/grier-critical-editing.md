# Grier, *The Critical Editing of Music*: reading notes

## 1. Reference, scope, authority

James Grier, *The Critical Editing of Music: History, Method, and Practice* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1996). Read in full, from the preface to the appendices; the bibliography and index were checked for leads. Page numbers are the book's own. The text was read in OCR and checked against the page images where the wording mattered (pp. 163–4, 172–3).

Grier was a Yale musicologist who worked on medieval Aquitanian sources. His own edition of the music in Adémar de Chabannes's hand is the book's running example. The book has no rival in English: it is the one general theory of music editing, built on classical stemmatics (Lachmann, Maas, Timpanaro), on Anglo-American copy-text theory (Greg, Bowers, Tanselle) and above all on McGann's view of the work as a social and historical object. Its main thesis is that "editing is an act of criticism" (p. xiii). Grier refuses to give a single prescriptive method (pp. 7, 18, 36). Each piece, source and edition is "a special case" (p. 19). His recommendations on presentation are offered as his own preferences among the options (p. 156).

Limits for us. The worked examples are mostly twelfth-century Aquitanian *uersus*, Bach, Haydn, Mozart, Schubert, Schoenberg and Verdi. Renaissance polyphony appears only through Josquin (*Domine, ne in furore*, *Missa Malheur me bat*), Petrucci and the Ogni Sorte editions. Nothing is Polish or Central European, apart from a citation of Mirosław Perz on text underlay in Polish sources (p. 134 n. 61). On text underlay Grier only points to the theorists (pp. 134, 166); our Towne/Harrán and Stoquerus notes go much further. Engraving gets general principles, not measurements. The book gives almost no numbers in staff spaces or points; the few figures it does give are recorded below. It dates from 1996: the chapter on electronic editions (pp. 177–9) imagines screens and foot switches, not encoded data.

## 2. Chapter by chapter

### Preface (pp. xiii–xiv)
- Editing is criticism. The term *Urtext* shows the wish for a neutral edition, but even Henle and Feder admit that the editor has to intervene (p. xiii).
- McGann's view that a work takes much of its meaning from its "socialization" suits music, where that process happens in public through performance (pp. xiii–xiv).

### 1. Introduction: the task of the editor (pp. 1–37)
- **The Hammerklavier crux** (bars 224–6: a♯ or a♮). The sources cannot settle it, and even the lost autograph would not. The editor or the pianist must choose, and the choice is theirs, not Beethoven's. "Editors are always free … to express their uncertainty", but performers cannot have it both ways (pp. 1–2).
- Editing balances the composer's authority, carried by the sources, against the editor's authority in judging them (p. 3). Bent: a good edition is an act of criticism; many "critical" editions are neither critical nor interesting (pp. 3–4). Brett: state your critical assumptions, and stop calling any edition "definitive" (p. 4).
- **"Obvious errors are silently corrected": "Obvious to whom?"** (p. 8). Users need to be told unequivocally which readings are editorial, and why one reading was preferred (p. 8).
- Five questions organise the book: the sources' nature and history; their relation to one another; what they tell us about the work; how this shapes the edited text; how best to present it (p. 5).
- Four principles: editing is criticism; criticism is historical; editing examines the *semiotic* import of notation, historically; the final arbiter is the editor's historically grounded sense of style (pp. 8, 36).
- Discourse survey (pp. 10–15). Performers' editions obscured the source; *Urtext* answered them, but an Urtext that later scholarship supersedes is no longer an Urtext (pp. 10–12). Post-war Collected Editions dropped some "philological purity", old clefs for instance, for performers' sake (p. 12). English-language early-music editors put presentation before criticism (Dart, Emery; p. 13). Caldwell and H. M. Brown are good on mechanics but brief on criticism (p. 14). Mendel and Brett: performers now take responsibility for performance details, so an editor who fixes definitive solutions for them "obstructs, rather than assists" (p. 14). Feder's *Musikphilologie* is the one full treatment, but it splits "lower" and "higher" criticism and aims at final intentions, and Grier rejects both moves (pp. 14–15).
- McGann. Replace "final authorial intention" with history: each source attests a historical state of the work, and each reading is a piece of evidence for the work's history (pp. 16–19). Examples: Joachim's part in Brahms's Violin Concerto, and Schumann's commercial revision of Op. 52 (pp. 17–19).
- **Work and text.** A score is a set of instructions to a performer; the work lives in score and performance conventions together; a text is one state of the work (pp. 20–23). A notational sign has meaning only through context and convention (Example 1-1, pp. 24–5). Tempo by note values (Corelli) against Beethoven's opposite convention (pp. 25–6). In mensural notation triple division comes from the mensuration and the dot is not obligatory, unlike in modern notation (p. 26). The editor must reconstruct the conventions within which the text was fixed (p. 27).
- **Style and the three categories of reading** (pp. 28–36). Every reading is (1) good, (2) a reasonable competing reading, or (3) a clear error, judged by style (p. 30). Style comes from the same texts, so the argument is circular (Feder's "hermeneutic circle"). Break in through "parallel passages" from works close in "time, place, genre and function" (p. 30). All readings are good until style shows them false. **"There is no such thing as an 'obvious error'"**: a *clear* error must be explained, an "obvious" one supposedly needs no explanation (p. 31). Unanimity of the witnesses is not enough: a composer can redefine style "with a single note" (p. 32). Examples: parallel motion in *Omnis curet homo* is a probable copying error (Ex. 1-2), but in *Senescente mundano filio* similar parallels are part of the style (Ex. 1-3) (pp. 32–5). In Bach's Bourrée II (BWV 1009), bar 4, a♭ and a♮ are both stylistically possible: a category-2 case that no rule can decide (p. 35).
- An editor who keeps a preconceived idea of the piece through the whole edition produces an edition of that idea, not of the evidence (pp. 36–7).

### 2. The nature of the musical source (pp. 38–61)
- Every source is both a historical document and a repository of readings. Authenticating a source does not authenticate each of its readings (Eisen's Salzburg Mozart copies, pp. 38–9).
- Music sources are mostly practical, made for performance and thrown away when obsolete, which is why fragments survive in bindings (p. 40). Copyists translate notation into their own conventions. Professional scribes "replace manifest corruptions with feasible readings" (pp. 41–6). *Semiotic equivalents*: two notations can mean the same thing (Ex. 2-1, 2-2), and the same notation can mean different things in different contexts (Anna Magdalena Bach's slurs, pp. 42–5).
- Dating and provenance change a source's weight without improving its readings (W1, F; pp. 46–8).
- **Collecting data** has four stages: location, inspection, description, transcription (p. 49). Work from photographs first, then check against the original. Photographs never show everything (pp. 49, 55).
- *Recentiores non deteriores* (Pasquali): a late or "worse" source can preserve a lost one. Diabelli's mutilated print of Schubert's D. 505 alone has one bar missing elsewhere (p. 52).
- Chant variants belong to regions and orders. A restricted study is worth more than a superficial survey of every source. The Solesmes books never named their sources (pp. 52–3).
- **Prints**: copies of one impression can differ (stop-press corrections, worn plates, shifted type). Collate several copies where possible; the point of diminishing returns is a matter of judgement (pp. 53–4).
- **Description**: the minimum is positive identification, by city, library and shelfmark. "The autograph in Berlin" will not do. For prints before 1800, cite exactly which copies were consulted, normally more than one (pp. 55–6). Summarise non-musical parts of a miscellany briefly (p. 56).
- **Transcription.** Grier misread a pin-prick as a note on microfilm; Anglés transcribed a bleed-through initial as notes (p. 57). Make "a meticulous list of readings to be checked" in situ (p. 57). "No transcription is objective" (p. 58). For collating near-contemporary sources, keep the original symbols. For collating tablature against mensural sources, reduce both to modern notation and check back later (pp. 58–9).
- Other evidence: theorists' citations, independent sources of the literary text (Monteverdi libretti), letters, paintings, and for twentieth-century music recordings (pp. 60–1).

### 3. Musical sources and stemmatic filiation (pp. 62–95)
- Lachmann's method. Category 1 readings go straight into the text; category 3 errors are corrected *ope codicum* or *ope ingenii*; category 2 is what the stemma is for (pp. 62–3).
- **Only clear errors show filiation.** Shared good readings, however rare, prove nothing, since they all descend from the original (pp. 63–4; again p. 87). Statistical methods (Quentin, Greg, Dearing) fail because they count agreement in correct readings (p. 66). Kane and Donaldson use category-2 readings, which Grier rejects (pp. 66–7).
- *Eliminatio codicum descriptorum* (p. 64). Bédier's objection to bipartite stemmata led to the "best-text" method: one witness, corrected only where manifestly corrupt (pp. 64–5). A stemma is an interpretation built on interpretation (p. 65).
- **Living traditions**: where performers and scribes legitimately vary the text, the stemma still serves, and it "flows both ways". It can reconstruct the archetype, but it can also isolate the distinctive variants of each witness and show when they entered the tradition (pp. 68–9).
- Multi-item sources need a stemma per piece (p. 70). Practical sources make **substantive alterations**: an inner voice simplified or dropped, an exposed voice elaborated, movements swapped, "transposition to a more comfortable key", instruments replaced (pp. 70–1). These are not errors (Fig. 3-1, *Omnis curet homo*'s added second voice).
- Scribal conjectural emendation is invisible when it succeeds (p. 72). Scribes correct chant from memory (Pa 1119 against its exemplar Pa 909, Ex. 3-1, pp. 73–4). Contamination (pp. 74–5). Errors in the literary text count as evidence where words and music travelled together (p. 75). Documentary and physical evidence: the Linz Symphony's Donaueschingen parts show errors of intrusion (an eye slipping to the wrong staff) and shared errors in pairs of parts, proving that they were copied from a faulty score (pp. 75–6).
- **Conjunctive and separative errors** (pp. 77–8). A conjunctive error must be unlikely to arise twice independently. A separative error must be one a scribe could not have corrected by conjecture or contamination. "The absence of either type of error does not permit any stemmatic deduction" (p. 78). Lost witnesses take Greek letters, the archetype Ω (pp. 78–9).
- Publish the errors used to build the stemma, with the reasoning (p. 79). How many are needed depends on length: "in shorter pieces even a single significative error" can be enough (p. 79).
- **On textual evidence alone, never eliminate a witness and never split more than two ways.** Both rest on an argument from silence and can raise unnoticed errors to the archetype (pp. 79–82). Such a stemma is a *stemma textuum*, bipartite throughout (p. 82). Historical, physical or geographical evidence can prove a multipartite split or a *descriptus* (pp. 90–1). Example: Adémar's deliberate dittography *mortis mortis* at a gathering join, copied into Pa 1119. Even a proven *descriptus* may contain good emendations (p. 91).
- Worked stemmata: Bach's cello suites (ACD share errors, so Eppstein's tripartite stemma is doubtful; pp. 82–7); *Veri solis radius* (p. 87); *Omnis curet homo*, where shared non-error features (the polyphonic version, the treatment of sequence) also show descent: "readings that are not clear errors can nevertheless show shared descent" (p. 88); *Ex Ade uitio*, where an unprovable tripartite split is rejected and the unplaced witness's readings carry no weight (pp. 89–90).
- Reconstruction: agreements eliminate, and the rest goes to *examinatio* (pp. 91–2). A stemma with one witness on its own branch makes that witness "virtually the best witness" (pp. 92–3). Check for contamination and emendation by asking whether a scribe would have had several exemplars, and whether easy errors were left uncorrected (p. 93).
- Results: Hoffmann-Erbrecht's Stoltzer edition (the first to use stemmata), and regional traditions of Josquin motets (pp. 94–5). The stemma is a tool, "only as good … as the readings on which it is based" (p. 95).

### 4. Errors, variants and editorial judgement (pp. 96–143)
- The dangers: to improve the composer, to impose an arbitrary stylistic standard, or to follow the sources or earlier editors "without revision or critical comment" (p. 96). The evidence is not neutral: it was shaped by choices made while collecting it (p. 96). Adémar's Introit doxology: the tonary's formula was preferred to the written-out verse's (pp. 96–8).
- With a single source, category 2 shrinks but does not vanish (p. 98).
- Theories weighed (pp. 98–109). Recension is a tool, not a method (p. 99). Eclectic text against best text. For a living tradition, edit "each version of each song separately", each "its own best text", and emend its errors with the stemma's help (pp. 102–4). The editor assumes only that a scribe *meant* to copy correctly, not what the scribe intended musically (p. 103). **Copy-text** (Greg's split into substantives and accidentals) fails in music because every graphic feature can carry meaning. Transcribing a C-clef tenor into octave treble loses the information about which voice range was meant (pp. 105–7). No a priori preference between autograph and first edition (p. 108). The historical, social approach is "an anti-method"; of the theories, "best text" is the most useful (pp. 108–9).
- Sources from the composer (pp. 109–16): sketches rarely give readings; autograph layers; revisions made under publishers' pressure still count (Schumann Op. 14); apographs (authenticating the copy does not authenticate unchanged readings, p. 115); performing materials, where performers' markings are separated from substantive changes (Schubert's Mass in F, pp. 115–16).
- Sources not from the composer (pp. 116–21). Written-out ornaments record reception, not a fixed text (Corelli Op. 5, pp. 116–18). **"Almost everything up to 1600"** survives only in such sources. Ask why the source was made and at what remove it stands (two Josquin sources from Rome and Rostock, pp. 118–19). Scribal freedom varies inversely with the precision of the notation (p. 119). Scribes may write down a "virtual" performance. Each surviving version "potentially possesses equal validity" (pp. 120–1).
- Stemma in practice: Josquin's *Domine, ne in furore* splits into Italian and German traditions, and one of them has swapped alto and tenor in bars 142–6 (Ex. 4-5). Smijers follows Petrucci where the two traditions weigh equally (pp. 121–2). Haydn Op. 33: Feder and Gerlach's tripartite stemma does not explain several shared errors (pp. 122–7).
- Eclecticism in practice: Eppstein picks single readings against his own stemma; Curtis's *Poppea* (pp. 129–30).
- **Theorists**: tell descriptive statements from prescriptive ones. Marchetto rewrote a chant to fit modal theory, and no source has his version (pp. 130–2). Composer-theorists (Rameau, Hindemith), and Fux's influence (pp. 132–4). **Underlay**: theorists' prescriptions are "especially valuable" here, since the sources say little and an edition meant for performance must give a usable scheme; "simply reproducing the source will not suffice". A facsimile of a page or two shows a source's underlay habits better than a diplomatic transcription. Apply only theory contemporary with the repertory or otherwise suited to it (p. 134).
- **Conjectural emendation**: Besseler's correction of Bach's parallel octaves in Brandenburg 3 and 4 follows Bach's own correction of the parallel bar. It gives a stylistically acceptable reading, not necessarily Bach's (pp. 135–6). The opposite danger: "printing the piece the composer would have written had he or she known as much as the editor". Monod's serial "corrections" to *A Survivor from Warsaw* recompose the piece (pp. 136–9).
- **Literary text**: edit to "the highest philological standards". Many editions get transcription, spelling, capitalisation, punctuation and syllabification wrong. Consult or collaborate with philologists (p. 139).
- Editors should be "scientific in their methodology and humanistic in its application" (pp. 139, 142). Style is the arbiter, and knowledge alone does not make for a sense of it (pp. 142–3).

### 5. The presentation of the text (pp. 144–79)
**Types** (pp. 144–55). Feder lists eight types; Grier reduces them to four: facsimile, printed replica of the original notation, critical edition, interpretative edition (p. 145).
- *Facsimile*: photographs vary with the photographer; enhancement can create marks. Editors should add folio numbers where the source's are unclear, a table of contents, and an introduction naming the exact copy photographed (with a page-by-page table if more than one), the reduction and the use of colour (pp. 145–8).
- *Printed replica*: a critical edition in a regularised version of the original notation, usually best-text. The Ogni Sorte Editions give Petrucci-style parts with a modern score; the differences from the source are "purely calligraphic" (pp. 148–50). Parts focus the eye on the melodic line, "arguably the most important aspect" of these repertories (p. 150). Simultaneous facsimile and transcription is costly (p. 150). Put the list of differences from the base source at the back, not at the foot of the page (p. 151).
- *Interpretative edition* (performers' editions): legitimate, and a record of performing tradition (p. 151). The fault of the old ones was an unfaithful text and silent rewriting (Rose's Haydn concerto with Gevaert's cuts; pp. 151–3). Performing indications are within the performer's discretion. Changing the notes is "arranging" and must be reported (p. 153). If the introduction says what kind of marks were added, they need not be distinguished graphically (Starker's Bach, p. 153). No apparatus is needed unless the text was changed in more than performing indications; then add a list at the end or in the introduction, and name the base text and the critical edition (p. 154). **Commentary at the foot of music pages is impractical**: Schnabel's Op. 111 first movement needs 8 page-turns against Schenker's 4 in the same format, and some pages hold only 2 systems against 5 (p. 154 n. 28–9; pp. 154–5).

**Critical edition** (pp. 155–76).
- Audience: performers, students, scholars, the musically literate public. Purpose: to give "the text that best represents the historical evidence of the sources" (p. 156). Clarity first. Keep archaic notation only where fidelity outweighs ease of reading. The facsimile and the replica make archaism unnecessary in a critical edition, provided the principles of modernisation are set out (pp. 156–7).
- **Only the text on the music pages.** This keeps page-turns down, and a clean text can be photo-reproduced as a study score or as the base of an interpretative edition (Bärenreiter study scores; pp. 157–8). The apparatus goes before or after the text, ideally in a separate volume, so that text and commentary can be read side by side while the text stays usable in performance (p. 158).
- First music page: full title from the sources, composer (speculative attributions marked), composer's dates, date and place of the work, editor; copyright at the foot. This also discourages unattributed photocopying (p. 159). Score order follows modern convention; show the source's own layout with a facsimile of its first page in the introduction (p. 159). The first system is complete and every staff named (p. 160).
- **Clefs**: three modern clefs (treble, bass, octave treble "always printed with the numeral 8" below). Report the original clefs in the introduction. Among clefs, choose the one with the fewest leger lines (p. 161).
- **Signatures**: follow the sources; modernise with caution. An editorial signature can spare a score many editorial accidentals: Grier gave Adémar's Gloria a one-flat signature with a full discussion (pp. 162–3).
- **Accidentals** (pp. 163–4). Give a clear convention for which inflections are in the source and which are the editor's. Duration: in a barred edition, the modern bar rule; without barlines, the sign affects "the note it precedes and those immediately repeated at the same pitch". Source accidentals go on the staff. In early vocal music, editorial ones go **above the staff, over the note**; they apply to that note "and immediately succeeding notes on the same pitch" and "need not be cancelled". *Musica ficta* is the main source of them. Leaving ficta unmarked suits only replica editions; non-specialists need guidance (p. 163). After about 1600 and in instrumental music, editorial accidentals mostly correct or clarify, so they belong on the staff (pp. 163–4). **"Courtesy" accidentals** wherever there is "the slightest chance of ambiguity" (p. 164). Caldwell's four levels of accidental are too complex to take in while reading or performing (p. 164 n. 38).
- **Time signatures**: no modern time signature for mensural music. Give the mensuration sign, and state any reduction (pp. 164–5).
- **Mensurstriche**: short lines between the staves, "usually at the end of each breve". They avoid ties and accentual barlines while keeping the parts aligned (p. 165); critics are Lowinsky, Mendel and Caldwell (n. 40). **At the end of a system**, "it is useless" to look for a point where every voice ends a note. Fill the line with a value and tie it to the remainder on the next system; printing the full value and leaving blank space at the start of the next line is "visually disruptive" (p. 165). Sixteenth-century scores often have barlines through the system at every breve, so normal barlines are also defensible. Grier calls the Mensurstrich "a creative solution" to avoiding metrical stress (pp. 165–6).
- **Spacing**: proportional spacing (four quavers take the room of two crotchets) is "highly desirable" where the range of values is narrow (p. 166).
- **Lyrics** (pp. 166–7). Defend the underlay from contemporary theory and the secondary literature. A single-note syllable is centred under its note; in a melisma, the syllable's first letter aligns with the first note. Hyphens between all syllables, spaced out repeatedly through an internal melisma. A final syllable's melisma takes an extender line, with punctuation between the syllable and the line. Set the text low enough that the extender is visibly further from the bottom staff line than the staff lines are from each other. In vocal music, put dynamics above the staff.
- Beams: before 1900, texted notes are unbeamed and a melisma is shown by beam plus slur (p. 167). In non-metrical music, beam groups can suggest a false metre (pp. 167–8).
- **Marking editorial work** (pp. 168–9). Marking it shows at a glance what is editorial, but no system is uniform and the marks clutter the page. The philologists' brackets are confusing. Square brackets for deletions are pointless: a performer needs to know what to play, and deletions belong in the apparatus. If editors mark at all, use the quotation convention (square brackets = editorial addition), as the NMA does. **Grier's own preference is to mark nothing** and enter every intervention in the critical report, keeping the page clean for reading and reproduction. "The text of any critical edition, however, is the editor's text"; an editor who states the policy and applies it consistently cannot be accused of misleading (p. 169).
- **Early music** (pp. 169–72). Order the voices by range. Where ranges are equal, go by the part's name (contratenor above tenor), first or last note, or behaviour at cadences. Keep the source's part names and explain archaic ones (pp. 169–70). **Reduction**: no consensus. Pick a reduction that gives the tactus "a reasonable value", a beat on the half, quarter or eighth note, sensitive to the source's palaeography and the piece's rhythmic style, and state it at the top of the score (p. 170). **Preliminary staff**: original clef, signature, mensuration sign and "first few notes". It shows the reduction and the clef displacement at a glance and can stand in for a facsimile (pp. 170–1). **Proportions**: an equation with arrows showing which value belongs to which passage, placed before the first affected note (p. 171). **Brackets**: ligature a continuous horizontal bracket; *coniunctura* a broken bracket; coloration "a framing bracket with no line between". In unmeasured music a ligature may be shown by a slur. **Liquescents**: in unmeasured music a smaller note slurred to its main note; in measured music a full-size note with an oblique slash through the stem, inside the ligature bracket if ligated (p. 172).
- **Apparatus** (pp. 172–4): after the text, preferably in its own volume. Content, in order: (1) every emendation, with the rejected source readings; (2) category-2 readings with their sources. A source with many attractive rejected readings may deserve its own edition (p. 173). Do not fill the apparatus with orthographic trivia; a facsimile does that better. Its purpose is to report readings that "affected the editor's deliberations" (p. 173). Model: the Neue Schubert-Ausgabe, with a selective apparatus in the volume and the full report deposited in libraries (p. 173). Entry: part and bar, siglum, variant. **Give variants in musical notation**, not "a string of alpha-numeric characters". A short, well-chosen notated list is better than a long coded one (pp. 173–4).
- **Commentary** "perhaps the most important part of the edition", and often missing or reduced to a list of variants. No subject is off limits. General questions (ficta, performance practice, tempo, breathing) go in appendices (p. 174).
- **Order** (p. 175): introduction (historical context, a paragraph or two placing the piece); description and classification of the sources, with a sample facsimile page; editorial method and the editor's point of view; what the edition adds to earlier ones. Then the text; then optional or alternative sections; then apparatus, commentary and appendices; a bibliography, preferably with brief comments on each item's merits; an index (pp. 175–6).
- **Performing materials**: page-turns at the end of a movement, or at a bar's rest or more at the foot of the recto; "V.S." is no excuse. Break up long rests with rehearsal letters; give cues after long rests; mark *tacet* (p. 176). A keyboard reduction is in effect a recomposition (pp. 176–7).
- **Electronic** (pp. 177–9): windows for variant versions, apparatus and commentary; a performer's layer of markings in another colour that can be removed with one command; it "should not be possible to delete or alter" the edited text itself.

### 6. Conclusion: the posture of the editor (pp. 180–3)
- There is no objectivity. Two failures: present all the ambiguous evidence and leave the user to decide ("sheer dereliction of duty"), or resolve every question with false finality. Instead, adopt a critical attitude, "clearly expressed … and, most important, consistently applied" (p. 180).
- An "omnibus edition" with every reading is not critical in itself; facsimiles may serve better (p. 181). "Critical editions should generate critical users" (p. 181). A hypothesis offered as speculation is worth more than an admission that the truth cannot be known (p. 182). Editions should be usable as students' first introduction to a repertory (p. 182).

### 7. Epilogue: three editions (pp. 184–213)
**Adémar** (Grier's own edition, Corpus Christianorum; pp. 184–99).
- The series and its audience of medievalists shaped every choice (pp. 185–6). Principal source Pa 909, Adémar's autograph. Pa 1121 is his "second edition" of the sequentiary; its unica are added as an appendix (pp. 186–9). Contemporary Saint-Martial books are consulted for context and for spoken texts, not to build a definitive Aquitanian reading (pp. 189–91).
- Unclefed (heighted) neumes. Starting pitch from concordances in Mo H. 159; for tropes, work back from the host chant; else from Adémar's mode numerals, working back from the final; else by modal analysis. One antiphon (*Aue pastor*) could be mode 2 or mode 8 with B♭; Grier chose mode 2 and explained the doubt in a note (pp. 191–2).
- A "conservative, if critical" stance: he wanted "overwhelming evidence" before calling an Adémar variant an error, and would rather keep an idiosyncratic reading that cannot be proven wrong than homogenise the tradition (p. 193). A real error: a passage written a step too low, where the whole Aquitanian tradition agrees on the other reading and the melodic shape is unchanged, so it is a heighting slip (p. 193). Later sources that depend on Adémar's are not independent witnesses (p. 193).
- Everything is printed complete as sung, with cues expanded from the nearest sources, so that non-specialists need not reconstruct the piece (pp. 194, 196). Where Adémar's own version cannot be reached, the witness closest in time and place is the base. His corrections are "my conclusion … not a proven or provable fact" (p. 195).
- Presentation (pp. 195–9). The literary text is printed twice, under the music and separately *after* the music "so that the music would not seem secondary". A detailed commentary for non-specialists. Octave treble clef throughout, to match earlier editions and avoid leger lines. Modern stemless noteheads; facsimiles show the relation to the originals. The literary-text apparatus is at the foot of its pages; the music apparatus sits separately, between the text edition and the commentary. All variants are given from the witnesses Adémar knew or that were copied from him, and a selection from the others; an asterisk marks variants discussed in the commentary; notes are keyed by lemma. Aural spellings (*iessit* for *gessit*) are read as signs of oral transmission (p. 198).

**Mozart, Linz Symphony, ed. Eisen (Peters 1992)** (pp. 200–5). A new edition must state clearly how it differs from the standard one (the NMA), and readers must be able to find the main differences quickly (p. 200). Eisen has 12 footnotes and a critical commentary that discusses only 4 readings. The case for the edition sits in a journal article, so "the edition can explain itself" fails (pp. 201–2, 205). Grier disputes one adopted reading as stylistically implausible (p. 202). The bassoon differences between the two sets of parts probably reflect performers adapting to circumstances. They are neither compositional fiat nor scribal error, a third source of variants (pp. 204–5).

**Verdi, *Don Carlos*, ed. Günther (Ricordi 1980)** (pp. 206–13). Seven versions are documented but hard to follow. Grier asks for a seven-column synoptic table with inclusive page numbers and consistent "go to" footnotes for every version (pp. 208–11), and for alternative versions printed by page with skip directions, not duplicated (Table 7-2, pp. 211–12). The sources must be identified with shelfmarks; that they are not is "inexcusable" for previously unpublished music (p. 212).

### Appendices (pp. 214–32)
- **A. Location**: RISM A/I, B/I and B/IV; library catalogues; Kristeller; *Scriptorium*'s bibliography indexed by shelfmark (pp. 214–18).
- **B. Inspection**: terms. *Manuscript*, *autograph*, *apograph*, *codex*; parchment and paper; watermarks; dry-point and rastrum ruling (pp. 219–21). Foliation: prefer "fol. 8r" / "8v", "fol. 8r–v" for both sides, "fols. 12ra–18vb" for columns (p. 221). *Opening*, *bifolium*, *gathering* (avoid "quire"), *binio*…*quinio*, folio/quarto/octavo as folding, *signature*, *catchword*, *custos*, *libellus*, *rubric*, *incipit*, *explicit*, *colophon*, flyleaves, paste-downs (pp. 221–4). Checklist: binding, owners' marks, title page and shelfmark of the copy used, plate and publisher's numbers, collation of gatherings, writing frame (binders cannot alter it), watermarks, hands and inks, notation, inventory with incipits (pp. 224–6).
- **C. Description templates.** Manuscript, 26 fields: siglum; location; shelfmark; contents; date; copyists; ownership and origin; notation; folios; collation; libelli; page layout; ruling; staves; systems; watermarks; paper maker; pecia and catchwords; decoration; binding; then per item composer, title, incipit and folios, editions, bibliography; bibliography of the codex. Print, 20 fields: siglum *and RISM siglum*; "complete transcription of title page, showing line divisions"; publisher's and plate numbers; date; **copies consulted with location and shelfmark**; notation; collation; layout; staves; watermarks; decoration; binding; special features of each copy; contents; bibliography. A clear layout matters; if in doubt, err on the side of more information (pp. 227–9).
- **D. Transcription.** Record clefs, signatures, accidentals, mensuration and proportion signs, coloration, and the underlay exactly as written (spelling, capitals, punctuation); line and leaf ends; custodes (p. 230). Irregularities, with abbreviations reusable in the apparatus: *add.*, *in marg.*, *b.l.*, *a.l.*, *h¹ h² h³*, *in eras.*, *eras.*, *canc.*, *exp.*, *ink¹ ink² ink³*, *om.* (p. 230). Two collation methods. Marking differences on a master copy is fast but tempts one to rationalise small differences away. Grier prefers a **grid**: transcribe the base text along the top of each recto, divided into units (for mensural music, by tempus or prolation, with Mensurstrich-like lines between the staves, not through them); give each further source its own line; write out the whole unit wherever it differs; mark omissions *om.*; use the facing verso for additions. Up to about six staves per grid; split larger scores by section of the orchestra. Keep a list of details to check in the original (pp. 231–2).

## 3. Against our practice

Each point gives what we do, what Grier says, our relation (agree / differ / silent), and a mark: **ADOPT** (clear gain, low risk), **CONSIDER** (Miki's call) or **NOTE** (context only).

### Principles §1. Stance
- **1.1 First-degree source.** Agree. Work from photographs, then check the original; photographs never show everything (pp. 49, 55, 57, 146). Grier treats a facsimile as a separate witness with its own distortions (p. 146), which fits our handling of *Zmierzka*'s redrawn F. **NOTE**: unseen originals behind facsimiles (the MMP D/12 Tenor, read "in a scan" of a facsimile) are the case for Grier's in-situ checklist (pp. 57, 232); see the project's archive-visit list.
- **1.2 Distrust critical editions, this one included.** Agree strongly. No edition is definitive (pp. 6, 36, 181–2). The text of a critical edition "is the editor's text" (p. 169). Following earlier editors "without revision or critical comment" is one of the three dangers (p. 96).
- **1.3 Modern editions are not evidence of habits.** Agree for stylistic statistics: build the style from works close "in time, place, genre and function" (p. 30). Qualification, *recentiores non deteriores* (pp. 52, 117–18): a modern edition made while a now-lost source was complete is a real witness to that source, filtered by its editor. For *Vox in Rama*, WDMP 12 (1933, "from the print of 1611" while the partbooks were complete) is therefore a witness to the lost Altus and Bassus, not only to Chybiński's taste. **NOTE**: this raises WDMP 12 and the Jachimecki/Szczepańska transcriptions to top priority among editions to obtain.
- **1.4 The music decides when the evidence runs out.** Agree: style is the final arbiter (pp. 8, 36, 142). Grier sets two guards on it. Do not "improve the composer" (pp. 96, 136–9). Be conservative about calling a source reading an error: he required "overwhelming evidence" (p. 193). **NOTE.**
- **1.5 Say what is editorial.** Agree in aim ("Obvious to whom?", p. 8). Grier would put the declaration in the report, not in signs (p. 169); see §8.

### §2. Two editions, one score
- **2.1 Same score, paratext simplified.** Agree, and Grier supplies the rationale. The clean critical text should be reproducible as the base of the performers' edition (pp. 157, 169). Performers' additions are legitimate if declared (p. 153). An editor who fixes performance details for the performer obstructs the performer (Mendel and Brett, p. 14). His interpretative edition needs no apparatus unless the notes change; it should name its base text and point to the critical edition (p. 154). Ours does, in its colophon line. **NOTE.**
- **2.2 Transposed performance edition.** Grier is silent on transposing vocal music. He lists "transposition to a more comfortable key" among the ordinary changes practical sources made (pp. 70–1), which gives the practice a period precedent. **NOTE.**
- **2.3 Editorial suggestions in round brackets.** Grier allows performers' additions to go unmarked if the introduction says what they are (p. 153). Ours are marked, which is stricter and fine. **NOTE.**
- **2.4 No working material in final editions.** Agree in spirit: the full record can live elsewhere, like the Schubert edition's deposited report (p. 173). **NOTE.**
- **2.5 Gaps.** Agree: completeness has diminishing returns, and stating the limits is honest (p. 54). **NOTE.**

### §3. Sources and sigla
- **Source descriptions (method and Sources sections).** Grier's minimum is city, library and **shelfmark** for every manuscript and every consulted copy of a print; for prints, the RISM siglum, a title-page transcription **showing line divisions**, and the special features of each copy (pp. 55–6, 212, 227–9). Our *Vox* Sources give the RISM number and the libraries, but no shelfmarks for the Wrocław partbooks, the Czartoryski *Partitura* or the Jagiellonian Cantus, and the title page is transcribed without line breaks. *Zmierzka*'s F has its shelfmark. **ADOPT**: shelfmark for every source and consulted copy; title-page transcriptions with line ends marked " | "; the copy consulted always named.
- **Prints in several copies.** Copies differ (pp. 53–4). Where a print survives in more than one copy, collate more than one or say that only one was seen. **NOTE** (it fits 2.5).
- **3.1 Framed sigla.** Silent. Grier's sigla are plain; he gives lost witnesses Greek letters and the archetype Ω (pp. 78–9). **NOTE**: if we ever draw a stemma, use Greek letters for lost witnesses (our "A lost" in *Zmierzka* would be a Greek letter in a stemma).
- **3.2 Base text = earliest complete witness.** Agree: best text, and each version its own best text where versions are legitimate (pp. 102–3, 109, 121, 195). Grier warns against preferring a source for its age alone (W1 dated earlier does not improve its readings, p. 48). **NOTE.**
- **3.5 Derivative editions.** Our rule infers a shared parent from agreement "in every note, including accidentals the source lacks". Grier: shared *good* readings prove nothing (pp. 63–4, 87); shared *errors* and distinctive *innovations* do (pp. 77–9, 88); one significant error can suffice in a short piece (p. 79); and a parent cannot be inferred from the absence of separative errors (p. 78). Our *Vox* argument actually rests on shared errors and innovations: the e♭′ at bar 15 that T prints as e♮′, the four sharps T lacks, and the Altus octave at bars 17–18. It also does not exclude that one edition was copied from the other (M 2009, N 2020). **ADOPT**: reword 3.5 so that agreement in errors or in editorial innovations the source lacks, not overall agreement, is the evidence, and the conclusion reads "descend from one another or from a common parent". In the edition, list those shared errors (p. 79: publish the errors the filiation rests on).
- **3.6 Recordings collated.** Grier treats recordings as primary sources only for recorded twentieth-century music (p. 61). For older music they belong with performers' editions as evidence of reception (pp. 13–14, 117, 151). Our rule already says the reading rests on the source and the counterpoint. **NOTE.**
- **3.8 Verdicts SOURCE RESTORED / [EDITOR] ACCEPTED.** Agree in spirit with the Eisen review: a new edition must show quickly how it differs from the standard one, and Eisen is faulted for scattering this and not grouping it in a table (pp. 200–2, 205). **CONSIDER**: where an edition overturns a standard earlier edition (Perz in *Nunc scio*), add one short table in the method section of all places where we differ, pointing to the notes.

### §4. Pitch
- **4.1 Written pitch; 4.4 chiavette.** Grier is silent on chiavette. His theorist caution applies: tell descriptive statements from prescriptive ones, since a theorist may reshape practice to fit a system (Marchetto, pp. 130–2). Banchieri's rule is more credible because his written-out example and the printed organ basses show it in practice; the *Partitura* would be the descriptive check. **NOTE.**
- **4.3 Unclefed sources: pitch by counterpoint.** Grier faced the same problem with heighted neumes: pitch from concordances, from mode numerals, then by modal analysis, with the doubt explained in a note (pp. 191–2). Our counterpoint test is the polyphonic equivalent. Agree. **NOTE.**

### §5. Note values, barring, signs
- **5.1 Unreduced values.** Differ in default. Grier expects a reduction chosen so that the tactus gets a familiar value, and stated "at the top of the score" (p. 170). He keeps original notation for the replica type of edition (pp. 148–51), and modernised editions are acceptable only with "a clear exposition of the principles" (pp. 156–7). Our choice is a deliberate middle path: modern note shapes, source values. His preliminary staff is the device that makes the relation obvious (pp. 170–1). **NOTE**: the incipit already does this job; the method sections state it. No change proposed beyond 5.5 below.
- **5.2–5.3 Bar = breve, dashed Mensurstriche, no ties.** Agree: Mensurstriche at each breve, notes kept whole, no ties, "a creative solution" (pp. 165–6). **Differ at system ends**: Grier ties a note that crosses the end of a system and rejects the alternative (full value, then blank space on the next line) as disruptive (p. 165). Our 5.3 says "the edition adds no ties", and LilyPond will not break inside a note, so our system breaks must fall where every voice changes note at once, or else the line breaks with a blank lead-in. **CONSIDER** (see question 2): allow a tie at a system break only, the one place Grier sanctions it. It is a printing device, not a reading, so it can be distinguished from the dashed `\divTie`.
- **5.4 Mensuration sign only, no modern time signature.** Agree (pp. 164–5).
- **5.5 Incipits: clef, sign, first note.** Agree, except that Grier says "first few notes" (pp. 170–1): enough for the reader to compare note shapes and see the reduction (here, the identity of values). **CONSIDER**: two or three notes where the opening has different values, cheap to do in `\prStaff`.
- **5.6 Ambitus.** Silent. **NOTE.**
- **5.7 Longs at finals; fermata only where the source has one.** Silent. **NOTE.**
- **5.8 Rests cut at bar lines in the performance edition.** Agree in spirit: break up long rests so players need not count from their last entry (p. 176). **NOTE.**

### §6. Clefs and voice names
- **6.1–6.3.** Agree: source names, archaic ones explained; voices ordered by range, and equal voices ordered by name, first or last note, or cadence behaviour (pp. 169–70). Three modern clefs, the octave treble always with its 8; original clefs reported (p. 161). Grier adds why: a modern clef loses the information about which range was meant (p. 107). Incipits and ambitus restore it. **NOTE.**
- **Performance clefs after transposition (2.2).** Grier's rule of thumb is the clef with the fewest leger lines (p. 161). **CONSIDER**: encode it as a check (section 4).

### §7. Accidentals
- **7.1–7.2 Source signs before the note, editorial ones small above.** Agree; Grier calls it the long-standing convention for early vocal music (p. 163).
- **7.2 Optional accidentals bracketed.** Grier gives three levels of sign at most and warns that Caldwell's four are too many to take in (p. 164 n. 38). We have exactly three: source, editorial, optional. Grier's Adémar signature leaves the score "free … to mark in" the user's own choices (pp. 162–3), and he accepts that editors may voice doubt where performers must still choose (p. 2). **NOTE.**
- **7.2–7.3 Scope: "that note only"; "forget" style; no cautionaries.** **Differs** on two counts.
  1. Grier's editorial sign, and in unbarred music any sign, covers the note "and immediately succeeding notes on the same pitch" (p. 163). Under "forget", a repeated note needs its own sign. For a *source* sign that means one of two things. Either we print a sign before the repeated note that the source does not have, which wrongly suggests a source accidental (7.1). Or we add an editorial sign above it, which is the clutter Grier's rule avoids. **CONSIDER**: let any sign govern immediate repetitions of the same pitch in the same voice, as period practice and Grier do. State it once in the series guide.
  2. Grier wants **courtesy accidentals** wherever there is "the slightest chance of ambiguity" (p. 164). The *Vox* method says that a b after a b♭ in the same bar "carries no sign" (principles 7.3). A modern singer reading a breve bar will carry the flat. **CONSIDER**: a small cautionary natural (or sharp) where the same letter returns, unaltered, within the breve after an altered note in the same voice. This can be found mechanically (section 4).
- **7.4–7.6 Grounds, consistency, justification.** Agree: non-specialists need ficta guidance (p. 163), and every decision is argued in the commentary (pp. 173–4). **NOTE.**
- **Zieleński after 1600.** Grier puts editorial accidentals on the staff in music after about 1600, where notation is more explicit and editorial signs correct rather than supply ficta (pp. 163–4). For the 1611 print, with its organ *Partitura*, the line is blurred. **NOTE**: keep our convention, but where the *Partitura* is seen, its accidentals may settle many signs as source signs.

### §8. Signs for editorial work
- **Overall.** Grier's preference is to mark nothing and report everything (p. 169). He accepts that early music needs some signs: ficta above the staff, ligature and coloration brackets (pp. 163, 172). Our table is fuller but each sign has one meaning, which answers his objection that bracket systems confuse (pp. 168–9). **NOTE.**
- **Ligature: horizontal bracket; coloration: corner brackets.** Exactly Grier's continuous and framing brackets (p. 172). Agree.
- **Coniunctura (currentes), liquescent in measured music.** Not in our table. Grier: a broken bracket, and a full-size note with a slashed stem (p. 172). **NOTE**: add to the table and the house file when a source needs them.
- **Note supplied: small notehead; illegible note supplied: square brackets.** Grier's convention for any mark is square bracket = editorial addition (p. 169), which our use matches. **NOTE.**
- **Emendation silent in the score; the note opens "Emended."** Exactly Grier's preference and his first category of apparatus entry (pp. 169, 173). Agree. **ADOPT** a refinement: where the error's origin can be inferred, say how it arose (a clef or octave slip by a transcriber, an eye slipping to the wrong part, a heighting slip). Grier treats the mechanism as part of the argument (pp. 72, 75–6, 99–100, 193). For *Vox* bars 17–18: an Altus copied an octave low by the parent edition, or a misread clef.
- **Text supplied, italics; [c.f.].** Silent. **NOTE.**

### §9. Repeats and structure
- **9.3 Liturgical order shown by rubrics.** Grier printed every item complete as sung, cues expanded from the nearest sources, so that non-specialists need not assemble the piece (pp. 194, 196). **CONSIDER** for the performance edition only: write out the repeated antiphon, or give a page reference at each rubric (*Don Carlos*: "go to" notes with page numbers, pp. 210–11).

### §10. Underlay
- **Overall.** Agree. Theorists are "especially valuable" here, but only contemporary or suited theory applies; an edition for performance must give a usable underlay, not just reproduce the source (p. 134). Defend the decisions from theory and the secondary literature (p. 166). Grier adds nothing to the rules themselves; the Towne/Harrán and Stoquerus notes cover them.
- **10.11 Block-texted sources.** A facsimile of a page or two shows a source's underlay habits better than a diplomatic transcription (p. 134). **CONSIDER**: print one facsimile page in every edition whose underlay is editorial from block text (*Zmierzka*'s F is public domain on Polona).
- **10.12 Departures shown by the source kept.** Agree: unanimity is not the test, and composers can break their style (pp. 32, 142). **NOTE.**
- **Lyrics engraving.** Agree with what the scores show (*Vox* p. 9): a syllable centred under a single note, left-aligned over a melisma, repeated hyphens, an extender with punctuation before it (pp. 166–7). Grier's one measurable rule: the extender must sit visibly further below the staff than one staff space, so that it cannot be read as a sixth line (p. 167). **ADOPT** as a check (section 4).
- **Melisma slurs and beams.** Grier's pre-1900 convention is a melisma shown by beam and slur, texted notes unbeamed (p. 167). We have no melisma slurs and auto-beaming off. **NOTE**: the extender does the slur's job, and partbooks had neither.

### §11. Text
- **11.1–11.2, 11.6.** Agree: edit the literary text to philological standards, the commonest faults being spelling, capitals, punctuation and syllabification; consult philologists (p. 139). **CONSIDER**: have the Old Polish texts (*Zmierzka*, *Bogurodzica*) read by a Polish philologist, as Grier recommends.
- **11.3 Diplomatic text.** Agree. Grier's transcription keeps spelling, capitals, punctuation, line and leaf ends and custodes (p. 230). **NOTE**: make capitals and punctuation explicit in 11.3.
- **Text and translation before the score (13).** Grier put the separate text *after* the music so that the music should not seem secondary (p. 196). Differ, minor. **NOTE.**

### §12. Chant
- **12.1–12.6.** Agree. Name the base witness, unlike Solesmes (p. 53). Regional versions are each valid (pp. 52–3). A restricted study beats a superficial survey (p. 53). Square notation is the "printed replica" type (pp. 148–50). Liquescents and ligatures follow their own conventions in unmeasured music (p. 172), which Gregorio keeps. **NOTE.**
- **12.5 Faint notes supplied from parallels.** Agree with Adémar's practice: a slip is proven by unanimity of the tradition plus an unchanged melodic shape (p. 193). **NOTE.**

### §13. Paratext
- **Order of the critical edition.** Agrees with Grier's order: introduction, sources and their classification, method and point of view, text, apparatus and commentary, literature (p. 175). Two of his items we lack. (a) A sample facsimile page with the sources (pp. 159, 175). We include one only where rights allow. **CONSIDER** making it the default. (b) Brief comments on the merits of each item in the literature (p. 175). *Vox* does this in places. **NOTE.**
- **Critical notes.** Our three-column notes combine Grier's apparatus and commentary, and they argue each decision, which is what he finds missing in most editions (pp. 174, 205). Agree. Differ in form: he asks for variants **in notation** (pp. 173–4). Our underlay notes are long strings like "'-ma' takes e″ and c″, 'au-' c″, '-di-' b′ …" (*Vox* bars 11–12), exactly the alphanumeric strings he calls the least comprehensible. **CONSIDER**: small notated snippets (two staves, source and edition) for underlay and multi-note variants.
- **13.2–13.3 Verify; label "my own reading".** Agree: a hypothesis labelled as speculation is worth more than an admission that the truth cannot be known (p. 182). Grier labels his own corrections "my conclusion … not a proven or provable fact" (p. 195). **NOTE.**
- **13.4 Do not restate the series rules.** Some tension. Grier faults Eisen for leaving the edition's justification in another publication: "the edition can explain itself" (p. 202). That concerned the edition's particular evidence, which our method sections do give, not general rules. **CONSIDER**: give the guide's URL in each critical edition's method opening, as the performance edition's colophon gives the repository, so that a photocopied edition still leads to its rules.
- **13.5 Performance edition argues from the music.** Silent. **NOTE.**
- **Performance edition: first page.** Grier: title, composer with dates, editor, copyright at the foot (p. 159). Our masthead and colophon line already do this. The critical score heading lacks the composer's dates and the editor (they are on the title page). **NOTE.**

### §14. Names, attribution, rights
- **14.2 Title from the main source.** Agree (p. 159). **14.6 Edition numbering.** Grier sees successive editions as "nodal points" of scholarship (p. 9). **NOTE.**

### House style
- **§1 one score, two outputs; GitHub source files and MusicXML.** Grier's electronic vision: layers of markings over an unalterable edited text (p. 178). Our two editions from one score, and the published encodings, realise this. **NOTE.**
- **§5 red never carries information.** Grier would use colour for a removable performer's layer on screen (p. 178); in print we are stricter. **NOTE.**
- **§7 performance edition: whole pages, no crowded pages.** Grier adds the page-turn rule: turn at the end of a section, or at a rest at the foot of a recto; never rely on "V.S." (p. 176). **ADOPT** as a warning check for multi-page performance scores.
- **§9 no notes on the music page.** Grier's strongest presentation point (pp. 154–8, 172). Agree.
- **§9 Mensurstriche dashed.** Grier describes them only as short barlines between the staves (p. 165). Dashing is our refinement. **NOTE.**
- **§10 critical notes "bar | voice | note"; Helmholtz pitches.** Agree on the entry form (p. 173); see notated variants above.
- **Spacing (pol-ren.ily `base-shortest-duration` 1/4).** Grier calls proportional spacing "highly desirable" where the range of values is narrow (p. 166). **CONSIDER** (section 4).

## 4. Parameters we could encode

1. **Accidental scope** (p. 163). If adopted: a lint pass that flags a sign (source or editorial) placed on a note that immediately repeats the previous note's pitch in the same voice, with no other note between. In LilyPond, a custom `accidentalStyle` rule list with memory limited to immediate repetitions (`Accidental_engraver` rule: same pitch, consecutive note only).
2. **Courtesy accidentals** (p. 164). Check: in each voice and breve, a note whose letter name matches an earlier altered note in the same breve but which is not itself altered → print a small cautionary sign (`\cautionary` or a parenthesis-free `AccidentalCautionary`), or at least report it.
3. **Incipit length** (pp. 170–1). `\prStaff` incipit: allow 1–3 notes; default 2 where the opening values differ.
4. **System-break ties** (p. 165). Allow `\tie` only at a forced break (`prBreaks…`), via `Completion_heads_engraver` limited to the break moment; lint rejects solid ties elsewhere (5.3).
5. **Bracket set** (p. 172). Existing: ligature (solid horizontal bracket), coloration (corner brackets). Add: `\coniunctura` = dashed horizontal bracket (`HorizontalBracket.style = #'dashed-line`); `\liq` = full-size note with slashed stem (`Stem.stroke-style = "grace"`), inside the ligature bracket if ligated.
6. **Proportion equation** (p. 171). Markup macro printing "old value ← = → new value" (e.g. ◊ = ◊◊◊ with arrows) above the first affected note, for any future tripla or sesquialtera passage.
7. **Lyrics** (pp. 166–7). Keep `lyricMelismaAlignment = #LEFT`; check that the gap from the bottom staff line to the lyric baseline (extender) is ≥ 1.5 staff spaces (Grier: "visibly larger" than one staff space); punctuation before the extender; repeated hyphens (`LyricHyphen.dash-period`) through long internal melismas.
8. **Proportional spacing** (p. 166). Trial: `\set Score.proportionalNotationDuration = #(ly:make-moment 1/2)` with `uniform-stretching`, or raise `SpacingSpanner.spacing-increment`, for scores whose values run mostly from minim to breve. Judge by page count.
9. **Clef choice after transposition** (p. 161). Check: for each voice in a transposed performance edition, count leger lines under treble, octave treble and bass; warn if the chosen clef is not the minimum.
10. **Page turns** (p. 176). Check, for performance editions over two pages: at each verso-to-recto turn every voice should end a section or rest. Warn otherwise.
11. **Notated variants in critical notes** (pp. 173–4). `lilypond-book` snippets at a small staff size (suggest 13–14) in the notes column: source reading against edited reading, underlay included.
12. **Source description fields** (pp. 55–6, 227–9). Lint of Sources entries: siglum; city, library, **shelfmark**; RISM siglum for prints; title page with " | " line ends; copy consulted named; foliation in "fol. 8r", "8r–v" form (p. 221).
13. **Collation and transcription conventions for working notes, not editions** (pp. 230–2). A grid collation with one row per source, per breve unit, whole units written out, *om.* for omissions; irregularities recorded as *add.*, *in marg.*, *a.l.*, *b.l.*, *in eras.*, *eras.*, *canc.*, *exp.*, *h¹/h²*, *ink¹/ink²*. In editions, spell these out (house style: no abbreviations like "ed.").
14. **Filiation rule** (pp. 63–4, 78–82, 88). Series rule: on textual evidence alone, never treat a witness as a mere copy and never split more than two ways; the evidence is shared errors or shared editorial innovations, listed in the edition.

## 5. Open questions for Miki

1. **Accidental scope and cautionaries (7.2–7.3).** Should a sign govern immediate repetitions of the same pitch (Grier, p. 163, and period practice), and should we print courtesy signs where a letter returns unaltered inside the breve (p. 164)? Both reverse parts of the "forget" rule.
2. **System breaks (5.3).** What does a note crossing a system break do in our scores now? Are breaks restricted to points where all voices articulate together? Grier ties at system ends and nowhere else (p. 165). Would you allow that one tie?
3. **3.5 rewording.** Rest the derivative-edition argument on shared errors and innovations (bar 15 e♭′, the bars 17–18 Altus octave, the supplied sharps), and allow "one from the other" as well as "common parent"?
4. **Notated variants.** Worth a small music example in the critical notes for underlay and multi-note readings, as Grier insists (pp. 173–4)?
5. **Facsimile by default.** One sample page in every critical edition where rights allow, and in particular wherever the underlay is editorial (pp. 134, 159, 175)?
6. **Differences table.** Where we overturn a standard edition (Perz), a one-table summary in the method section (pp. 200–2)?
7. **Liturgical repeats in the performance edition.** Write out the antiphon repeats, or give page references at each rubric (pp. 196, 210–11)?
8. **Philological check.** A Polish philologist to read the Old Polish texts before release (p. 139)?
9. **Proportional spacing.** Trial on one score (p. 166)?
