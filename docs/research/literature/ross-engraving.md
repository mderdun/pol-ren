# Ross, *The Art of Music Engraving and Processing*: reading notes

Read in full: all four chapters and the appendix (pp. 1–210, A-1–A-56), with the music examples looked at as page images where the OCR failed (staff-size chart, spacing proportions, beam-slant chart, barline examples, lyric lines, lyric centring, appendix size tables). Page numbers are the book's printed folios. "ss" = staff space (the book's "space"). Conversions to millimetres use our sizes: LilyPond staff 17 (critical) = 5.97 mm staff, 1.49 mm ss; staff 19 (performance) = 6.68 mm staff, 1.68 mm ss. LilyPond line thickness at 17 is 0.47 pt (0.11 ss), at 19 0.49 pt (0.10 ss).

## 1. Reference and character of the book

Ted Ross, *The Art of Music Engraving and Processing: A complete manual, reference and text book on preparing music for reproduction and print* (Miami, London, New York: Hansen Books, © 1970). The copy read is the second, revised edition published after the author's death (preliminary note by Henry Kass); it is undated, but its computer section cites a 1972 patent (A-33), so it was printed after 1972. Copy: archive.org, "artofmusicengrav0000tedr", 292 scanned pages.

**What it is.** A trade manual written by a working American engraver. It sets down the rules plate engravers passed from journeyman to apprentice without writing them down (preface, v), and teaches them to music typists, autographers and other "process engravers" who were replacing plate engraving in the 1950s–60s. Chapter I is a history of music printing and a survey of processes (plate engraving, autographing, stamping, the Halstan stencil process, photo-type, music typewriters, transfers). Chapter II is page planning (casting off, vertical layout, ruling, horizontal spacing). Chapters III–IV are the notation rules, with numbers in staff spaces. The appendix covers page sizes, page turns, title-page layout, penwork, reductions, proofreading and offset printing.

**Authority.** For the plate-engraving workshop's practical norms it is a primary witness. It gives stem lengths, beam placement, key-signature spacing, lyric placement and page proportions as numbers. The author says the rules descend from eighteenth-century practice and changed little since (81). For the physical process (pewter plates, punches, intaglio proofs) it is first-hand.

**Limits.**
- *Repertory and market.* American commercial publishing c. 1970: sheet music, band and dance orchestrations, stage band, guitar frames, hymnals. Choral music appears only as "chorals" on octavo pages (58, A-2) and as the four-part hymn (152–157). There is nothing on early music, mensural notation, editorial signs, incipits, ambitus, or critical apparatus.
- *History is unreliable.* The 16th-century account omits Petrucci. It dates Attaingnant's first printed underlay to 1527 in one place (6) and 1529 in another (A-35). It says beams appeared in engraved music in the mid-16th century (88), although its own first engraved music is dated 1581 (7). Do not cite Ross for history.
- *Internal inconsistencies.* The Musicwriter staff is "very closely" No. 3 (43), "between No. 1 and No. 2" (A-15), and smaller than No. 1 and 2 in the size tables (A-30). Sheet music is No. 3 (58), but No. 5 is "the normal size for sheet music" (A-26). The staff-size chart (57) gives no dimensions. Ross admits that one engraver's No. 2 can differ from another's by a line's thickness (56).
- *Polemic.* Much of chapter I and the appendix attacks music typewriters and amateur penwork. It is useful as a statement of what "engraved-looking" meant to an engraver, but it is partisan.

## 2. Chapter-by-chapter notes

### Chapter I. Music reproduction (1–54)

**History (1–10).** Manuscript copying, then woodblocks: early blocks printed red staff lines with black notes added by hand or stencil, or the reverse (4). Burtius, *Musices opusculum* (Bologna 1487), is an early block with staff and notes together (4). Type: Schoeffer 1490, Oeglin 1507 single impression "with little success" (5). The note-plus-staff-segment type is credited to Haultin (1525) and used by Attaingnant, the first to print text under the notes (6). That type gave "very uneven and disjointed" staff lines (6). Even after 400 years typography never solved breaks in the staff lines (12). Copper engraving begins 1581 (7). Pewter and punches arrive c. 1700 with Walsh, and punched plates had replaced cut plates by c. 1730 (7–8). Ross finds the cut ("pure") engraving inconsistent between characters (7). Punches reached real artistic quality only in the late 19th century (8). Lithography (Senefelder, 1796) printed hand-copied pages, not engravings (9–10).

**Music typography (11–12).** A case holds c. 400 characters. One 3/4 bar of eight notes in tenor clef needs about 75 pieces of type (12). The type was too fragile to print from, so stereos or electros were taken. Its print is "never too clear"; spacing is dictated by the type bodies, not notation rules (12).

**Plate engraving (13–30).**
- *Plate.* Pewter ("tin") of antimony, lead and tin. More tin gives a sharper print (15). Plates 9–13.5 in long and 6–9.5 in wide, 0.05 in (1.3 mm) thick. A 10 × 7 in plate weighs one pound (15).
- *Tools (16–19).* Scorer (five-toothed claw, one per punch size) to pull the staff lines; hook for barlines; double scorer for a thick and a thin line; marking pin; spring dividers for leger lines; compass for spacing; correcting calipers; busk; levelling hammer; scraper; burnisher; gravers (tint, lozenge, elliptical, flat) for stems, slurs and hairpins.
- *Punches (20–23).* About 50 punches a set. Of 12 catalogued sizes only eight are in use (20, 57). Slurs come in about 11 sizes, plus roman and italic alphabets. Designs differ between sizes of one set, as if from different die-makers (21–22).
- *Process (24–29).* Cast off, then rule the staves: two or more passes of the scorer from the margin line, with the burr scraped off (24). Mark the plate backwards, right to left (24–25). Space is allotted "in proportion to their value", judged from the commonest note value by a compass set and walked along the staff, usually right at the second or third try (24–25). Leger-line trick: set the dividers to lines 1 and 5, then move one point (25). Lyric: a base line is traced and each syllable's position marked. Titles are struck from the central letter outwards (26). Punching on a stone or steel block raises bulges, which are planished flat (26). Barlines, stems, leger lines, slurs and hairpins are cut by hand with the graver, then burnished and sanded (27). Proofs are inked on the surface only, with green or blue ink, giving white notes on a coloured ground for the proofreader (27). Corrections are hammered flat from the back and re-engraved (28).
- *Printing from the plate (28–29).* Formerly a copperplate press, one page at a time. Now a single black-and-white pull serves as camera copy for offset. The pull: paste ink is rubbed into the plate, warmed so it enters the cut lines, worked in with a squeegee, the excess scraped off with card and the surface wiped clean with cotton and a lye pad. Damp paper goes on, with felt as cushion, and the plate runs through the press and back (29). Weather affects ink and paper. Engravers mixed their own ink. The paper must be damp "just enough" for the ink to take "and not run" (29). Alternatives: a powder rubbed into the lines with surface-rolled black ink, photographed as a negative; or a pull on near-transparent paper used as a negative (29).

**Autographing, stamping, stencils, photo-type (31–38).** Autographing: drawing in greasy ink on transfer paper for lithography (from 1805). Later it was done for photo-offset, with staves ruled by a five-point pen or roller and noteheads drawn freehand (31–33). Stamping: steel dies inked from a brayer onto paper. The ink should be "as stiff as possible" to avoid a squashed impression. Dies are held vertical with even pressure on a cushioned sheet. Only heads and short ties are stamped; stems, beams, barlines and long slurs are drawn with pen and ink (33–35). Halstan stencils are cut oversize and reduced photographically (36–37). Photo-type uses white celluloid symbols on a layered black board, with a 5/8 in staff, reduced (37–38).

**Typewriters and transfers (39–54).** Keaton Musictyper (1947, two-pica staff between No. 0 and No. 1; cloth ribbon fills small counters) (42–43). Musicwriter (1955), Pavey Musigraph, Harmony Writer, Pro Musica Compositor (43–48). Leroy templates, acetates, and Not-a-Set rub-down transfers in staff sizes 1–7, which Ross ranks above any proof pulled from a plate for clean camera copy (49–53). A 17-step chronology of processes closes the chapter (54).

### Chapter II. Preparing music for engraving (55–81)

**Planning (55–58).** The plate engraver says layout takes nine years to master (56). Questions to settle first: page size, whether page turns fall right, and the staff size. A whole folio must keep one staff size, so all its manuscripts are studied before engraving starts (56). Staff numbers are universal for scorers but do not match punch numbers (57). Trade names and uses (58): No. 1 Giant (instruction books); No. 2 English (band, orchestra); No. 3 Regular (sheet music, classics); No. 4 Peter (folios, organ); No. 5 Large Middle (bands, sheet music); No. 6 Small Middle (*chorals*, condensed sheet music); No. 7 Cadenza (pocket editions, cues); No. 8 Pearl (thematic adverts). Nos. 3–6 cover most work (58).

*Staff heights (derived, not stated).* The chart on p. 57 has no figures. They can be derived from the appendix. A No. 5 staff's height goes 31½ times into a 7½ in line, which gives 6.05 mm (A-26–27). The Musicwriter staff goes 31½ times into 9 in, which gives 7.26 mm (A-27). The size tables (A-30–31) give the working width each staff size needs on the Musicwriter for a fixed printed width. From these: No. 1 ≈ 8.1 mm, No. 2 ≈ 7.5, No. 3 ≈ 6.9, No. 4 ≈ 6.7–6.8, No. 5 ≈ 6.05–6.2, No. 6 ≈ 5.6, No. 7 ≈ 4.9 mm (the concert and octavo tables differ by up to 0.15 mm; the book marks some values "approximate, within 1/16"). **Our critical staff (5.97 mm) is a No. 5; our performance staff (6.68 mm) is a No. 4. Ross's choral size is No. 6 (≈5.6 mm) on an octavo page.**

**Casting off (59–60).** Rules for distributing bars over staves (59–60):
1. Keep staves on a page roughly uniform. Do not put six bars on one and three on the next when their content is alike.
2. Successive staves with similar content should not be cast off identically; avoid barlines aligned from staff to staff. This is a rule for parts.
3. Key changes at the start or end of a staff.
4. Rehearsal letters and repeat signs at the start of a staff, or not much past the middle; never one bar before the end.
5. First and second endings on the same staff.
6. Do not split a hairpin (or, Ross adds, a tie) across a staff break.
7. "D.S. al Coda" at the end of a staff; the coda starts a new staff.
8. Do not put an uncrowded staff beside a crowded one. Balance characters and bars together. His example: two bars of semiquavers and sixteen bars of semibreves, each 34 characters, should not share a page.
9. With lyrics, long words take precedence over the balance (60).

**Vertical layout (60–69).** Staves are not equidistant: leger lines, inner titles and opposed stems change the gaps (60). First staff goes below the title block with room for leger-line notes above it; last staff above the copyright, with room below (60–61). The eye may draw the first and last staves slightly inward to gain room (61). The plate engraver measures all vertical distances with a compass set to the staff. Machine engravers use a printed chart of staff segments (61). The book works a three-staff song layout step by step (62–68). The working values:
- **1 ss clear** below the lowest ink of a staff (note, stem tip, flag, accidental) before the next item, and 1 ss clear above the next staff's highest ink (64–68).
- A dynamic ("mf") takes 2½ ss; a chord name 2 ss; **one lyric line 2 ss; two lyric lines 4 ss**, one staff height (64, 66, 68).
- A tie that rises toward the next staff takes 1 ss (66). A volta line plus its slur plus a triplet figure: 1 + 1 + 2 ss (68).
- Where nothing stands between two staves, leave **at least 4 ss** (68 note).
- *Distributing surplus* (69): if the trial layout is 3–6 ss short of the page, add the surplus equally between systems. If more is needed, add it between staves, "always" preferring the staff gaps "at a ratio of anywhere from three to one, to six to one". (Read as: the staff gaps take three to six times the share of the system gaps. The sentence is not fully explicit.) To condense, close the generous allowances for lyrics and chord names first (69).

**Ruling (70–72).** Pre-printed staff paper is "the curse" of typists: every page must be spaced for its content. Staves with leger-line music need more room than staves without (70). Five-point ruling pens give lines too thin for more than 25% reduction (71). Line weight on the Staff-o-graph depends on pressure (72).

**Punctuation (horizontal spacing) (73–81).**
- Three kinds: mathematically perfect, mathematically imperfect, lyrical (73).
- The engraver picks the commonest value as the unit and sets the compass. Typical: crotchet 3½ ss, quaver 2½ ss, minim 5 ss, closed to 3 / 2 ss if the staff overflows (73).
- **One space after the barline before the first note** (74–75; measured left edge to left edge; 1½ ss to the note centre). Ross keeps this against those who would give the barline no space (74).
- Within one staff, equal values get equal space. Across staves they need not (74, 81).
- Accidentals have no time value. Give them extra space only when there is no room. Most plate engravers add it anyway, which Ross dislikes (76); his "Engraved in England" example shows the extra space (79).
- **Proportion table (77, read from the page image):** crotchet 3½ ss; minim 4¾; quaver 2½; dotted quaver 3; semibreve 7¼ ss. Successive ratios are 1.40, 1.36, 1.53, close to √2 per doubling. A half note in place of two crotchets shortens the bar by half a unit (≈1¾ ss) and is still enough to read the rhythm "even if the notes were replaced by dots" (77).
- Stems back to back get ½ ss extra (77).
- A semibreve alone in a bar is indented 3 ss, not 1½ ss. Do not place it left of centre (78). Multi-bar rests get bar widths that grow with the number (78).
- In multi-staff music, the shortest values in each beat set the spacing (79).
- *Lyrical spacing* (79–80): when words are long, the music is spaced to the words; plate engravers sometimes stamped the words first (79). Words are centred under the head by eye, with a slight leftward bias. "To": the middle of the word under the head. "The": the left side of *h*. "There": the right side of *h*. "Through": between *r* and *o* (79). In tight places any part of the word under the head will do (80). **A syllable sung to several notes starts flush with the left side of the first note.** Ross reports the rule is followed "very closely by most plate engravers" but sees "no musical advantage" in it (80).
- When a lyric note tied across a staff end carries an extension line, leave extra space after it (81).

### Chapter III. General notation (82–127)

**Stems (82–87).** Line quality: each line has a set weight relative to its staff and must keep it along its whole length. A thin barline or a thick stem is a glaring error. Stems are "somewhat thinner than the staff line" (1970 practice; see 151) and never "flabby and distorted" (82). Normal stem **3½ ss** (one octave). Plate engravers call it 3 ss because they measure from the near edge of the head (83). Minimum 2½ ss "only under extreme circumstances" (83). The turning point is the middle line, which now takes a down-stem (83). Chords: the outer note farther from the middle line decides; ties go by majority, then by surrounding pattern (84–85). Notes beyond the second leger line: the stem reaches the middle line (86). A flagged stem is never under 3 ss (86). Stem ends should rise and fall with the heads, so a stem may be shortened or lengthened to follow the line of neighbours (87).

**Beams (88–126).** Beam thickness ½ ss (88; repeated 83, 147). Its ends must sit on, hang from or straddle a staff line (88). Vocal music uses flags, beams only for melismas, though publishers are moving to beams (88). The **white wedge** argument (98–99): a slanted beam inside the staff leaves small acute white wedges against a line. "Years ago", before printing improved, these filled with ink. Engravers therefore placed beam ends on, under or across lines, or slanted the beam more to make the wedge large and clear. Slants by interval: second ¼ ss; third ½–1; fourth 1–1¼; fifth 1¼; sixth 1¼–1½; seventh 1¼–1¾; octave 1¼–2 ss. Beams of notes outside the staff ≤ ½ ss (101, 111). Notes closer than 3–4 ss apart take ¼–½ ss (100, 112). Distance between beams ¼ ss, so the beam pitch is ¾ ss; machine dies left ½ ss (119–120). Beam rules by metre (89–93). Fractional beam = head width (124). Each beam beyond two adds ¾–1 ss of stem (125). **Not used in our scores (no beams).**

**Flags (127).** Always on the right of the stem. Slightly over 3 ss long, reaching nearly to the head's middle for up-stems and touching the head for down-stems. A 3½ ss stem carries one flag (3 ss is possible); each flag beyond two adds 1 ss (127).

### Chapter IV. Specific notation (128–210)

**Articulation (128–130).** Staccatissimo wedge ≈1 ss. Tenuto slightly thicker than a staff line and head-wide, in a space, a third to a fourth from the head. Marcato ≈2 ss wide with 1 ss opening, kept outside the staff where possible. Sforzando ≈1 × 1 ss. Marks go inside slurs except at phrase ends (128–130).

**Accidentals (130–135).** Some engravers use a punch one size smaller for accidentals, "especially" for reminders in parentheses (130; again 146). A single accidental stands ≈1½ ss before its note, left edge to left edge (131). Stacking rules follow: a seventh or more aligns vertically; otherwise the highest goes nearest the note (131–134). Avoid a stem between an accidental and its head (134). The double flat is one die (135).

**Ties and slurs (136–143).** A tie is horizontal; inside the staff its centre lies in a space, clear of the lines (136, 137). It runs head to head without touching either (136). Shape: "a thick center which gradually tapers to uniformly thin ends", not a crescent (136). Short ties and slurs are punched; long ones are cut with the graver (136). Long ties have their ends cross the lines, with the centre in a space (137). Direction is opposite the stem; in chords the outer notes' ties curve outward (138). Keep ties clear of dots (139). Slurs clear the staff where possible (139). A long slur is nearly straight with both ends bent uniformly toward the notes; no French curves (140). Slurs start and end over head centres; a slurred passage ending in a tie runs to the second tied note (141). "Two notes of the same pitch can be tied, but never slurred" (142).

**Start-of-staff spacing (143–147),** measured left edge to left edge with a compass:
- clef indented ½–1 ss from the staff start or system barline (144, 166);
- clef → first flat or sharp 3½ ss; then 1 ss per flat; 1–1¼ ss per sharp (144–145);
- clef → time signature 3½ ss; last key accidental → time signature 2½ ss (145);
- clef → first note 5½ ss; last key accidental → first note 3½ ss; time signature → first note 3½ ss (145);
- an accidental on the first note moves the note ½ ss right, to 4 ss from the last key accidental (146);
- repeat bar after clef 5½ ss (to its dots 7); after a key or time signature 3½ ss (to its dots 5) (147).
- **Repeat bar anatomy (147 n.):** thick line ½ ss ("as thick as a beam"), white ½, thin line plus white ½, dots ½: 1½ ss in all.

**Key changes, clusters (148–150).** Double bar before a change, cancellations first; changes at staff ends where possible (148). Clusters: the upper note of a second goes right of the stem (149–150).

**Barlines (151–154).** Barline weight "can only be roughly estimated": one cutting tool serves two or three staff sizes (151). **Weight hierarchy:** before offset printing, "staff lines were thin, stems thicker, and barlines thickest". In 1970 barlines are thick, staff lines thinner and stems thinnest (151). A barline never precedes a clef unless it is a system barline (151). **Vocal ensemble and hymn: a systemic barline at the start, then separate barlines through each staff**. Choir and piano: the piano staves connect (152–153, figure). Double barline: lines about ¾ ss apart. Final barline: thin, ½ ss white, thick ½ ss (152).

**Brace and bracket (155–157).** Brace for one instrument's staves and for like instruments (155); also for grouping lyric lines (155). **Bracket: a vertical line ½ ss thick**, the same tool as a beam, with curved ends beyond the outer staves. Used for choir, sections and "chorals" (155–156). In typeset hymnals the bracket replaces the system barline (157).

**Tuplets (157–164).** Bold italic numerals; the 3 is ≈1⅓ ss high (159). The bracket is as thick as a barline (161). Not relevant to us.

**Clefs (165–168).** The treble clef's body fills the two lowest spaces. The bass-clef ball sits on the F line, its dots in the spaces either side (165). C clef "more and more" centred on the third space in choral works (166). For male tenor parts, some publishers use a "double treble clef": two clefs touching, not overlapping (166). Clef changes are 2–3 sizes smaller, placed before the barline at the end of the staff (167–168).

**Dots (168–173).** Formerly two or three dot sizes (augmentation normal, staccato smaller, signs and clefs larger). By 1970 one size, **about ⅓ ss** (169). An augmentation dot goes ≈1 ss right of the head, past the flag tail if flagged. It always sits in a space; for notes on lines and leger lines, in the space above (169). Two voices: upper-voice dots go in the space above, lower-voice dots in the space below. Never put two dots in one space (169). Dots must not touch staff lines (171).

**Rests (173–181).** Whole rest: solid, ½ ss thick (the beam), about the width of a crotchet head. It is centred between the barlines as the whole-bar rest. The breve rest is used for 4/2 (173–174). Half rest: same shape, on the middle line. It expresses two crotchets only where the bar splits into two equal halves, never in 3/4 (174–175). The last two thirds of a tripartite unit may not be combined in one rest (175). Crotchet rest 3 ss tall, with exact placement against the lines given (175). Quaver rest just under 2 ss (176). Dotted half and crotchet rests are not used in simple time (170, 179). In compound time, "a fraction of one beat cannot be combined with a fraction of another beat" (178). Several pages of correct and incorrect rest groupings (180–181).

**Leger lines (182).** One space apart; "somewhat thicker than the staff line"; extend "less than a half space" each side of the head, shortened when crowded (182). Suspended (second-cluster) heads get lengthened leger lines (182).

**Lyric lines (183).** Four kinds: melisma, protraction (ties), extension, omission. All sit at the baseline and are **about the thickness of the period**, except with unusually large type. They run from the end of the word "to the beginning of" the last tied or slurred note, not through its duration (183, figure: CORRECT stops under the start of the last note). An omission line stands for a missing word under notes (183).

**Fermata (184–185).** About ½ ss high and a little over 2 ss wide, centred on its dot. Above the staff, aligned with the head (not the stem). Placed a third above a symbol outside the staff. For lower voices sharing a staff it is inverted below. For choir on two staves with lyrics between, above the upper staff and below the lower (184). Over a tie, raised (185).

**Dynamics (185–188).** Special bold italic font. Heights: *f* 2½ ss, *p* 2, *m* 1 (185). **In vocal music dynamics sit above the staff**, to keep clear of the lyrics (186). Hairpin lines are no thicker than a staff line, open at most 1½ ss, and kept nearly horizontal (187).

**Cue and grace notes (189–191).** Cue notes use the punch of a smaller staff: for staff sizes 1–3, the No. 6 note; for 4–6, the No. 7 note (189). Grace notes are slightly smaller than cues and stemmed up (190). A small solo staff above a piano part keeps the cue ratio (189–190).

**Other (192–210).** Passing tones (192); syncopation (193); endings: bracket lines slightly thicker than staff lines; numerals ≈2 ss high, followed by a dot; brackets aligned with the barlines; the vertical stroke reaches at least the foot of the numeral (194–195). Trill sign just under 2 ss (197). Arpeggio dies ≈3 ss long (198). Tremolo (199–200). Repeat signs (202). Octave signs (203). Sharing a staff: vocal dynamics above the staff (204–205). Spoken text (206–208). Pause: comma above the staff. Caesura: two slashes, each half a beam thick and 2 ss long (208). The fermata "first used" in 16th-century polyphony (208; inaccurate). Stage-band articulations (210).

### Appendix (A-1–A-56)

**Page sizes (A-2–A-3).** Concert 9 × 12 in, ½ in margins, 8 × 11 in printing area (or 7½ wide; or 8½ × 11 page with 7½ × 10 area). **Octavo** (dance orchestrations, *chorals*, some band): 6¾ × 10½ in (171 × 267 mm), printing area about 5⅞ × 9½ in (149 × 241 mm; width 5½–6). Quick-step 6¾ × 5¼. Hymn 5¾ × 8¼ in, area 4½ × 7½. Oblong 12 × 9 in, area 10 × 7½. **Margins at least ½ in (12.7 mm) on all sides** (A-3).

**Page numbers and turns (A-3–A-5).** Folios at the top outer corner, close to the printing-area edge; type matched to the staff, no smaller than 8–10 pt (A-3). Turn after a rest, with the rest on the page before the turn (A-3–4). Number the pages so that a three-page part opens like a book (A-4). Stagger turns in band parts (A-4). Keyboard turns go where a hand is free (A-5).

**Page layout and design (A-6–A-9).** "Traditional balance is at all times valued the most" (A-6). The title is centred over the printing area (the width of an unindented staff); a good title size has a point size equal to three staff spaces (A-7). Subtitle smaller, in parentheses, centred below. A dedication or source credit goes centred above the title (A-7). Writers' credits sit flush right, midway between the title and the first staff, raised if notes rise above that staff. **On sheet music the lyricist goes flush left and the composer flush right** (A-7). Tempo mark in bold roman, its first letter aligned with the time signature (A-8). Rehearsal letters go above and slightly after the barline, nearer the staff below (A-8). Copyright is centred at the foot of the printing area (A-9).

**Penwork (A-9–A-14).** In finishing, there is "no substitute for artistic excellence" (A-9). Ball-point pens cannot "look engraved" (A-10; 136). A crow-quill pen draws stems, barlines and hairpins; a Graphos pen draws beams (A-12–13). Corrections: white-out or paste-over (A-14).

**Complementary equipment (A-14–A-16).** Plate engravers punch titles letter by letter, "somewhat uneven and out of alignment", so publishers often had titles typeset and pasted in (A-14). Texts, credits and copyright in high-quality work: bold or medium-bold traditional type, 8–10 pt (A-15).

**Chord frames (A-17–A-21).** Not relevant.

**Arranging, editing, proofreading (A-22–A-24).** The editor adapts notation to the user's grade level (A-22). The proofreader's checklist (A-23):
- notes against the score;
- clefs, key and time signatures, tempo;
- ties and slurs, especially across staff breaks;
- positions of all symbols in the staff;
- vertical alignment across staves;
- auxiliary marks consistent within a section;
- credits;
- bar counts equal in all parts and the score.

The lyric is proofed in a separate pass, never with the music: capitals, spelling, punctuation, syllabification, melisma lines (A-23). Proofreader's marks (A-24).

**Reductions (A-25–A-31).** Process work is done oversize and reduced. Reduction makes blemishes less "outstanding", but it is untrue that it removes them (A-25). **Lyric type 9–10 pt; as a rule of thumb, 1½ to 2¾ ss tall** (A-25). Engravers' punched letters are "slightly narrower and taller" than typewriter type and can be packed closely when words are long. Comparison on A-26: hyphenated *stum-ble*, *break-fast* set tight. A-28 shows the same lyric line on staff sizes 2–8. To match a staff size, compare the ratio of staff height to line width (No. 5: 1 to 31½) (A-26–29). Tables of working sizes (A-30–31).

**Names of voices (A-32–33).** Full names on the first system, abbreviations after. Voices: S., A., T., Bar., B. (A-32–33).

**Computers (A-33).** Dal Molin's computed cast-off and punctuation (1953); the Music Reprographics CPS-300 (1969).

**Engraving steps (A-34).** Page size → staff size → cast off → vertical layout → rule → punctuate → stamp → penwork → lyric → paste-up.

**Chronology (A-35).** Unreliable (see §1).

**Offset printing (A-36–A-41).** Camera (18%–300%), negative, opaquing, stripping, plate burning on aluminium, offset press: plate to blanket to paper. A cheap plate gives 5,000–15,000 impressions (A-38). Web press, binding.

**Other (A-42–A-56).** International terminology table (A-42–44, rotated). Metronome marks use a cue-size note, never a regular one, with type about lyric size (A-45). Tempo marks in bold roman, 10–12 pt, never smaller than the text (A-46); tempo glossary (A-47–49). Copying for publication: hyphenate lyrics by a standard dictionary; put words under the notes they belong to (A-50). Extra beam examples (A-52). Shape notes (A-53–55). Octave names (A-56).

## 3. Against our practice

ADOPT = clear gain, low risk. CONSIDER = judgement call for Miki. NOTE = context only.

### Editorial principles

**§1–4 (stance, two editions, sources, pitch).** Ross is silent on all of this. One contrast: he expects the publisher's editor to adapt notation to the user's grade level (A-22). Principles 2.1 refuse that: "no reason to baby the performer". NOTE.

**5.2–5.3 Barring, Mensurstriche.**
- *What we do:* dashed lines between the staves only; notes cross the bar unbroken.
- *Ross:* for vocal ensembles, a system barline at the start, then a separate barline through each staff and none between (152–153). The barline "commands" one space before the next note (74–75).
- *Verdict:* we differ, deliberately: the opposite placement. Ross gives no reason to change it. One side effect to check: whether LilyPond still reserves the barline's space inside the staff when the in-staff bar glyph is empty. If it does, notes after each Mensurstrich sit a little late. NOTE.

**5.7 Longs and fermatas.** Ross gives the fermata's form and position (184–185): ½ ss high, a little over 2 ss wide, aligned with the head; in a two-staff choir score above the top and below the bottom. With lyrics between staves it is placed this way to avoid the words (184). We print fermatas only where the source has one. If one is printed in a lower voice whose lyric line is just below, his placement logic (keep it clear of the lyric) supports placing it above. NOTE.

**5.8 Rests (performance edition: cut at bar lines, grouped by the bar).**
- *Ross:* the rests must show the bar's halves at a glance. A half rest covers two beats only where the bar divides into two equal halves (174–175). "a fraction of one beat cannot be combined with a fraction of another beat" (178). The half and crotchet rest are never dotted in simple time (170, 179).
- *Translated to a breve bar under ¢:* within the bar, rests should break at the semibreve (tactus) boundary. For example, a minim rest on beat 2 followed by a semibreve of silence is written minim rest + semibreve rest, not semibreve + minim. This matches the "entries can be counted" purpose of 5.8.
- *Verdict:* CONSIDER as an explicit sub-rule of 5.8.
- *Also:* Ross's whole-bar rest is a semibreve rest centred in the bar (174). We place rests at their time position, which is right for mensural rests. NOTE.

**6.1 Voice names.** Full on the first system, abbreviated after (A-32). Agree. NOTE.

**6.2 Clefs.** Ross knows the octave-treble problem for tenors. Some publishers used a "double treble clef" (two clefs touching); in choral work the C clef was moving to the third space (166). We use the octave treble clef. Agree in effect. NOTE.

**7.1–7.3 Accidentals.**
- *Size:* reminder or parenthesised accidentals were punched one size smaller (130, 146). Our editorial accidentals above the note are small (font-size −1.5); optional ones in brackets −2.5. Agree. NOTE.
- *Spacing:* about 1½ ss from an accidental's left edge to its note's left edge (131). Add no space for accidentals that fit (76).
- *Scope:* Ross's accidentals govern the bar (130); our "forget" style is a deliberate departure. NOTE.

**8. Editorial signs.**
- *Small noteheads (supplied notes).* Our `\ed` is font-size −3 (71%). Ross's cue note is the next punch class down: for staff sizes 4–6 the No. 7 note (189). From the derived heights that is about 0.73–0.82 of normal, depending on staff size (≈ −1.7 to −2.7 in LilyPond font-size). His grace note, "slightly smaller" than the cue (190), is about where our −3 sits. A supplied note is sung like any other note; Ross's cue size is the one meant to be read and performed. **CONSIDER `\ed` at −2**, the size `\sugg` already uses. The two signs stay distinct: `\sugg` adds round brackets and appears only in the performance edition.
- *Dashed tie for divided notes.* Ross's tie shape (thick centre, thin ends; centre in a space, clear of the lines; 136–137) is LilyPond's default. Agree. NOTE.
- *Ligature and c.f. brackets.* Ross's comparable brackets (tuplet, volta) are barline weight or "slightly thicker than staff lines" (161, 194). Our c.f. line is deliberately light (0.6) and dashed. NOTE.

**9.1–9.2 Repeats.** Ross: repeat signs at the start of a staff, or no later than the middle, never one bar before its end; first and second endings on the same staff (59–60). Our 9.1 (each repeated half starts and ends on its own system) satisfies the first. *Plaude euge* already keeps both endings on one system (checked, performance p. 1). Agree. NOTE: write the "both endings on one system" preference into `engraving.ily` practice.

**10. Underlay.** Ross is silent on syllable placement rules. On how underlay is set:
- *Melismatic syllables flush left* with the first note (80). This is the trade rule, though Ross himself saw no advantage in it. LilyPond's `lyricMelismaAlignment` default is LEFT. Agree. NOTE.
- *Extender length.* The line ends at the start of the last note, not through its duration (183). LilyPond ends it at the last head, which is close enough. Agree. NOTE.
- *Extender weight.* Ross: about the thickness of a period (183). Ours is LilyPond's 0.8 × line-thickness ≈ 0.38 pt. A Junicode period at our 10.5 pt lyric size measures 0.115 em ≈ 1.2 pt, so our extenders are about a third of the period weight. Visibly thin in the built *Już się zmierzka*. **CONSIDER** raising it (see §4.6).
- *Centring.* Words are centred on the head with a slight leftward bias for longer words (79). LilyPond centres geometrically. NOTE (marginal).
- *Text and hyphenation.* Lyrics hyphenated by a standard dictionary (A-50) and proofread separately for syllabification (A-23). Relevant to 11.1–11.2: Polish and Latin hyphenation should be checked against an authority, not by eye. CONSIDER as a check (§4.12).

**11.1–11.3 Text.** Silent.

**12 Chant.** Silent (the preface excludes square notation).

**13 Paratext.** Ross's sheet-music title block (A-6–A-9): title centred; subtitle smaller, centred below; lyricist flush left; composer flush right; source or dedication line above the title. This is the same arrangement as our performance masthead (house style 8). His subtitle is in parentheses, ours in italics. Agree. NOTE.

**14.2 Titles.** NOTE: Ross's title size, a point size of 3 ss (A-7), is for single sheet-music pages and does not bear on a book-like title page.

### House style

**§1 One place for design / per-score breaks.** Ross's casting-off rules (59–60) are the plate trade's equivalent of `prBreaksCritical/Performance`.
- *Already ours:* break at sections, keep repeats at system starts, keep endings together.
- *Missing:* "do not cast off staff lines that are uncrowded alongside" crowded ones, balancing characters and bars together (60, rule 8).
- *Long words:* lyrics with long words take precedence (60, rule 9).
- *Verdict:* CONSIDER a density check (§4.13).

**§2 Page.**
- *Margins:* we have A4 with 20/17/23 mm margins. Ross's minimum is ½ in (12.7 mm) on all sides (A-3). We exceed it. Agree.
- *Folio:* outside top, in the running head. Ross puts it top outer, near the printing area, and no smaller than 8–10 pt (A-3). Agree.
- *Proportion:* staff height to line width. Ross's concert page with No. 5 is 1 : 31½ (A-27). His choral octavo with No. 6 is about 1 : 26.6 (149 mm / 5.6 mm). Ours: critical 1 : 28.5 (170 / 5.97, before the 27 mm first-system indent), performance 1 : 25.4. Within the range of plate-engraved pages. Agree. NOTE.
- *Staff size:*
  - our 17 = No. 5 (Ross's "Large Middle": sheet music, band);
  - our 19 = No. 4 ("Peter": folios, organ);
  - Ross's choral size is No. 6 (≈5.6 mm) on a smaller octavo page.
  - On A4 the larger sizes are justified, and the proportion check above shows the pages are not over-scaled. Agree. NOTE.

**§3 Type.**
- *Lyric type:* ours is about 10.5 pt in the critical edition and 11.7 pt in the performance edition, both 2.47 ss. Ross: 9–10 pt, 1½–2¾ ss (A-25). Agree.
- *Bold:* Ross wants tempo marks in bold roman (A-8, A-46) and dynamics in bold italic (185). Our "no bold" rule is safe only while we print no tempo or dynamic marks in the score. NOTE.
- *Punched lyric letters:* narrower and taller than typewriter type (A-26). Junicode is a fairly narrow old-style face, which fits. NOTE.

**§5a Finish (`tools/novello_vector.py`).** The processing chapters bear on this most directly.

1. *Ink fills small acute white spaces.* Plate engravers avoided small white wedges between beam and staff line because "Years ago" they "became filled with ink" in printing (98–99). This is direct evidence of the effect our finish models.
   - *Gap:* our finish grows and closes each mark separately (`treat()` per drawing). The wedges that filled in the plate era lie between *different* marks: staff line and beam, staff line and notehead, line and slur end, flag and stem. They are not modelled.
   - *Proposal:* run the small closing step (the `r = 0.025 mm × k` buffer-out/buffer-in) once more on the union of each colour layer, so that acute white corners where two marks meet close slightly, as on a real pull.
   - *Risk:* small legitimate white spaces must survive: the counters of noteheads, the space between dot and line, and hairline gaps between the dashed Mensurstriche and the staff (0.5 ss = 0.75 mm, safe). Keep the radius under about 0.04 mm. CONSIDER.
2. *Line weight hierarchy.* In plate printing, before offset, "staff lines were thin, stems thicker, and barlines thickest" (151). LilyPond's defaults at our sizes give the same order:
   - staff line 0.47 pt;
   - stem 1.3× = 0.62 pt;
   - barline 1.9× = 0.90 pt;
   - leger line ≈ 0.90 pt ("somewhat thicker than the staff line": 182).
   - So our current weights already imitate the plate era, not the 1970 offset look (where stems are thinnest). Keep them. ADOPT as a stated rule: do not thin stems below the staff line.
3. *Uniform line, soft edge.* Ross: each line keeps its weight "for its entire length". A "flabby and distorted" stem is the first fault he names (82).
   - Our edge wander is up to about (0.016 + 0.022) × 1.5 ≈ 0.06 mm against a stem 0.22 mm wide (critical). That is up to about ±25% local width change, enough to read as a wobbly stem at reading distance.
   - On a plate the cut is straight. The softness comes from ink and paper (29: damp paper, paste ink, "not run").
   - *Proposal:* keep the edge softness, but limit low-frequency wander on long thin straight strokes (stems, staff lines, barlines, leger lines) to about 10% of their width. Check at 100% and 200% zoom. CONSIDER.
4. *Alloy and press.* More tin gives a sharper print (15). Paste ink is rubbed into a warmed plate; the paper is just damp enough to take ink without spreading (29). Gain was a controlled, small effect, not blotting. This supports our modest strength 1.5. NOTE.
5. *Dot sizes.* Older engraving used two or three dot sizes (augmentation normal, staccato smaller, sign and clef dots larger; 169). NOTE.
6. *Title letters.* Punched titles came out "somewhat uneven and out of alignment" (A-14). Publishers paid to avoid this, so imitating it would copy a fault. NOTE; do not imitate.
7. *Typographic staves.* The 16th-century partbook look (house style intro) is *type*, not plate: Ross stresses the staff-line breaks at every type join (6, 12). Our finish is plate-and-paper. If the series wanted the partbook surface, staff-line joins would be its signature, not ink gain. NOTE.

**§7 Headings and breaks / performance pages.** Ross's page-turn rules (A-3–A-5) support our "no short or crowded pages". In a choral performance edition the turn should fall where the voices rest or at a section end. Ross puts the rest *before* the turn (A-3–4), never after it. NOTE.

**§9 Score.**
- *Voice names:* abbreviated after the first system. Agree (A-32).
- *Bar numbers:* Ross is silent. NOTE.
- *Incipits and ambitus:* not discussed. NOTE.
- *Start of staff:* Ross's distances, left edge to left edge (144–146):
  - clef indent ½–1 ss;
  - clef to first key accidental 3½;
  - 1 ss per flat;
  - key to time signature 2½;
  - time signature to first note 3½;
  - accidental on the first note: the note moves ½ ss right.
  - Our start of staff is incipit, clef, ambitus, key, sign. LilyPond's break-align spacing is close but not tuned to these. CONSIDER checking one system against them.
- *Final and section bars:*
  - Ross: thick line ½ ss, gap ½ ss (147 n., 152).
  - LilyPond at staff 17: thick 6 × 0.47 pt = 2.84 pt = 0.67 ss; kern 3 × 0.47 = 1.42 pt = 0.33 ss.
  - So our final bar is heavier and tighter than the plate standard. The double bar lines are ¾ ss apart in Ross; in LilyPond 0.33 ss.
  - CONSIDER (§4.3).
- *System bracket:* ½ ss thick (155); LilyPond 0.45. Agree.
- *Volta:* numerals ≈2 ss tall with a dot; lines slightly heavier than staff lines (194–195). Our `VoltaBracket.font-size −2` makes the numerals smaller than 2 ss. NOTE.
- *Stanzas under the score, numbered:* Ross's hymn example (157) shows both stanzas under the notes with "1." and "2." at the left, which is what we do. Agree.

**Vertical spacing (pol-ren.ily "breathing room").**
- *Agree:*
  - we measure ink-to-ink clearance;
  - "staves are not always equidistant" (60);
  - every page is custom-made (70).
- *Values:*
  - Ross allows 1 ss clear to and from a lyric block (64–68); ours is 0.9× breathe to the lyric and 2.0× breathe from lyric to the next staff, more generous below.
  - Ross allows 2 ss per lyric line (64, 66); measured on *Już się zmierzka* our stanza line pitch is 2.79 ss in both editions (11.86 pt and 13.25 pt).
  - Ours is more open. That is reasonable for Polish diacritics and the larger type, but tighter than plate practice. NOTE.
- *Distributing surplus (69):*
  - Ross puts a small surplus between systems and a large one mainly *between staves within the system* (3–6 : 1).
  - Our structure prevents this: lilypond-book hands LaTeX one graphic per system, so all page stretch goes into `\systemskip` (4 mm plus 3 critical; 3 mm plus 4 performance) between systems.
  - A short page therefore opens its system gaps while the staves inside the systems stay tight. That is the reverse of Ross's preference, and in a choir score it detaches lyrics from the staff below less than it separates systems.
  - CONSIDER: where a page has more than about 6 ss of surplus per system gap, raise `prBreathe` for that score (the existing per-score lever) instead of letting LaTeX stretch the gaps between systems. Perhaps also cap the stretch in `\systemskip`.
- *Gap between systems:* Ross uses the same 1 ss clearance rule between systems as between staves (64, step 9). He does not ask for a markedly larger gap between systems. Our system gap is LilyPond's padding plus 3–4 mm. NOTE.

**§10 Tables and lists / §11 Language.** Silent.

## 4. Parameters we could encode

1. **Stems** stay at LilyPond's 3½ ss default (83). Minimum 2½ ss (83); flagged stems at least 3 ss (86). Already default. No change.
2. **Line weights:** staff < stem < barline ≈ leger line, the plate-era order (151, 182). Already default at sizes 17/19. Add a lint note: no override may thin `Stem.thickness` below 1.0.
3. **Final and double bars** (147 n., 152). In `pol-ren.ily`, `\layout { \context { \Score ... } }`:
   - `BarLine.thick-thickness`: Ross's ½ ss is 4.5 line-thicknesses at 17 and 4.85 at 19. Suggest `#4.6`.
   - `BarLine.kern`: ½ ss for "|." and ":|.". Suggest `#4.6`.
   - The double bar wants ¾ ss (152), about 6.8. LilyPond uses one `kern` for both, so a compromise of about 5 is possible, or a `\tweak` on section bars.
   - CONSIDER.
4. **Repeat sign:** thick ½, white ½, thin plus white ½, dots ½: 1½ ss in all (147 n.). Compare with LilyPond's `:|.` after (3).
5. **Leger lines:** less than ½ ss beyond the head on each side (182). LilyPond `LedgerLineSpanner.length-fraction 0.25` gives about 0.33 ss. Already conforming.
6. **Lyric extender:** about period weight (183).
   - Junicode period = 0.115 em. At 10.5 pt that is 1.21 pt = 2.55 line-thicknesses (critical); at 11.7 pt, 1.35 pt = 2.75 (performance).
   - Proposal: `\override LyricExtender.thickness = #2.0` in the `Lyrics` context. That gives about 0.95 pt: a little under the period, and about twice the weight of Junicode's hyphen stroke (0.59 pt).
   - Test 2.0 against 2.5 on a proof. CONSIDER.
   - The extender ends at the last note (183). Already default.
7. **Lyric hyphen** (no Ross value; for consistency with item 6). LilyPond draws it 0.62 pt thick and 0.42 ss above the baseline. Junicode's own hyphen is 0.59 pt thick with its centre about 2.8 pt above the baseline at 10.5 pt, which is 0.66 ss. If the extenders are thickened, check that the hyphen does not look lighter or lower than the text's own hyphen. NOTE.
8. **Small notes** (`\ed`): font-size −2 (cue size, 189) instead of −3. CONSIDER.
9. **Horizontal proportions** (77):
   - Ross: crotchet 3½, minim 4¾, semibreve 7¼ ss, a √2-like curve.
   - LilyPond's default is logarithmic: with our `base-shortest-duration` of 1/4, about 2.4 / 3.6 / 4.8 / 6.0 ss for semiminim / minim / semibreve / breve before stretching.
   - Ross's longer values get relatively more room (semibreve to minim 1.53 against LilyPond's 1.33).
   - In our minim-and-semibreve music, a closer match would be `SpacingSpanner.spacing-increment` raised slightly with `shortest-duration-space` lowered.
   - Lyrics drive most of our spacing anyway (79: "lyrical" punctuation). CONSIDER only as an experiment on a melismatic passage.
10. **Barline space:** 1 ss after a barline before the first note (74–75). For Mensurstriche: verify that no space is reserved in the staff for the invisible in-staff bar (see §3, 5.2–5.3). NOTE.
11. **Accidental to note:** about 1½ ss, left edge to left edge (131). Add no extra space for accidentals unless needed (76). LilyPond default is close. NOTE.
12. **Lyric syllabification check** (A-23, A-50). Run the Polish and Latin lyric hyphenation through a hyphenation dictionary: `hyphen`/LibreOffice `hyph_pl_PL`, and a classical Latin pattern set. List every split that differs, for review rather than automatic correction, since sung syllabification can differ from dictionary breaks. CONSIDER for `tools/`.
13. **Casting-off density check** (60, rule 8). From the LilyPond output, compute notes plus syllables per system width. Warn when adjacent systems on a page differ by more than about 40%. CONSIDER.
14. **Proofreading checklist** (A-23) as a release step:
    - bar counts equal in all voices;
    - vertical alignment;
    - every tie and slur across a system break;
    - lyrics proofed in a separate pass (capitals, spelling, punctuation, syllabification, melisma lines).
    - ADOPT the "separate lyric pass" into the release checklist; it costs nothing.
15. **Vertical surplus rule** (69). In the edition workflow: a page with more than about 6 ss of slack per system gap gets a raised `prBreathe`, not a stretched `\systemskip`. CONSIDER.
16. **Finish, cross-mark closing** (98–99), and **straight-stroke wander cap** (82). In `novello_vector.py`: an extra union-closing pass per colour layer (radius ≤ 0.04 mm × k), and a reduced low-frequency `wander` for thin straight strokes. CONSIDER.
17. **Rest grouping** (174–178). Under ¢ with breve bars, a performance-edition rest never spans the bar's midpoint unless it begins on it. Lint: flag a semibreve rest that starts on the second or fourth minim of a breve bar. CONSIDER (with 5.8).

## 5. Open questions for Miki

1. **Extender weight.** Ross wants lyric lines as heavy as a period; ours are about a third of that. Thicken to about 2× line-thickness?
2. **Final and double barlines.** Our thick line is 0.67 ss with a 0.33 ss gap; the plate standard is ½ and ½ (double bar lines ¾ ss apart). Change to Ross's proportions?
3. **Supplied-note size.** Keep `\ed` at grace-note size (−3), or move it to cue size (−2), the size Ross's trade used for notes meant to be sung?
4. **Where spare page height goes.** Ross opens the gaps *inside* a system before the gaps *between* systems; our LaTeX structure does the reverse. Use `prBreathe` per score to follow Ross, or accept the current look?
5. **Rests in the performance edition.** Add the tactus-boundary rule (no rest across the middle of a breve bar unless it starts there) to principles 5.8?
6. **Finish.** (a) Let ink close the small acute corners where two marks meet (line and head, line and slur), as Ross's wedge passage describes? (b) Cap the wobble on stems and staff lines so they stay "uniformly thick"?
7. **Horizontal spacing.** Worth one experiment tuning LilyPond toward Ross's proportions (more room for semibreves and breves relative to minims), or leave lyric-driven spacing as it is?
8. **Partbook versus plate.** The house style names the 1550 partbook as the model, but the finish imitates a plate pull. Ross makes the difference concrete: type gives broken staff lines; the plate gives continuous lines with ink gain. Is the plate look the intended one?
