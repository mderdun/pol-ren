# Stoquerus (ed. Rotola 1988) and Meconi, "Is Underlay Necessary?": reading notes

All page numbers are Rotola's printed pages. The introduction ("Intro") runs pp. 1–97. The treatise runs pp. 100–255, with the Latin on even pages and Rotola's English on odd pages. Quotations are from Rotola's translation.

## 1. Stoquerus, *De musica verbali libri duo*

### Date and context (per Rotola)

**Source.** The treatise survives only in Madrid BN MS 6486, a late-16th-century scribal copy. Ch. 31 breaks off mid-sentence ("Caetera desiderantur"). All the music examples are missing (pp. 1, 14–16, 95–96).

**Date.** Lowinsky dates it to 1567–77, "most likely sometime around 1570". The terminus post quem is Salinas's arrival at Salamanca; Salinas's 1577 print is not quoted (pp. 5–6).

**Author.** He was German, probably Kaspar Stocker. He was Salinas's student and assistant at Salamanca, and had earlier lived in Italy, "possibly in Venice" (pp. 6–7). Salinas asked him to write the rules for teaching at Salamanca (p. 113).

**Sources.** Gaffurius, Vicentino and Glareanus, above all Zarlino IV.33. The rules seem to rest on Zarlino as observed by "Josquin and Willaert" (p. 9).

**Structure.** Book I (chs. 1–9) defines the art and treats solmization. Book II (chs. 10–31) gives the rules (pp. 14–16).

**Authority of the rules (ch. 10).** The rules "existed before anything began to be written". A singer may "sing differently from what is written" (p. 149). Where the composer has departed from right reason, the singer supplies the deficiency "if it can be done conveniently" (p. 153), and acts as the composer to avoid "egregious faults" (p. 155).

### The fifteen rules

**A. Necessary rules (ch. 11, p. 159: "four or five rules that are necessarily observed by all")**

- **N1 (ch. 12, pp. 161–165).** Never more syllables than notes. Puncti, the non-initial notes of a ligature, and most semiminims and smaller values do not count as notes here. Singers who spend notes too early are forced at the end to divide notes or drop syllables. The converse, more notes than syllables, is *not* necessary: Willaert's school avoided it and the older composers did not (p. 163).
- **N2 (ch. 14, pp. 171–175).** A punctus takes its note's syllable. It may be split off only when the case is clearly an error and no dissonance lies hidden under the dot. In doubt, keep the rule (p. 175).
- **N3 (ch. 13, pp. 167–171).** A ligature takes one syllable, on its first note. It may be broken only to correct a notational error. Its first note sometimes takes no syllable, when it continues an implied ligature from the preceding note.
- **N4 (ch. 15, pp. 175–181).** Notes repeated "in one and the same place" each take their own syllable. For the one limitation, see "Repeated notes" below.
- **N5 (ch. 16, pp. 181–183).** The first syllable goes on the first note of a phrase and the last syllable on the last note. With a notated ligature, the last syllable "starts where the figure of the ligature begins". Implied ligatures fall to the discretionary rules.

Ch. 17 (pp. 183–185) adds that anyone "skilled in the rules" can correct errors by composers or scribes. Stoquerus sometimes renumbers the ligature and punctus rules by their order of discussion (Rotola p. 229 n. 212). Citing them by chapter avoids the confusion.

**B. Discretionary rules of the older composers (listed pp. 201–203)**

"Discretionary" means the judgment of a good musician acting by right reason (pp. 187–189).

- **O1 (ch. 20, pp. 203–207).** "To the accented penultimate or antepenultimate syllable of any word many semiminimae can be adjoined." In practice this means the phrase accent on the last word.
- **O2 (ch. 21, pp. 209–211).** A syllable is "frequently" given to a semiminim standing alone. If so, the following note must have one too. If the second of a run of equal semiminims is texted, all of them are. After a semiminim followed by smaller notes, the first of those takes a syllable and the rest are tied to it.
- **O3 (ch. 22, pp. 213–217).** The two notes that complete the punctus of a semibreve or minim take no syllable, "nor frequently" does the next note.
- **O4 (ch. 23, pp. 217–221).** A pair of minims or semiminims shares one syllable, on the first note. That is the note that begins the tactus, or for semiminims the one on the thesis or arsis. A syncopated cadential semibreve may go untexted. This rule belongs only to the older composers.
- **O5 (ch. 24, pp. 221–227).** A run of semiminims or smaller notes takes one syllable, on its first note. After a dotted minim the dot counts as the first semiminim, so the syllable stays on the minim. The longer note after the run should also go untexted, although the older composers often texted it.

Which of O2, O4 and O5 applies depends on how many syllables are available. Plenty of syllables gives one per semiminim; one syllable gives one for the whole run; anything in between gives pairs (pp. 225–227). Under **C**, the moderns use semiminims as minims, so the rules apply at fusa level (p. 227).

**Ch. 25: procedure (pp. 227–233).** Stoquerus sets out an order of work:

1. Put the first syllable on the first note.
2. Place the middle syllables "as soon as can be conveniently done". Keep them off puncti and ligatures, and text repeated notes.
3. Give a semiminim standing among larger notes its own syllable, "unless this occurs in formal clausulae".
4. Text runs of semiminims by O2, O4 or O5, according to the syllables available.
5. Give whatever notes remain to the accented syllable. If enough remain to repeat the phrase, repeat it with "no length on the accented syllable" (p. 231).

Frequent repetition breeds "aversion and disgust". Repetition is justified only to let the voices catch up, or rarely for emphasis (p. 231).

**C. Discretionary rules of the newer composers (listed p. 239)**

The moderns keep these rules almost as strictly as the necessary ones (p. 237).

- **R1 (ch. 27, pp. 241–243).** Every minim and larger note takes a syllable. Under C, semiminims act as minims. Under ¢ they may also, when all voices move in semiminims, when the semiminims leap, or when several syllables are written under them. In formal clausulae the minim often goes untexted.
- **R2 (ch. 28, pp. 243–247).** A run of semiminims and smaller notes takes one syllable, "regardless of their number", because their speed would cause "a great slurring of syllables". The same applies to minims in triple proportion (p. 245).
- **R3 (ch. 29, pp. 247–251).** The note after the run keeps the run's syllable, because the speed of delivery is judged "from the end of the delivery". The syllable may continue onto the next note as well, so that the next syllable falls on the thesis. When a run comes before the final note, the last syllable falls on the run's *first* semiminim (p. 251).
- **R4 (ch. 30, p. 253).** Avoid repetition, "more, however, of words than phrases". Weighty phrases may be repeated rarely, for emphasis.
- **R5 (ch. 31, p. 255).** "Short notes must be granted to short syllables, long notes to long syllables." The chapter breaks off after its first sentence.

**Necessary versus optional.** Group A is necessary (p. 159). Groups B and C are discretionary and change only when "right reason suggests something else" (p. 187). Group C has near-necessary force among the moderns (p. 237).

### Older and younger composers

- **Josquin and Willaert.** Josquin leads the older music. Willaert is its end and the "father" of a "new music". The new music "wholly removed that erratic freedom of the older composers". Its followers include Lasso and Rore, and its printed form is *Le pecorine* (p. 195).
- **Same rules, applied more strictly.** The moderns invented nothing new; they "removed their excessive license" (p. 197).
- **Older practice.** The older composers gave "notes of any number and kind" to one syllable (p. 241). They set any number of syllables to semiminim runs (p. 245).
- **Underlaying older music.** It is harder: the singer faces "many roads" (pp. 237–239).
- **No national label.** Stoquerus never says "Netherlanders". Rotola calls the split one of degree (Intro pp. 63–64) and dates Willaert's new style from the late 1530s (Intro p. 63).

### Specific topics

- **Accent.** In speech an accent takes "more length", so in song it "can be granted more notes" (pp. 197, 203). Word-accent precepts "are not fittingly carried over to music". The phrase accent, "almost the same as that of the last word", is what counts, as when church lectors slow on the last accent of a sentence (p. 205).
- **Phrase.** For unskilled musicians, a phrase is whatever ends with a rest or formal clausula (p. 205).
- **Quantity.** Covered by R5 (p. 239). Solmization ignores quantity and accent; texted singing must not (p. 113). Ch. 31 is lost (p. 255).
- **Melismas.** O1 puts them on the phrase-final accent (pp. 201, 207). Extra notes may also arise from necessity (imitation, completing a tempus, catching up) or from "a more agreeable harmony" (p. 165). That can mean impressing a weighty word, or simply that "the melody itself sounds much fuller and nicer" (p. 165). Singers may embellish (glossare, diminuere, colorare), starting on the plain note's syllable (pp. 165, 223). A remainder that is too long should be turned into a repeat of the phrase (p. 231).
- **Repeated notes.** N4 (pp. 175–181). The limitation (p. 179) is narrow. It applies only when two same-pitch notes begin with a semiminim or smaller, *and* that note is preceded by a note or punctus of like value. Then either the second note alone takes a syllable or, preferably, neither does. A semiminim "put down alone" takes a syllable "necessarily" (p. 181), and so does the note after it (Intro p. 55).
- **Different pitches.** In principle each takes its own syllable, but not necessarily, because implied ligatures exist (p. 177).
- **Semiminims and fusae.**
  - The moderns "give no syllables at all to semiminimae" under ¢ (p. 221), except the first of a run (p. 249).
  - Fusae take syllables "much less" (p. 223).
  - Under C, everything shifts down one level (pp. 227, 243).
- **Ligatures.** Covered by N3. The minim rule rests on the claim that ligatures were invented to mark exceptions (p. 241).
- **Text repetition.** pp. 165, 231, 239, 253. Plainsong has none.
- **Penultimate syllable.** If the antepenult carries the accent, the penult takes the penultimate note; otherwise the penult counts as a middle syllable (p. 207).
- **Tactus.** A syllable goes on the note that begins the tactus, or for semiminims on the thesis or arsis, because consonance is managed there. The other notes are "of practically no importance" (p. 219). Lowinsky calls the tactus criterion new (Intro p. 80 n.).
- **Barbarism.** Bad underlay risks a "barbarism", which Rotola reads as wrong accentuation (p. 119; Intro p. 25).
- **Not found:** monosyllables, conjunctions, vowels, elision, rests dividing words.

### Rotola's own conclusions

- Lowinsky's exposition drew "a clear line of division" between arbitrary and rational editing (Intro pp. 3–4).
- Stoquerus shows "a fondness" for the moderns. Performers and editors, "functioning as composers with Stoquerus's blessing", may apply the older rules (Intro p. 47). The extension to *editors* is Rotola's, not Stoquerus's.
- N4 is, as far as is known, first given separate treatment by Stoquerus (Intro p. 52 n. 88).
- Ch. 31 might have exempted cases where counterpoint forces a long syllable onto a short note. This is conjecture (p. 255 n. 268).
- In ch. 25, "third" is Stoquerus's slip for the second discretionary rule (p. 229 n. 214).

## 2. Meconi

**Citation.** Honey Meconi, "Is underlay necessary?", ch. 40, pp. 284–91. The PDF does not name the volume. It is probably T. Knighton and D. Fallows (eds.), *Companion to Medieval and Renaissance Music* (London: Dent, 1992). This is my inference; verify it.

**Argument.** In music of the 15th and early 16th centuries, the problem lies "more with our desire for finality" than with the music (p. 290). Underlay probably varied with each performance, "more akin to tempo, articulation, performing pitch, and dynamics than to musica ficta" (pp. 287–88).

**Findings.**

- **Harran's appendix (pp. 284–85).** Meconi lists its twelve most-cited statements on words and notes. They include:
  - stressed syllables on long notes (29 citations);
  - one syllable per ligature (22);
  - no syllable on the first large note after semiminims (7, Lanfranco to Burmeister 1606);
  - syllables on the semiminim and white note after a dotted minim "sometimes" (6, Lanfranco to Tigrini 1588).
- **When the rules date from.** The specific rules date from 1533 onwards, the period when they are "least" needed (p. 285).
- **Stocker on Josquin.** Stocker's obligatory rules "go for the Josquin generation as well", and he would "improve upon the composer". Meconi comments that "a rare editor" would accept that (p. 285).
- **Inconsistent sources.** Even autographs (Isaac, Berlin 40021) and Habsburg-Burgundian court manuscripts are inconsistent. This suggests leeway, or "differing practices in different parts of Europe" (pp. 285–86).
- **Singers.** Singers knew the texts by heart and handled underlay themselves (p. 286).
- **Composers and theorists.** Composers could write syllabically and mostly chose not to. Theorists before 1533 are silent (p. 286). Josquin was soon "old school" (pp. 286–87).
- **Two assumptions to drop.**
  - That precise underlay is better. Before Lanfranco, composers "often preferred melismatic to syllabic settings, moving melodies to repeated notes", and text could be "a foil for musical interplay" (p. 287).
  - That a single solution exists (p. 287).
- **Twelve performer questions (p. 288).** These include regional Latin pronunciation, individual voices, and "simultaneity of vowel sound", which "vastly aids intonation".
- **Conclusions.**
  - The 16th-century rules "make a good starting place", and many underlays were acceptable (pp. 288–89).
  - Vocalization and solmization deserve trial; Stocker notes that imperfect musicians "are content with solmization" (p. 289).
  - Editions could be distributed on disk to escape "the tyranny of editing" (p. 290).

## 3. Against our rules (principles §10, §1.4)

- **10.1.**
  - R1 supports minims and larger notes taking syllables (p. 239).
  - Stoquerus splits the dotted-minim semiminim case by generation:
    - O2: a lone semiminim takes a syllable "frequently", and the next note must then have one too (p. 201).
    - O3: the two notes completing a punctus take none (p. 201).
    - Moderns under ¢: no syllables on semiminims (p. 221).
  - Under **C**, semiminims behave as minims (pp. 227, 243). This matters for any source in C.
  - In formal clausulae the minim, or the syncopated semibreve, may go untexted (pp. 221, 243).
  - Meconi's tally has "sometimes" for the semiminim case (p. 285).
- **10.2.** Supported by N5 and the definition of a phrase (pp. 183, 205).
- **10.3.** Supported, with two qualifications. With a ligature, the last syllable starts at its head (p. 183). After a run, it starts on the run's first semiminim (p. 251).
- **10.4, 10.5, 10.11, 10.14.** Not found in either text.
- **10.6.**
  - Melisma "fuller and nicer" apart from the words: supported (p. 165).
  - The stress clause is qualified: phrase accent counts over word accent (p. 205).
  - Weighing by ear rather than counting: supported by Meconi (pp. 287–89).
  - Vowels and light words: not found.
- **10.7.** Matches O1: the melisma goes on the accented syllable of the last word, and the final syllable on the last note (pp. 201, 207).
- **10.8.** Consistent with O1's "penultimate or antepenultimate" (p. 201). Nothing on Kraków.
- **10.9.**
  - The attribution is correct: N4, ch. 15.
  - "Unless semiminims" is **over-broad**. The exception needs a preceding note or punctus of the same value; a lone semiminim and the note after it are both texted (pp. 179–181).
  - Suggested wording: "Repeated notes take a new syllable, except where the first is a semiminim or smaller joined to a preceding note or punctus of like value (Stoquerus, ch. 15)."
  - "Melismas replaced by repeating the clause": supported (p. 231).
- **10.10.** Partly supported. Imitation (*fuga*) justifies extra notes, and singers may divide or compress notes so as "to provide imitation" (p. 165).
- **10.12.** Stoquerus takes the opposite stance: the singer emends departures from the rules (pp. 149–155). Two of his own rules counsel caution, though. In doubt, the punctus rule stands (p. 175). A ligature is broken only for a clear notational error (p. 167). Meconi notes that few editors accept emending (p. 285). Keep 10.12 as our policy, not as Stoquerus's.
- **10.13.** Qualified. Dividing a note to fit syllables is the fault N1 guards against (pp. 163, 175), although singers may divide notes for imitation (p. 165).
- **§1.4.** Supported by Meconi (pp. 287–89) and by p. 165.

**Citations in our texts** (*Nunc scio* critical.tex ll. 140–148, 222–223; the archived choral .ly):

- **"c. 1570, ch. 15": correct.** Ch. 15 is the necessary rule on repeated notes, and the date is Lowinsky's as reported by Rotola (p. 6). Meconi gives "c. 1570–80" (p. 290).
- **"unless it is a semiminim": over-broad**, as under 10.9.
- **"the latitude Stoquerus allows the older generation … the Netherlanders": misattribution.**
  - Stoquerus's older group is "headed by Josquin" (p. 195).
  - He calls its freedom "erratic" and "excessive license" (pp. 195, 197, 211). That is not latitude he grants, although he does give rules for older music (pp. 201–233).
  - "Netherlanders" and Wacław's kinship with that group are our claims.
  - By date, Wacław belongs to the era of Willaert's new music, with Lasso and Rore as its followers (p. 195; Intro p. 63).
  - Suggested wording: "the discretionary rules Stoquerus gives for the older composers, Josquin's generation".
- **"Repeated whole … (Stoquerus)": supported** (pp. 231, 239, 253).
- **Vicentino's "second white note" point.** Stoquerus says the same (R3, pp. 239, 249) and adds that the older composers often neglected it (p. 225).
- **Name.** Rotola's title page and cataloguing read "**Caspar** Stoquerus"; our bibliography has "Gaspar".

## 4. The editor's tendencies

- **(a) Light words off the beat.** Not found: neither text mentions *et*, *in*, *de* or other monosyllables. The related mechanics point both ways:
  - O4 puts the syllable of a pair on the on-beat note, the off-beat notes being "of practically no importance" (pp. 199, 219). This goes *against* (a) for paired notes.
  - O2 (p. 201; Rotola p. 209 n. 160) gives an off-beat syllable on a lone semiminim, typically after a dotted note, leading into a texted longer note. This is the pattern (a) describes.
  - R3 lets a syllable be prolonged so that the next one lands on the thesis (p. 251).
  - Lowinsky, quoted by Rotola: a syncopated note's "ambiguous play between pull and stress… is lost when the syncopated note receives a syllable" (Intro p. 78 n. 131).
- **(b) Open vowels in melismas.** Not found. Meconi mentions only "simultaneity of vowel sound" for intonation, and singers' voices (p. 288).
- **(c) Momentum.** Not found as a concept. The nearest are O2 (p. 201) and the argument that delivery is judged by where the syllable ends, which carries it onto the longer note (p. 249).
- **(d) Long melismas need not sit on the stress.**
  - Supported: melody "sounds much fuller and nicer" with extra notes, apart from the words (p. 165).
  - The older composers set "notes of any number and kind" to one syllable (p. 241).
  - Word accent "not fittingly carried over to music" (p. 205).
  - Meconi: melismatic settings were preferred, and text could be "a foil for musical interplay" (p. 287).
  - Qualified by O1, which ties the phrase-final melisma to the accent (pp. 201, 207), and by the advice to repeat the phrase rather than over-extend (p. 231).

## 5. Relevance

- **Not a German witness.** Stoquerus was German but wrote in Spain, on the Italian model of Zarlino and Willaert (pp. 6–9, 195). He is not evidence of German or Polish usage. His German content is limited to glosses ("colorare", p. 165; "den Text singen", p. 117). Rotola points to Finck, *Practica musica* (Wittenberg 1556), for Stoquerus's distinction between ars and usus (Intro p. 20 n.). He also cites Heyden (p. 171 n.) and Listenius and Dressler (Intro p. 67 n.); these are the Central European leads.
- **Wacław (1550s).** Stoquerus offers two frames: Willaert's strict new style, or the older rules. Choosing between them is an editorial decision.
- **1611.** The mensuration sign matters: under C, semiminims act as minims (pp. 227, 243). In triple proportion, minims behave like semiminims (p. 245).
- **Latin motets.** These are Stoquerus's explicit case: Willaert's songs are "some in Latin" (p. 195), and church lectors are his model for phrase accent (p. 205).
- **Untexted or block-texted sources.**
  - The ch. 25 procedure (pp. 227–233) is a working algorithm for texting voices.
  - Stoquerus licenses the singer to supply what the source lacks (pp. 153–155).
  - Meconi argues for several acceptable underlays and for offering alternatives (pp. 287–90).

## 6. Leads

- **Lowinsky, "A Treatise on Text Underlay by a German Disciple of Francisco de Salinas"**, *Festschrift Besseler* (1961), pp. 231–51. The first study, translating key passages (Intro p. 1).
- **Lowinsky, *Medici Codex* (1968), 3:90–107.** Stoquerus applied in an edition, with worked cases (Intro p. 2 n.; pp. 50, 54, 78 nn.).
- **Harran, *Word-Tone Relations* (1986), pp. 218–58.** Its appendix of 116 rules is Meconi's base (Intro p. 2 n.; Meconi pp. 284–85).
- **Harran, "New Light" (1973), "Vicentino and His Rules" (1973) and "In Pursuit of Origins" (1978).** The earlier rules that Stoquerus builds on.
- **Reese and Jones, "Textunterlegung", *MGG* 16 (1979), cols. 1843–52.** Stoquerus-based, with musical examples for nearly every rule (Intro pp. 46–94 nn.).
- **German witnesses:** Finck, *Practica musica* (1556); Heyden, *De arte canendi* (1540); Burmeister (1606) (Intro p. 20 n.; p. 171 n.; Meconi p. 285).
- **Practical digests:** Atlas on Luchini (*JM* 1983); Duffin and Slobin, "Rules for Text Underlay ca. 1440–ca. 1590" (1983); Jeppesen, *Counterpoint*, pp. 159–60, on repeated notes (Intro pp. 3, 52 nn.).
