# Review of principle 10.1 (syllables on short notes)

4 October 2026, for Miki's second *Vox in Rama* review. The question is whether 10.1 ("a new syllable only on a minim or longer, or on a semiminim straight after a dotted minim … never on a fusa or a dot") rests on overwhelming evidence or on contested evidence. Sources: our reading notes in `docs/research/literature/` (towne-harran, stoquerus-meconi, mitchell-fitch, caldwell, grier). Key points were checked against the PDFs: Harrán 1973a and 1973b, Towne 1990 and 1991, Rotola's Stoquerus and Meconi. Page numbers are the printed ones. All values are under ¢ unless stated. *Vox* is in ¢, in the print's values, with a breve bar (edition, "Pitch, values and barring").

**Verdict.** 10.1 is **not** overwhelmingly supported as worded. Its core is well attested: minims carry syllables, dots and fusae do not, and a semiminim run normally takes one syllable. But its blanket ban on a new syllable on any semiminim is stricter than every theorist it cites. That includes Stoquerus's rules for the "modern" composers, which are the strictest of all. The theorists agree that the first semiminim of a run takes the run's syllable. Several of them also allow syllables on semiminims and on the note after a run under stated conditions. The scholars who tested the rules against music (Harrán, Towne, Meconi, Mitchell) treat them as a starting point, not a law.

---

## 1. Where 10.1 comes from, theorist by theorist

10.1 is a composite. Its "minim or longer" core is Lanfranco's rule II as restated by Zarlino. Its strict ¢ wording is Stoquerus's account of the modern composers. Its one exception (the semiminim after a dotted minim) is Lanfranco's rule V. Each theorist in turn:

- **Lanfranco, *Scintille* (1533).**
  - Rule II: every separate note carries a syllable, "excepting, almost always, the semiminim" (Harrán 1973a, 35). The "almost" marks real exceptions (36).
  - Rule IV: no syllable on a dot (39).
  - Rule V: "only on rare occasions" a syllable goes on the semiminim after a dotted minim, and then on the next white note as well (40).
  - **Rule VI: "the initial semiminim [of a series] must necessarily carry its own syllable."** The middle and last semiminims do not, and nor does the white note after the run. French-chanson imitations are excepted (41). By Harrán's reading, this is a rule about *where* a run's syllable goes: on its first note (46).
- **Vicentino, *L'antica musica* (1555)**, as reported by Harrán 1973b:
  - Rule 1: after semiminims or fusae, a new syllable goes on the *second* white note, not the first. Breaking this is a "barbarism" (622).
  - Rule 2: near rests or at a phrase end, "by necessity", a syllable on the first minim after the run "will not be so great an error". Harrán finds this "frequent enough" in early sixteenth-century music, though not in Vicentino's own madrigals (622–623).
  - Rule 3: a syllable on the black note of a dotted figure is "no great fault" (623).
  - Towne lists rule 2 as his rule 19 (1990, 273).
- **Zarlino, *Istitutioni* (1558), IV.33**, as reported by Harrán 1973a:
  - Rule I: no syllable on the semiminim "and all notes smaller than it" (35).
  - Rule IV: only "rarely" on any semiminim, smaller note, or the note that follows it (40).
  - Rule V: no syllable on notes after a dot that are *smaller* than the dot (40–41).
  - Rule VI: if a semiminim takes a syllable, the next note may take one too (41).
  - Harrán reads V–VI as permitting a syllable on a note equal to the dot (the semiminim after a dotted minim). He adds that this happened "more often than" Lanfranco's "rare" (41). He also says that by Zarlino's time, syllables on *undotted* lone semiminims did not occur under ¢. Under C they were common from the 1540s, in note nere madrigals (40). This is the strongest support for a restrictive reading. It concerns the lone semiminim, not the first note of a run.
- **Stoquerus, *De musica verbali* (c. 1570; Rotola ed.)**: two generations.
  - **Older composers (Josquin's generation):**
    - A lone semiminim "frequently" takes a syllable, and then the next note must take one (O2, p. 201).
    - If a texted semiminim is followed by smaller notes, the first of those takes its own syllable (ch. 21, p. 211).
    - The two notes completing a dot take none (O3, p. 201).
    - Pairs of semiminims share a syllable on the accented note (O4, pp. 217–221).
    - The older composers "often" texted the note after a run (pp. 225, 251).
  - **Modern composers (Willaert onwards):**
    - They give all minims syllables and "no syllables at all to semiminimae" (ch. 23, p. 221).
    - In the same treatise, a run's syllable "is always adjoined to the first" semiminim (ch. 29, p. 249), and a syllable "is usually granted to the first semiminima" (ch. 24, p. 221). That first note is the one on the thesis or arsis.
    - Under ¢, individual semiminims may take syllables if all voices move in semiminims, if the run leaps, or if several syllables are written under it (R1, ch. 27, p. 243).
    - The note after a run keeps the run's syllable. The moderns keep this "no less than the necessary rules" (R3, p. 251).
    - When a run precedes the last note, the last syllable goes on the run's first semiminim (p. 251).
  - **Under C:** semiminims act as minims (pp. 227, 243).
  - **Necessary rules, for all composers:**
    - A dot takes no syllable (N2, ch. 14, pp. 171–175).
    - Repeated pitches each take a syllable (N4, ch. 15, pp. 175–181). The exception is narrow: the first note must be a semiminim preceded by a note of like value. A semiminim "put down alone" on a repeated pitch takes a syllable "necessarily" (p. 181).
  - **Fusae** take syllables "much less" (p. 223).
- **Later German theorists** (via Towne 1990):
  - Calvisius, *Melopoeia* (1592):
    - semiminims in a run may take separate syllables if the line leaps;
    - the larger note after a run "may require its own syllable", especially as the phrase's penultimate (272).
  - Burmeister (1606): small notes may take long or short syllables, "since their speed obscures the accent" (282).
  - These are the writers nearest in date to *Vox* (1611).
- **Tally.** Meconi's count of Harrán's appendix includes:
  - no syllable on the first larger note after a run of semiminims: 7 citations, Lanfranco to Burmeister;
  - the semiminim and white note after a dotted minim "sometimes" texted: 6 citations, Lanfranco to Tigrini 1588 (Meconi, 285).

**So Miki is half right.** The strict "only on a minim" reading is Stoquerus's description of the moderns (p. 221). Even Stoquerus puts the run's syllable on its first semiminim (pp. 221, 249), and he lists conditions under which semiminims take syllables (p. 243). The dotted-minim exception is Lanfranco's, not Stoquerus's.

## 2. What modern scholars find in the music

- **Harrán 1973a.**
  - The semiminim after a dotted minim was texted "more often than" the theorists' "rare" (41).
  - Lanfranco wrote for Masses and motets, taking Willaert as his model (51–53).
  - No counts are given.
- **Harrán 1973b.** Vicentino breaks his own rules often in his 1546 madrigals (622–630). His 1572 book gives syllables to all semiminims (630).
- **Harrán 1997.** "No firm conclusions" about practice can be drawn from the prescriptions alone (251). Strict accent and underlay were expectations of the mid-century, and even then they were not observed scrupulously (243, 257–260).
- **Towne 1990, systematic rules** (Latin sacred music):
  - R11: semiminims generally take no syllable. A lone semiminim "generally receives its own syllable", and so does the next note (268).
  - R16: a run takes one syllable, which "should begin on the first semiminim". It may begin on the longer note before. It may begin on the first semiminim only if that note is accented relative to the next (271).
  - The note after the run keeps the run's syllable in modern works, "but not always" in older ones. It "may require its own syllable", especially as the penultimate (Vicentino, Calvisius) (271–272).
  - R18: individual syllables on a run if all voices move in semiminims or the line leaps (272).
  - **A semiminim syllable followed by smaller notes:** the smaller notes then receive their own syllable (273; Table VII 17f in Towne 1991, 155).
  - **Dotted figures:** R22 lets the semiminim after a dotted minim take a syllable; Stocker says the next note "must" too (277). In the figure dotted minim–semiminim–minim plus a fourth minim, either minim "may be divided into semiminims and still bear a syllable" (280).
  - Syncopated cadential notes take no new syllable in older works; the moderns treat them equally (R25, 278).
- **Towne 1991, editor's digest:**
  - 17a–f: the run options (155).
  - 19b–d: the dotted figure takes three syllables if necessary, two if unavoidable, and "never" a syllable on the second note without one on the third (155).
  - **19f: syllables "may fall in the same places" if the last minims are split into semiminims (156).**
  - 19g: no syllable on two notes smaller than the dot, nor on the longer note after them (156).
  - Repeated notes take separate syllables, except in the ornament of a cadential suspension, where a new syllable is optional (154).
  - On the Albertis Masses: many passages have several defensible solutions (151, 166). His "chattering semiminims" each take a syllable (164).
  - Lowinsky ranks the ban on a syllable after a run above "last syllable on the last note". Towne calls that ranking "disputable" (145 n. 8).
- **Mitchell 2025** (c. 1400–60):
  - There was "little or no standardisation" (1, 177), and theory "followed practice rather than establishing it" (178).
  - He reports Stocker's older rule 2 and the ban on the note after semiminims (pp. 6, 8–9).
  - He gives no statistics on semiminims. The period is earlier than ours.
- **Meconi:**
  - The sixteenth-century rules "make a good starting place" (288).
  - Many underlays were acceptable (288–289).
  - The problem lies with "our desire for finality" (290).
  - Composers after 1533 wrote syllabically, with "repeated notes" common (285).
- **Fitch** treats text setting as "subject to competing priorities" (106).

None of these studies counts how often semiminims are texted in sacred music under ¢. The disagreement is between theorists, and between theory and the composers' own works (Vicentino). It is not settled by data.

## 3. Overwhelming or contested?

- **Firm.** Every source agrees, and nothing in the music contradicts them:
  - no syllable on a dot: Lanfranco IV, Zarlino III, Stoquerus N2 (a necessary rule);
  - none on notes smaller than the dot they follow: Zarlino V; Towne 1991, 156;
  - none on the middle or last semiminims of a run, unless one of the conditions applies;
  - a texted lone semiminim passes a syllable to the next note: Lanfranco V, Zarlino VI, Stoquerus O2, Towne 1991 19d.
- **Nearly firm: no fusa under ¢.** Zarlino I; Stoquerus p. 223. The one exception is Stoquerus's older-generation case of a texted semiminim followed by smaller notes (p. 211; Towne 1990, 273). It is too rare to adopt.
- **Contested or wrong.**
  - "Never on a semiminim except after a dotted minim" goes against Lanfranco VI, Stoquerus pp. 221, 243 and 249, and Towne R16–R18.
  - The note-after-a-run rule is a strong modern tendency, with stated licences: Vicentino 2, Calvisius, and older practice.
- **Mensuration.** Under C everything moves down a level (Stoquerus pp. 227, 243; Towne 1990, 270). 10.1 already says so.

## 4. Polish context

- **Wacław's *Lamentationes* (1553).** 10.7 describes them as "mostly syllabic, repeated notes take new syllables". This is our own reading from the *Nunc scio* work, not a published study. It supports Stoquerus's N4 over the semiminim ban where the two conflict. We have no count of semiminim syllables in the print. Mitchell's methods would make one possible (Munn's eligibility count; mitchell-fitch notes §4).
- **Perz 1984** (cited by Grier, 134 n. 61) covers fourteenth- and fifteenth-century underlay in Polish sources. It has not been read and is earlier than our repertory.
- **Other Polish and Central European material.**
  - Mitchell cites the Glogau partbooks (Kraków BJ 40098), which are "fully texted" (146 n. 257), and Kras (174). Both are fifteenth-century.
  - Fitch cites Gallus's *Moralia* (Prague 1596) for "speech-like declamation" (163). That is the nearest Central European parallel in date.
- **Stoquerus as evidence.** He is a German who wrote in Spain on Zarlino's model. He is not evidence of German or Polish usage (Rotola, 6–9). The German theorists nearest in date to 1611 (Calvisius, Burmeister) are more permissive about short notes, not less.
- **Conclusion.** There is no Polish evidence for the strict reading.

## 5. Proposed wording of 10.1

> 1. **Note values.** Under ¢. Under C every value counts one level down: semiminims as minims, fusae as semiminims (Stoquerus, Rotola ed., 227, 243; Harrán 1973a, 40). *Firm:*
>    - never a new syllable on a dot;
>    - never on a note smaller than the dot it follows;
>    - never on a fusa;
>    - never on a middle or last note of a semiminim run, except as in (c);
>    - a lone semiminim that takes a syllable passes one to the next note.
>
>    (Lanfranco IV–VI, Zarlino I, III, V–VI, in Harrán 1973a, 35–41; Stoquerus 171–175, 201, 223; Towne 1991, 155–156.)
>
>    *Default:* a new syllable on a minim or longer, preferring the longer note where the music allows.
>
>    *Allowed, with the ground named in the critical note:*
>    - (a) a new syllable on the first semiminim of a run, where it falls on a minim beat; the run and the following white note then keep it (Lanfranco VI, Harrán 1973a, 41; Stoquerus 221, 249; Towne 1990, 271);
>    - (b) a lone semiminim after a dotted minim (Lanfranco V, Vicentino, Zarlino V–VI: Harrán 1973a, 40–41; 1973b, 623), or on a repeated pitch after a longer note (10.9; Stoquerus 179–181), or where a minim after a dotted figure is split (Towne 1991, 156, 19f);
>    - (c) separate syllables on a run where all voices declaim in semiminims or the line leaps (Stoquerus 243; Towne 1990, 272);
>    - (d) a new syllable on the white note straight after a run, only at a phrase end where the words leave no other place (Vicentino rule 2, Harrán 1973b, 622–623; Calvisius, Towne 1990, 272);
>    - (e) any placement the period source shows (10.12).
>
>    The strict ¢ practice is that of Willaert's successors (Stoquerus 221, 251); older licences are used only with a reason. (MD, [date])

This keeps the old rule's whole firm content. It makes the semiminim cases explicit, conditional and citable. It also gives the analyser (U101) something concrete to test: the beat position of the run's first note, a repeated pitch, a phrase end.

## 6. Miki's three blocked *Vox* edits

All three readings are Marchesano's (M), as the critical notes record (bars 31–33 A, 36–37 T, 38–40 T). The edition says the print's Tenor "places its text … loosely" and that "its words fall where M's do". Before any of these is printed under (e), check the facsimile spacing at Tenor bars 36–40.

**1. Altus 32.4, *con-* on g′.** The g′ is the first of four semiminims after the plain minim f♯′; *so-* follows at 33.3.
- **Allowed under (a).** g′ is on a minim beat and accented against the a′ that follows. The run and the a′ at 33.2 keep *con-*, and *so-* takes the second white note, exactly as Lanfranco VI, Vicentino 1 and Stoquerus R3 require.
- It also fits Towne's 19f: the bar opens with dotted minim, semiminim, minim, and the fourth minim is split.
- **The present reading is worse by the theorists' own rules.** It puts *con-* on a′ straight after the run (Vicentino's "barbarism"). It also gives the run to *-it*, a stopped syllable, which goes against 10.6.
- **Adopt.**

**2. Tenor 36–37, *con-so-la-ri*.** *Con-* is on g′ (36.4), *-so-* on e′ (37.1), *-la-* on the semiminim d′ (37.2), and *-ri* on b (37.3), straight after d′ c′.
- ***-la-* is allowed under (a):** it is the first of a two-note run, on a minim beat, and the stressed syllable.
- ***-ri* on b needs (d).** b is the phrase's last note before *et noluit*, and the print's words leave no other place:
  - the four syllables have only g′, e′, d′ and b;
  - Stoquerus's alternative, the last syllable on the first semiminim (p. 251), would leave three syllables for two notes.
  - This is Vicentino's "by necessity" case. His licence strictly speaks of two minims near a rest, so the critical note should say it is applied by extension.
- The present reading sings *noluit* again, in italics: text the print lacks (11.9). It also puts *-it* on the same b after the run, so it breaks the same tendency.
- **Allowed.** It is the weakest of the three, and the note should name 10.1(d) and 10.12.

**3. Tenor 40.1, *-la-* on e′.** *-So-* is on the minim e′ (39.4), *-la-* on the repeated semiminim e′ (40.1, the first of four), and *-ri* on a (40.3).
- ***-la-* is allowed on two grounds:**
  - (a): the first semiminim of the run, on the downbeat, carrying the stressed syllable;
  - (b): a repeated pitch after a minim. Stoquerus's exception to N4 does not apply, because the first e′ is a minim, so his necessary rule *requires* a new syllable here (pp. 179–181; our 10.9).
- The present reading holds *-la-* across e′–e′, which breaks 10.9. The one escape is if the repeat ornaments a cadential suspension; then the new syllable is optional (Towne 1991, 154). That is worth checking against the harmony.
- ***-ri* on a, straight after the run,** is the same in both readings and rests on (d): a phrase end, with *qui* following.
- **Allowed.**
